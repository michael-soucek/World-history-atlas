The Islamic Golden Age was a period of cultural, economic and scientific flourishing in the Islamic world, traditionally dated from the eighth to the thirteenth century, centred on Baghdad under the Abbasid caliphs but extending to Córdoba, Cairo, Bukhara and other cities. Scholars working in Arabic and Persian preserved, translated and built upon the knowledge of the Greeks, Persians and Indians, making major contributions to mathematics, astronomy, medicine, philosophy and other fields.

## Foundations

The Abbasid caliphs, who came to power in 750, founded Baghdad in 762 as their capital. The Islamic empire connected regions from Spain to Central Asia, allowing the exchange of goods and ideas. Paper making, learned from China after the Battle of Talas in 751, spread rapidly, making books cheaper. Arabic became a common language of learning. Patrons, from caliphs to wealthy families, supported scholars, who included Muslims, Christians, Jews and others.

## Translation

In the translation movement, many Greek works of philosophy, mathematics, astronomy and medicine, by Aristotle, Plato, Euclid, Ptolemy and Galen, were translated into Arabic, often through Syriac, by scholars such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq. The House of Wisdom in Baghdad, associated with the caliph al-Ma'mun in the ninth century, was a centre of this activity. Persian and Indian works were also translated.

## Mathematics

Al-Khwarizmi, in the ninth century, wrote a book on al-jabr, from which the word algebra comes, and his name gave the word algorithm. Muslim scholars adopted the Indian numeral system, including zero, which reached Europe as "Arabic numerals". Omar Khayyam, in the eleventh century, solved cubic equations geometrically, and also wrote poetry, the Rubaiyat.

## Astronomy

Astronomers built observatories, such as at Maragha, in Iran, in the thirteenth century, and later Samarkand, improved the astrolabe and compiled astronomical tables. Al-Battani refined measurements of the solar year. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi developed mathematical models that may have influenced Copernicus. Many star names, such as Aldebaran and Betelgeuse, come from Arabic.

## Medicine

Al-Razi, Rhazes, in the ninth and tenth centuries, distinguished smallpox from measles and ran a hospital in Baghdad. Ibn Sina, Avicenna, wrote the Canon of Medicine, which was used as a textbook in European universities until the seventeenth century. Al-Zahrawi, in Córdoba, wrote an illustrated treatise on surgery and surgical instruments. Ibn al-Nafis, in thirteenth-century Cairo, described the pulmonary circulation of the blood. Hospitals, bimaristans, treated patients and trained doctors.

## Science and philosophy

Ibn al-Haytham, Alhazen, in Cairo in the eleventh century, wrote the Book of Optics, explaining vision as light entering the eye and using experiments, an important step towards the scientific method. Al-Biruni studied astronomy, geography and the cultures of India. Philosophers such as al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd, Averroes, in Córdoba, discussed and commented on Aristotle, and debated the relationship between reason and faith with theologians such as al-Ghazali.

## Arts and society

Literature flourished, including the One Thousand and One Nights and Persian poetry, such as Ferdowsi's Shahnameh and the work of Rumi. Architecture produced great mosques and palaces. Agriculture improved with new crops and irrigation.

## Decline and legacy

The traditional end date, the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258, is debated, as scholarship continued in many places, such as Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal lands. Arabic works were translated into Latin in Spain and Sicily in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, contributing to the revival of learning in Europe.
