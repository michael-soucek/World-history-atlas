The Mississippian culture was a Native American civilisation that flourished in the Mississippi valley and the south-eastern United States from about 800 to 1600 CE. Its people built large earthen mounds, farmed maize and lived in towns ruled by chiefs. Its largest centre, Cahokia, near today's St Louis, was the largest city in North America north of Mexico before European colonisation.

## Origins

The Mississippian culture developed from earlier Woodland cultures, which also built mounds, such as the Hopewell. Around 800 CE, maize agriculture, which had spread from Mexico, became central, with beans and squash, allowing larger, more settled populations. Mississippian people also hunted, fished and gathered.

## Mounds

Mississippian towns were built around plazas surrounded by platform mounds, flat-topped earthen pyramids on which temples, chiefs' houses and other important buildings stood. Mounds were built by carrying baskets of earth. Some towns were protected by wooden palisades.

## Cahokia

Cahokia, in today's Illinois, was the largest Mississippian centre, flourishing between about 1050 and 1200, with perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people at its height. Its central mound, Monks Mound, is the largest earthen structure in the Americas, about 30 metres high, with a base covering about six hectares. Cahokia had about 120 mounds, a great plaza and circles of wooden posts, called "Woodhenge", which may have been used to mark solstices and equinoxes. In Mound 72, archaeologists found the burials of high-status individuals with tens of thousands of shell beads, and the remains of many sacrificed people, mostly young women. Cahokia declined after 1200 and was abandoned by about 1350, perhaps because of environmental problems, floods, drought, conflict or social change.

## Other centres

Other major Mississippian centres included Moundville, in Alabama, Etowah, in Georgia, and Spiro, in Oklahoma, which contained rich burials with ornaments. Chiefdoms of varying size and power covered much of the South-east.

## Society and religion

Mississippian society was organised in chiefdoms, with hereditary chiefs and elites, priests and commoners. Art, found across the region, features similar symbols, such as falcons, crosses, hands with eyes and figures of warriors, in copper plates, shell gorgets and pottery, called the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, or Mississippian Ideological Interaction Sphere. It reflects shared religious ideas, including a cosmos of upper, middle and lower worlds. The game of chunkey, played by rolling a stone disc and throwing spears at it, was popular.

## Contact

The Spanish expedition of Hernando de Soto, from 1539 to 1543, travelled through the South-east and encountered Mississippian chiefdoms, such as Coosa, describing large towns, temples and powerful chiefs, and fought battles, such as at Mabila. European diseases devastated the population in the following decades. When the French explored the region in the late seventeenth century, many chiefdoms had disappeared, though the Natchez still had a Mississippian-style society.

## Descendants

The Mississippians were ancestors of many South-eastern peoples, such as the Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw and Natchez. Cahokia Mounds is a World Heritage Site.
