Phoenicia was a civilisation of city-states on the coast of the eastern Mediterranean, in what is now Lebanon and parts of Syria and Israel, which flourished from about 1500 to 300 BCE. The Phoenicians were great seafarers and traders who founded colonies across the Mediterranean, including Carthage, and spread the alphabet that became the ancestor of the Greek, Latin and many other scripts.

## The cities

The Phoenicians called themselves Canaanites, or by the names of their cities. The name Phoenician comes from the Greek phoinix, possibly referring to the purple dye they produced. Their main cities, Tyre, Sidon, Byblos and Arwad, were independent, each with a king, though often under the control of larger empires, such as Egypt, Assyria, Babylon and Persia. The cities were built on the coast or on islands, with harbours, backed by the mountains of Lebanon, which provided cedar wood, prized for building ships and temples.

## Trade

After the Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE, the Phoenician cities, which survived, became the leading traders of the Mediterranean. They traded cedar wood, glass, ivory carvings, metalwork, wine, olive oil, textiles and, most famously, cloth dyed with Tyrian purple, a dye made from murex sea snails, so expensive that it became the colour of royalty. They carried goods from Egypt, Mesopotamia and elsewhere. According to the Bible, King Hiram of Tyre sent cedar wood and craftsmen to King Solomon to build the Temple in Jerusalem.

## Colonies

The Phoenicians founded trading posts and colonies across the Mediterranean, in Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, Malta, North Africa and Spain, where they founded Gadir, today's Cádiz, possibly around 1100 BCE, and beyond the Strait of Gibraltar. Carthage, in today's Tunisia, founded by settlers from Tyre, according to tradition in 814 BCE, became the most powerful Phoenician city, with its own empire, until its destruction by Rome in 146 BCE. According to Herodotus, Phoenician sailors, sent by the pharaoh Necho II, sailed around Africa around 600 BCE.

## The alphabet

The Phoenicians used an alphabet of 22 letters, all consonants, written from right to left, which developed from earlier Semitic scripts. It was much simpler than cuneiform or hieroglyphs. Around the eighth century BCE, the Greeks adopted it, adding vowels. From the Greek alphabet came the Latin and Cyrillic alphabets. The Aramaic alphabet, also derived from it, gave rise to the Hebrew, Arabic and many Asian scripts. The word "alphabet" comes from the first two Greek letters, alpha and beta, derived from the Phoenician aleph, "ox", and bet, "house".

## Religion

The Phoenicians worshipped gods such as Baal, Astarte, Melqart, the god of Tyre, and Eshmun. Greek and Roman writers accused the Carthaginians of child sacrifice to the god Baal Hammon; burial grounds called tophets, containing the remains of infants, have been found, and scholars debate their interpretation.

## Decline

The Assyrians and Babylonians dominated the Phoenician cities. In 332 BCE Alexander the Great besieged and took Tyre after seven months, building a causeway to the island city. The Phoenician cities became part of the Hellenistic and later Roman world, and their culture merged with Greek culture.
