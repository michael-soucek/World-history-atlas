The Scientific Revolution was the period, roughly from the mid-sixteenth to the late seventeenth century, during which developments in astronomy, physics, biology, chemistry and mathematics transformed views of nature in Europe. Thinkers replaced ancient and medieval ideas, based largely on Aristotle, with knowledge based on observation, experiment and mathematics, laying the foundations of modern science.

## The old view

Medieval European scholars largely followed Aristotle's physics and Ptolemy's astronomy, which placed the earth at the centre of the universe, surrounded by crystalline spheres carrying the moon, sun, planets and stars. Medicine followed Galen. These ideas had been preserved and developed by Islamic scholars and transmitted to Europe.

## Copernicus

In 1543 the Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus published On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, proposing that the sun, not the earth, is at the centre, and that the earth rotates daily and orbits the sun yearly. The heliocentric theory was slow to be accepted. The same year, Andreas Vesalius published On the Fabric of the Human Body, based on his dissections, correcting many of Galen's errors.

## Tycho and Kepler

The Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe made the most accurate observations of the planets before the telescope. His assistant, Johannes Kepler, used Tycho's data to discover, in 1609 and 1619, three laws of planetary motion, showing that planets move in ellipses, not circles, and faster when closer to the sun.

## Galileo

Galileo Galilei, in Italy, used a telescope from 1609 to discover the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, sunspots and mountains on the moon, which supported Copernicus. He studied motion, showing that falling objects accelerate at the same rate regardless of weight. His support for Copernicus led to his trial by the Inquisition in 1633 and house arrest.

## The scientific method

Francis Bacon, in England, promoted the inductive method: gathering observations and experiments to reach general conclusions. René Descartes, in France, emphasised reason and doubt and developed analytic geometry, and a mechanical view of nature. Scientists increasingly used experiments and measurements, and new instruments, such as the telescope, microscope, thermometer, barometer and air pump.

## Discoveries

William Harvey described the circulation of the blood in 1628. Robert Boyle studied gases and helped found modern chemistry, rejecting alchemical ideas; Boyle's law relates the pressure and volume of a gas. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using microscopes he made, observed bacteria and other microorganisms. Christiaan Huygens invented the pendulum clock and proposed a wave theory of light.

## Newton

Isaac Newton's Principia, published in 1687, set out the laws of motion and universal gravitation, uniting the physics of heaven and earth in a single mathematical system. Newton also studied optics and developed calculus, independently of Gottfried Leibniz.

## Institutions

Scientific societies were founded, such as the Royal Society in London in 1660 and the French Academy of Sciences in 1666, which published journals and promoted the sharing of results. The Scientific Revolution inspired the Enlightenment. Some historians question whether it was a single "revolution", noting continuity with medieval and Islamic science and the role of alchemy and religion in the thinking of figures like Newton.
