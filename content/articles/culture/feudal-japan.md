Feudal Japan is the period of Japanese history from the late twelfth century to the Meiji Restoration of 1868, when the country was dominated by a warrior class, the samurai, and ruled in practice by military governments, the shogunates, while the emperor reigned in Kyoto as a ceremonial figure.

## The rise of the samurai

In the Heian period, from 794 to 1185, the imperial court in Kyoto, dominated by the Fujiwara family, produced a refined culture, including Murasaki Shikibu's novel The Tale of Genji. In the provinces, warrior families, such as the Taira and the Minamoto, grew powerful. In the Genpei War of 1180–1185, the Minamoto defeated the Taira at the sea battle of Dan-no-ura.

## The Kamakura shogunate

In 1192 Minamoto no Yoritomo was appointed shogun, "barbarian-subduing generalissimo", by the emperor, and established a military government at Kamakura, in eastern Japan. He appointed military governors and stewards in the provinces. After his death, the Hōjō family ruled as regents. In 1274 and 1281, the Mongols under Kublai Khan invaded Japan, but were repelled with the help of typhoons, called kamikaze, "divine wind". New schools of Buddhism, such as Pure Land, Nichiren and Zen, spread.

## The Ashikaga shogunate

In 1333 the emperor Go-Daigo overthrew the Kamakura shogunate, but in 1336 Ashikaga Takauji drove him out and became shogun, ruling from the Muromachi district of Kyoto. For decades, two rival imperial courts existed. The Ashikaga period saw the development of Noh theatre, the tea ceremony, ink painting, Zen gardens and buildings such as the Golden Pavilion, Kinkaku-ji.

## The Sengoku period

After the Ōnin War of 1467–1477, central authority collapsed, and Japan entered the Sengoku, "Warring States", period, in which regional lords, daimyo, fought each other. Portuguese traders introduced firearms in 1543, and Jesuit missionaries, such as Francis Xavier, Christianity. Three leaders unified Japan: Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu, who won the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600.

## The Tokugawa shogunate

Ieyasu became shogun in 1603, ruling from Edo, today's Tokyo. The Tokugawa period brought over 250 years of peace. The shogunate controlled the daimyo through the system of alternate attendance, requiring them to spend every other year in Edo. Society was divided into classes: samurai, farmers, artisans and merchants. Christianity was banned, and contact with the outside world was restricted to the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki, Korea and the Ryukyu Islands.

## The samurai

The samurai were warriors who served their lords in exchange for land or stipends. They followed a code of honour, later called bushidō, "the way of the warrior", emphasising loyalty, courage and self-discipline, though the code was idealised in later centuries. Samurai trained in archery, horsemanship and swordsmanship, and many were educated in Confucianism and Zen Buddhism. In the peaceful Tokugawa period, many became bureaucrats. The story of the forty-seven rōnin, masterless samurai who avenged their lord in 1703 and were ordered to commit seppuku, ritual suicide, became famous.

## The end

In 1853 American warships under Commodore Perry forced Japan to open its ports. Opposition to the shogunate grew, and in 1868 the Meiji Restoration restored imperial rule. The samurai class was abolished in the 1870s. Whether "feudalism", a term from European history, fits Japan is debated by historians.
