Sumer was the earliest known civilisation in southern Mesopotamia, in what is now southern Iraq, between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. From around 4500 to 1900 BCE, the Sumerians built the first cities, developed writing, the wheel, irrigation, mathematics and law codes, and created the world's earliest known literature.

## The land

Southern Mesopotamia was a flat, dry, hot land, but the rivers provided water for irrigation, and the soil was fertile. Farmers built canals to irrigate fields of barley and wheat, grew dates, raised sheep and goats and fished. Lacking stone, timber and metals, the Sumerians built with mud brick and reeds, and traded for other materials.

## The first cities

During the Uruk period, from about 4000 to 3100 BCE, the city of Uruk grew into perhaps the world's first true city, with tens of thousands of people, and monumental temples. By the third millennium BCE, Sumer was divided into independent city-states, such as Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Umma, Kish, Eridu and Nippur, each with its patron god and temple, often on a stepped platform, the ziggurat, ruled by a king, lugal, or governor, ensi. City-states often fought each other over land and water; the Stele of the Vultures records a victory of Lagash over Umma.

## Writing

Around 3300 to 3200 BCE, the Sumerians developed one of the earliest writing systems, cuneiform, "wedge-shaped", made by pressing a reed stylus into clay tablets. It began as pictographs for keeping records of goods, such as grain and livestock, and developed into a script with hundreds of signs representing words and syllables. Cuneiform was later used for many languages, including Akkadian, Babylonian, Hittite and Persian, for about three thousand years. Scribes trained in schools, called "tablet houses".

## Inventions

The Sumerians are credited with early wheels, used for pottery and carts, the plough, sailboats, bronze metallurgy and a number system based on sixty, from which we get sixty seconds in a minute, sixty minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle.

## Literature and law

Sumerian literature includes hymns, proverbs, laments, such as the Lament for Ur, and myths. The Epic of Gilgamesh, about a legendary king of Uruk who seeks immortality, began as Sumerian poems, later combined into an Akkadian epic, which includes a flood story. The priestess Enheduanna, daughter of Sargon of Akkad, wrote hymns and is the earliest known author by name. The Code of Ur-Nammu, from around 2100 BCE, is the oldest known law code.

## Religion

The Sumerians believed that gods, such as An, the sky god, Enlil, god of air and the chief god, Enki, god of water and wisdom, and Inanna, goddess of love and war, controlled nature and lived in their temples, and that humans were created to serve them. The Royal Cemetery at Ur, excavated by Leonard Woolley in the 1920s, contained rich burials, with gold, lapis lazuli, the Standard of Ur and many sacrificed attendants.

## The end

Around 2334 BCE, Sargon of Akkad conquered Sumer, creating the Akkadian Empire. After its fall, the Third Dynasty of Ur, from about 2112 to 2004 BCE, revived Sumerian culture and built the great ziggurat of Ur, until the Elamites sacked Ur. Sumerian ceased to be spoken, replaced by Akkadian, but remained a language of religion and learning for centuries.

## On our maps

Our 4000, 3000 and 2000 BCE maps show southern Mesopotamia in the age of Sumer, beside [Elam](/place/elam) to the east.
