The Age of Enlightenment was an intellectual and philosophical movement in Europe in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, which emphasised reason, science, individual liberty and scepticism of traditional authority, including the church and absolute monarchy. Its ideas inspired the American and French revolutions and shaped modern ideas of human rights, democracy and secularism.

## Roots

The Enlightenment grew from the Scientific Revolution, especially Isaac Newton's demonstration that the universe follows natural laws discoverable by reason and observation. Philosophers such as René Descartes, who emphasised reason, and John Locke, who argued that knowledge comes from experience and that government depends on the consent of the governed, laid its foundations. The wars of religion led many to value tolerance.

## The philosophes

In France, writers known as philosophes led the movement. Voltaire attacked religious intolerance, fanaticism and censorship, campaigning to clear the name of Jean Calas, a Protestant executed in 1762. Montesquieu, in The Spirit of the Laws of 1748, advocated the separation of powers into legislative, executive and judicial branches. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in The Social Contract of 1762, argued that legitimate government is based on the general will of the people. Denis Diderot and Jean d'Alembert edited the Encyclopédie, published from 1751 to 1772, which aimed to collect all human knowledge and promote critical thinking; it was banned by authorities several times.

## Across Europe

The Enlightenment was international. In Scotland, David Hume questioned religious belief and causation, and Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations of 1776, argued for free markets. In Germany, Immanuel Kant defined Enlightenment as "man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity", urging people to "dare to know", sapere aude. In Italy, Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments, in 1764, argued against torture and the death penalty. Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, in 1792, argued for women's education and equality. The Jewish philosopher Moses Mendelssohn promoted tolerance.

## Salons and coffeehouses

Enlightenment ideas spread through books, journals, academies, Masonic lodges, coffeehouses and salons, gatherings hosted by educated women, such as Madame Geoffrin in Paris. Literacy and the printing of books increased.

## Enlightened despots

Some monarchs, called enlightened despots, adopted Enlightenment ideas while keeping absolute power. Frederick the Great of Prussia corresponded with Voltaire, promoted religious tolerance and abolished torture. Catherine the Great of Russia corresponded with Diderot and Voltaire. Joseph II of Austria abolished serfdom and granted religious toleration.

## Revolutions

Enlightenment ideas inspired the American Declaration of Independence of 1776, with its statement that "all men are created equal" with "unalienable rights", and the United States Constitution, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789.

## Limits and critiques

Many Enlightenment thinkers held views that excluded women, non-Europeans and enslaved people from the rights they proclaimed, and some developed racial theories. Critics, such as the Romantics, argued that the Enlightenment overvalued reason. Debates about the Enlightenment's legacy continue.
