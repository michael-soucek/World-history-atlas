The Nok culture was an early Iron Age culture in what is now central Nigeria, which flourished from about 1500 BCE to the first centuries CE. It is famous for its terracotta sculptures, the earliest known figurative sculptures in sub-Saharan Africa, and for some of the earliest evidence of iron smelting in West Africa.

## Discovery

The Nok culture is named after the village of Nok, in Kaduna State, where in 1943 tin miners found a terracotta head in alluvial deposits. The British archaeologist Bernard Fagg, who was working in Nigeria, recognised its importance and collected more pieces, many found by miners. Since 2005, a German research project at Goethe University Frankfurt, led by Peter Breunig and Nicole Rupp, has excavated many Nok sites, greatly improving knowledge of the culture.

## Dates

Recent research divides the Nok culture into phases. Early Nok, from about 1500 to 900 BCE, was a culture of farming settlements. The Middle Nok period, from about 900 BCE to the first centuries BCE, was the height of the culture, when the terracotta sculptures were made and iron working began, around the mid-first millennium BCE. Late Nok lasted until around the turn of the era. Why the culture ended is unknown.

## Terracottas

Nok terracottas are made of clay, fired at low temperatures, and are mostly human figures, some nearly life-size, and heads, often broken off from larger figures. They are highly stylised: heads are large compared with bodies, eyes are triangular or semicircular, with pierced pupils, and hairstyles are elaborate, with buns, braids and crests. Figures wear jewellery, beads and other ornaments, and some show people sitting, kneeling or holding objects. Some depict animals, such as snakes, monkeys and elephants, and some show people with diseases or physical features. The sculptures were probably made by skilled specialists. Their purpose is unknown; they may have represented ancestors, been used in rituals or marked graves. Excavations have found them often deliberately broken and placed in pits.

## Iron

The Nok people smelted iron in furnaces, producing tools and weapons, from around the mid-first millennium BCE, among the earliest evidence of iron working in West Africa. Iron working may have developed independently in Africa, though this is debated. They also used stone tools.

## Farming

The Nok people were farmers. Plant remains show that they grew pearl millet and cowpeas and gathered wild plants. Chemical analysis of pottery residues suggests they used honey. They lived in villages on hilltops and plains, though no large towns have been found.

## Looting

Since the 1990s, Nok terracottas have become highly valued on the international art market, and widespread illegal digging has destroyed many sites, removing objects from their archaeological context. Many sculptures sold abroad are looted, and some are fakes. Nok terracottas are on UNESCO's list of objects at risk of illicit trafficking, and Nigeria seeks to prevent their export and recover looted pieces.

## Legacy

Some scholars see similarities between Nok art and later Nigerian art, such as the terracottas and bronzes of Ife, from the twelfth to the fifteenth centuries, suggesting a long artistic tradition, though a direct connection is uncertain.

## On our maps

The Nok culture's region in central Nigeria appears on our maps from 1000 BCE onward, before the rise of later states such as the [Oyo Empire](/place/oyo-empire) and the [Kingdom of Benin](/place/kingdom-of-benin).
