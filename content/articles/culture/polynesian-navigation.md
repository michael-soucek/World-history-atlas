Polynesian navigation was the system of techniques used by the peoples of Polynesia to make long voyages across the Pacific Ocean in double-hulled canoes, without instruments or charts. Using the stars, the sun, ocean swells, winds, birds and clouds, they discovered and settled the islands of the vast Polynesian Triangle, from Hawaii to New Zealand to Easter Island, one of the great achievements of human exploration.

## Austronesian origins

The ancestors of the Polynesians were Austronesian-speaking peoples who originated in Taiwan and spread through the Philippines and Indonesia from around 3000 BCE. Around 1500 to 1000 BCE, people of the Lapita culture, known for their distinctive decorated pottery, voyaged from the Bismarck Archipelago, near New Guinea, into the remote Pacific, reaching Fiji, Tonga and Samoa by around 900 to 800 BCE. There, Polynesian culture developed.

## Settling the Pacific

After a long pause, Polynesians voyaged east, reaching the Society Islands, such as Tahiti, and the Cook Islands, perhaps around 1000 CE, then spread rapidly in all directions: to Hawaii, Rapa Nui, Easter Island, and Aotearoa, New Zealand, the last major landmass settled by humans, around 1250 to 1300 CE. Some Polynesians probably reached South America; the sweet potato, a South American plant, was grown across Polynesia before European contact, and genetic studies suggest contact around 1200 CE.

## Canoes

Polynesians built large voyaging canoes, such as the double-hulled wa'a kaulua of Hawaii, with two hulls joined by a platform, and sails made of woven pandanus leaves. They could carry dozens of people, plants, such as taro, breadfruit, bananas and coconuts, and animals, pigs, dogs and chickens, to settle new islands. Rats also travelled with them.

## Wayfinding

Navigators, highly respected experts who trained for years, used a variety of techniques. They memorised the rising and setting points of stars on the horizon, a "star compass", and steered towards stars that rose or set in the direction of their destination. They read the direction and pattern of ocean swells, which could be felt through the hull. They observed the flight of birds, such as terns and noddies, which return to land at night, cloud formations that form over islands, the colour of the sea and floating debris. In the Marshall Islands, navigators used stick charts, made of sticks and shells, to represent swell patterns.

## Debate

In the twentieth century, some scholars, such as Andrew Sharp in 1956, argued that the islands were settled by accidental drift voyages. Thor Heyerdahl argued that Polynesians came from South America and sailed the raft Kon-Tiki to the Tuamotus in 1947 to show it was possible. Most scholars now reject this, as linguistic, archaeological and genetic evidence shows that Polynesians came from the west.

## Revival

In 1976 the Polynesian Voyaging Society's canoe Hōkūleʻa, a replica of a traditional voyaging canoe, sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti, about 4,000 kilometres, navigated without instruments by the Micronesian navigator Mau Piailug, from the island of Satawal, one of the few people still practising traditional navigation. The voyage proved that intentional long-distance voyaging was possible, and inspired a revival of traditional navigation and cultural pride across the Pacific. From 2013 to 2017, Hōkūleʻa sailed around the world.
