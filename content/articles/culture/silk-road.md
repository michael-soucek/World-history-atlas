The Silk Road was a network of trade routes that connected China with Central Asia, India, the Middle East, Africa and Europe from about the second century BCE to the fifteenth century CE. Along it travelled not only silk and other goods, but also people, religions, technologies, ideas, art and diseases, making it one of the most important channels of exchange in world history.

## The name

The term "Silk Road", Seidenstraße, was coined in 1877 by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen. It was never a single road, but a web of overland routes, and maritime routes across the Indian Ocean, sometimes called the Maritime Silk Road. Few traders travelled the whole distance; goods passed through many hands.

## Origins

In the second century BCE, the Han dynasty of China sent the envoy Zhang Qian to Central Asia, around 138 BCE, to seek allies against the Xiongnu. His reports of the lands to the west, and the Han's desire for the "heavenly horses" of Ferghana, led to Chinese expansion into the Tarim basin. The Han built garrisons and extended the Great Wall west to Dunhuang. At the same time, the Parthian Empire in Iran and later the Roman Empire created demand for Chinese silk, which Romans considered a luxury. Roman writers complained about the gold spent on it.

## Routes

From Chang'an, today's Xi'an, the routes went west through the Hexi corridor to Dunhuang, then divided around the Taklamakan desert, through oasis cities such as Turpan, Kucha, Khotan and Kashgar, and crossed the Pamir mountains to Central Asian cities, such as Samarkand and Bukhara, then to Iran, Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean, or south to India. Caravans of Bactrian camels crossed deserts and mountains, stopping at caravanserais.

## Goods

China exported silk, porcelain, tea, lacquer and paper. From the west came horses, glassware, gold, silver, wool, linen, wine, spices, gems, ivory and new crops, such as grapes and alfalfa. Sogdian merchants, from the region of Samarkand, were the main traders across Central Asia for centuries, with communities in Chinese cities.

## Religions and ideas

Buddhism spread from India along the Silk Road to Central Asia and China from the first century CE, and monks, such as Xuanzang, in the seventh century, travelled to India to collect scriptures. Buddhist cave temples, such as the Mogao Caves at Dunhuang, contain thousands of paintings and sculptures; a sealed library found there in 1900 contained tens of thousands of manuscripts, including the Diamond Sutra of 868, the oldest dated printed book. Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity, Zoroastrianism and later Islam also spread along the routes. Technologies such as paper making, printing and gunpowder travelled west.

## The Mongol peace

After periods of disruption, the Mongol Empire, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, unified most of the routes, making travel safer, the Pax Mongolica. European travellers, such as Marco Polo, reached China. The Black Death may have spread along trade routes from Central Asia in the 1340s.

## Decline

After the fragmentation of the Mongol Empire, overland trade declined, while sea routes became more important, especially after Europeans reached Asia by sea around Africa in 1498. In 2014 a section of the Silk Road routes in China, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan was named a World Heritage Site. China's Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, invokes the Silk Road.
