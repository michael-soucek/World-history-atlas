The Swahili are a people and culture of the East African coast, from southern Somalia to northern Mozambique, including the islands of Zanzibar, Pemba, Lamu and the Comoros. Between about the eighth and sixteenth centuries, Swahili city-states became wealthy through Indian Ocean trade, developing a distinctive African and Islamic urban culture. Their language, Kiswahili, is today one of the most widely spoken languages in Africa.

## Origins

The Swahili culture developed from Bantu-speaking communities on the coast, who farmed, fished and traded. From the first millennium CE, they engaged in trade across the Indian Ocean, using the monsoon winds, which blow from the north-east in winter and the south-west in summer, allowing ships to sail between Africa, Arabia, Persia and India. The name Swahili comes from the Arabic sawahil, "coasts". Archaeology has shown that Swahili towns were African in origin, though older theories, now rejected, credited them to Arab or Persian settlers.

## Islam

Islam spread along the coast from the eighth century, through trade and intermarriage, and by the twelfth century many Swahili were Muslims. Mosques, built of coral stone, were the centres of towns. Swahili culture blended African traditions with influences from Arabia, Persia and India.

## City-states

Swahili towns, such as Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Mogadishu, Lamu, Pate and Zanzibar, were independent city-states, ruled by sultans and merchant elites. They built houses, palaces and mosques of coral stone and lime. Kilwa Kisiwani, on an island in today's Tanzania, became the richest, controlling the gold trade from Great Zimbabwe through the port of Sofala; its Great Mosque and the palace of Husuni Kubwa, from the fourteenth century, are impressive ruins. Kilwa minted its own copper coins. The traveller Ibn Battuta, who visited in 1331, called Kilwa one of the most beautiful and well-built towns in the world.

## Trade

Swahili merchants exported gold, ivory, rhinoceros horn, tortoiseshell, mangrove poles, iron and enslaved people, and imported cloth, glass beads, Chinese porcelain, Persian and Islamic pottery and other goods. In 1418, ships of the Chinese admiral Zheng He visited the coast, and Malindi sent a giraffe to the Ming emperor.

## The Portuguese

In 1498 Vasco da Gama reached the coast, and Malindi provided him with a pilot to guide him to India. The Portuguese attacked and sacked Kilwa and Mombasa in 1505, seeking to control the Indian Ocean trade, and built Fort Jesus in Mombasa in 1593. The Swahili towns declined. In the late seventeenth century, the Omani Arabs expelled the Portuguese from much of the coast.

## Zanzibar

In the nineteenth century, the Omani sultan Said bin Sultan moved his capital to Zanzibar in 1840, which became a centre of the clove trade and of the East African slave trade, with caravans from the interior. Britain forced the end of the slave trade in 1873.

## Language and literature

Kiswahili is a Bantu language with many words borrowed from Arabic, Persian, Portuguese, Hindi and English. It has a written literary tradition, originally in Arabic script, including epic poems, utendi, from the eighteenth century. Spread inland by traders, it became a lingua franca and is today an official language of Tanzania, Kenya and the African Union, spoken by perhaps 100 to 200 million people. Lamu Old Town and Kilwa are World Heritage Sites.
