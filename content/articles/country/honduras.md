Honduras lies where the Mesoamerican world of the Maya and the Lenca met the peoples of the Caribbean lowlands, whose cultures looked south towards Central and South America. Its west was home to Copán, one of the most brilliant cities of [Maya civilisation](/culture/maya-civilization), and its Lenca chiefdoms fought the Spanish conquest under the leader Lempira. For three centuries the colony was a poor mining province of Spanish Central America, while Britain and the Miskito people contested its Caribbean coast. After independence in 1821 Honduras became a byword for instability and, from the 1900s, for the power of American banana companies, giving rise to the term "banana republic". Long military rule gave way to elected governments in 1982, but coups, poverty, hurricanes, violent crime and mass emigration have shaped the country into the present.

## The first peoples

Hunter-gatherers were living in the caves and valleys of Honduras thousands of years before farming began. Rock shelters such as the Cueva del Gigante in La Paz preserve traces of early foragers, and in the Talgua caves of Olancho people placed the painted bones of their dead around 1000 BCE. As maize farming spread, villages grew into ceremonial towns. Los Naranjos, beside Lake Yojoa, and Yarumela, in the Comayagua valley, built large earthen platforms and earthworks in the first millennium BCE, centuries before the Maya arrived, and show influence from the [Olmecs](/place/olmecs) of the Gulf of Mexico. These towns are generally associated with the ancestors of the Lenca, whose language has no close relatives and who came to dominate the central and western highlands. Their position between the Pacific and the Caribbean made the region a corridor for trade in jade, obsidian, cacao and marine shell.

## Copán and the Maya

The Maya city of Copán, in the far west near the border with [Guatemala](/country/guatemala), became the southeastern capital of the Classic Maya world. In 426 or 427 CE a lord named K'inich Yax K'uk' Mo', who seems to have come from the central Petén and claimed links to the great Mexican city of Teotihuacan, founded a dynasty that ruled for some four centuries through sixteen kings. Under them Copán grew into a city of temples, plazas, ball courts and finely carved stelae and altars, famous for its sculpture in the round and its astronomical and calendrical learning. In 738 its thirteenth king, Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil, was captured and beheaded by his former vassal, the ruler of Quiriguá. His successors recovered, and in 755 they dedicated the Hieroglyphic Stairway, whose more than 2,000 glyphs make up the longest known Maya inscription, a history of the dynasty.

From the late eighth century the valley's population outgrew its land, erosion and deforestation spread, and royal authority weakened. The last king, Yax Pasaj, died early in the ninth century, and the last known monument was left unfinished. People continued to farm the valley for centuries, and the Ch'orti' Maya of the Copán and Motagua region are descendants of this world, but by the time the Spanish arrived the city lay under forest. Copán was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980.

## Peoples of the land before the conquest

After the Classic period, the Lenca built fortified towns such as Tenampúa and remained the largest and most organised society in the west and centre. Nahua-speaking migrants, connected with the [Toltec](/place/toltec) world of central Mexico, settled in pockets from the Caribbean to the Gulf of Fonseca, and Chorotega peoples lived around today's Choluteca in the south. In the east and northeast lived peoples whose languages are related to those of Colombia and Lower Central America: the Pech, the Tawahka and the Miskito of the Mosquitia, as well as the Tolupan, or Jicaque, of the north. Between about 500 and 1000 CE the Mosquitia rainforest held towns with plazas and earthen pyramids, among them sites popularly linked to the legend of a lost "White City", whose remains archaeologists continue to survey. The Bay Islands were a stopping point for seaborne trade; it was there, in 1502, that [Christopher Columbus](/explorer/christopher-columbus) met a large Maya trading canoe. Estimates of the total population before the conquest range up to two million, though the Library of Congress country study suggests about 500,000 was more likely.

## The Spanish conquest and Lempira

Columbus sailed along the north coast in 1502 and is said to have named the region after the deep waters, hondura, he found off it. The conquest began in 1524, when expeditions sent by rival Spanish commanders entered the country at once. Gil González Dávila came from the Caribbean, and Cristóbal de Olid, sent by [Hernán Cortés](/person/hernan-cortes), tried to set up his own rule; his rivals took him prisoner and beheaded him. Cortés then marched overland from Mexico himself and stayed at Trujillo on the north coast until 1526, while forces from Guatemala under Pedro de Alvarado and from [Nicaragua](/country/nicaragua) also pushed into the territory. The fighting among Spaniards, revolts, disease and the capture of large numbers of Indigenous people for sale as slaves in the Caribbean islands almost destroyed the young colony by 1534.

The discovery of gold around Gracias, founded by Alvarado's men in 1536, brought new demands for forced labour. In 1537 a Lenca leader known as Lempira gathered thousands of warriors in one of the largest Indigenous uprisings in Central America, holding the fortified rock of Cerquín against the Spanish. He was killed in 1538, according to Spanish accounts during a parley, and the revolt collapsed by 1539. The number of Indigenous people under Spanish control fell from an estimated 15,000 in 1539 to 8,000 two years later. Lempira is now a national hero, and the Honduran currency bears his name.

## Colonial Honduras

From 1544 Gracias briefly housed the Audiencia de los Confines, the court that governed Central America, before it moved to Guatemala in 1549, leaving Honduras a province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala within the [Spanish Empire](/place/spanish-empire). Its economy rested on gold and then silver mining around Comayagua, the colonial capital, and Tegucigalpa, a silver town that grew in the late sixteenth century. As the Indigenous population fell, enslaved Africans were brought to work the mines, and by 1545 there may already have been 2,000 in the province. Cattle ranching and the trade in hides, indigo and tobacco sustained the colony when the mines declined.

Spain never controlled the Caribbean coast east of Trujillo. English, French and Dutch pirates raided its towns, and the Miskito, who had absorbed African survivors of a shipwrecked slave ship in the seventeenth century, formed a kingdom allied to the English in [Jamaica](/country/jamaica). Britain declared a protectorate over the Mosquito Coast in 1740 and settled the Bay Islands, and Spain built the fortress of San Fernando de Omoa from 1759 to guard the coast. As the Bourbon reforms revived mining, Spain regained the Bay Islands in the 1780s, and an Anglo-Spanish convention of 1786 recognised Spanish sovereignty. In 1797 the British deported the Garifuna, a people of mixed African and Indigenous Caribbean descent, from [Saint Vincent](/country/saint-vincent-and-the-grenadines); about 2,000 survived to reach Roatán, and they soon settled along the mainland coast from [Belize](/country/belize) to Nicaragua, where their communities remain.

## Independence and the Central American federation

When news of [Mexico](/country/mexico)'s independence reached the Captaincy General, its leaders declared independence on 15 September 1821, in the wider wave of the [Spanish American wars of independence](/event/spanish-american-wars-of-independence). Honduras was briefly part of the Mexican Empire before joining the Federal Republic of Central America in 1823. Its most famous son, Francisco Morazán, became the federation's Liberal president in 1830 and fought to preserve it until his defeat by Guatemalan Conservatives in 1840. Honduras declared itself a separate state in 1838.

The decades that followed were among the most unstable in the Americas, with frequent revolts and interventions by neighbouring governments in Honduran politics. In 1860 the American filibuster William Walker, who had briefly made himself president of Nicaragua, landed at Trujillo and was executed by a Honduran firing squad. Britain handed the Bay Islands and the Honduran part of the Mosquito Coast to Honduras by a treaty of 1859. The Liberal reforms of Marco Aurelio Soto from 1876 encouraged mining and foreign investment, and in 1880 Soto moved the capital to Tegucigalpa.

## Bananas and the companies

Banana exports began from the Bay Islands in the 1870s, at first grown by small farmers. From 1899 the New Orleans-based Vaccaro brothers, later Standard Fruit, Samuel Zemurray's Cuyamel Fruit Company and, from 1912, United Fruit obtained vast land concessions on the north coast, usually in exchange for promises to build railways. The companies' plantations, railways and ports turned La Ceiba, Tela and San Pedro Sula into boom towns and by 1930 made Honduras the world's largest exporter of bananas. They also took sides in Honduran politics: in 1911 Zemurray financed a revolution that returned Manuel Bonilla to power. The American writer O. Henry, who had stayed in Honduras in the 1890s, coined the phrase "banana republic" in a book of stories published in 1904. United States forces landed repeatedly between 1903 and 1925 to protect American interests during civil wars, and the [United States](/country/united-states) brokered settlements on board its warships.

The [Great Depression](/event/great-depression) and plant diseases devastated the banana economy. General Tiburcio Carías Andino, elected in 1932, ruled as a dictator until 1949, extending his term through new constitutions, outlawing the communist party and silencing opponents while keeping good relations with the fruit companies and with Washington. Honduras joined the Allies in the [Second World War](/event/world-war-ii) in December 1941.

## Military rule and the Cold War

In 1954 a general strike by banana workers, involving tens of thousands of people, won the right to form unions and a labour code. The reformist Liberal Ramón Villeda Morales, president from 1957, introduced social security and a modest land reform, but in October 1963 the army under Colonel Oswaldo López Arellano overthrew him in a bloody coup, beginning almost two decades of military rule. Tension with [El Salvador](/country/el-salvador), aggravated by the expulsion of Salvadoran migrant settlers under a new land law, exploded in July 1969 into the Football War, named after qualifying matches for the World Cup played at the time, which killed thousands of people in about a hundred hours of fighting. After Hurricane Fifi killed thousands of Hondurans in 1974, López Arellano was forced out in 1975 when it emerged that United Brands, United Fruit's successor, had bribed officials to cut a banana export tax.

A constituent assembly elected in 1980 drew up the constitution of 1982, and civilian presidents took office, but the armed forces remained powerful. During the [Cold War](/event/cold-war) conflicts of the 1980s Honduras became the main base for American operations in Central America: the United States built airfields and bases, and the Contra rebels fighting Nicaragua's Sandinista government operated from Honduran territory. At home, a military intelligence unit trained by the United States, Battalion 3-16, carried out the disappearance and killing of students, trade unionists and suspected leftists. In 1992 the International Court of Justice settled most of the land border with El Salvador.

## Democracy and its crises

In October 1998 Hurricane Mitch stalled over Honduras, killing more than 5,000 people, destroying about 70 percent of crops and most bridges and roads, and setting the economy back by years. Street gangs such as MS-13 and Barrio 18, formed in part by young men deported from the United States, spread through the cities, and by the early 2010s Honduras had one of the highest murder rates in the world. Drug cartels moving cocaine north used its coasts and airstrips.

In 2009 President Manuel Zelaya, who had moved to the left and allied with Venezuela, pressed ahead with a non-binding poll on convening an assembly to rewrite the constitution, which the Supreme Court had ruled illegal. On 28 June 2009 soldiers seized him and flew him to Costa Rica, and Congress named its speaker, Roberto Micheletti, as president. Most foreign governments condemned the removal as a coup, and Honduras was suspended from the Organization of American States until 2011. Porfirio Lobo of the National Party won elections held later that year. His successor, Juan Orlando Hernández, benefited from a 2015 court ruling that set aside the constitutional ban on re-election and won a second term in 2017 in a vote marred by allegations of fraud and deadly protests. In 2016 the Lenca environmental leader Berta Cáceres, who had opposed a hydroelectric dam on the Gualcarque river, was murdered; an executive of the dam company and others were later convicted.

In 2021 Xiomara Castro, Zelaya's wife and the candidate of the left-wing Libre party, won the presidency, becoming the country's first woman president. Hernández was extradited to the United States in 2022, convicted of conspiring to traffic cocaine and sentenced to 45 years in prison, but President Donald Trump pardoned him in late 2025. The general election of 30 November 2025 produced a narrow and bitterly disputed count between Nasry Asfura of the National Party and Salvador Nasralla of the Liberals, during which Trump endorsed Asfura. The electoral council declared Asfura the winner on 24 December, and he was sworn in on 27 January 2026. Remittances from the large Honduran diaspora in the United States remain one of the mainstays of the economy.
