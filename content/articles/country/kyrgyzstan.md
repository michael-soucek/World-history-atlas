Kyrgyzstan is a land of high mountains in Central Asia, dominated by the Tian Shan and the Pamir-Alay ranges, with the great lake of Issyk-Kul and the fertile Chuy and Ferghana valleys at its edges. For thousands of years it was grazed by nomads, from the Saka and Wusun of antiquity to the Turkic peoples of the Middle Ages, while Silk Road towns grew up in its valleys under Sogdian, Chinese, Turkic and Islamic rulers. The Kyrgyz, who trace their origins to the upper Yenisei in Siberia, became the main people of the mountains by the 16th century, and after rule by the Dzungars, the Qing and the Khanate of Kokand they were absorbed into the Russian Empire in the 19th century. Soviet rule created the Kyrgyz republic and its borders, and since independence in 1991 the country has seen three revolutions, ethnic violence and a return to strong presidential government.

## Saka, Wusun and the first states

Stone tools found in the Tian Shan show that early humans lived in the region tens of thousands of years ago, and later rock carvings, such as those at Cholpon-Ata by Lake Issyk-Kul, show hunters, ibex and horsemen. In the first millennium BCE the mountains and steppes were held by the Saka, Iranian-speaking nomads related to the [Scythians](/place/scythians), who buried their chiefs under large mounds with gold ornaments. In the second century BCE the Wusun, a nomadic people pushed west by the [Xiongnu](/place/xiongnu), took over the Issyk-Kul basin and the Chuy valley, where Chinese envoys of the [Han dynasty](/place/han-dynasty) sought them as allies.

The Ferghana Valley in the south, today shared with [Uzbekistan](/country/uzbekistan) and [Tajikistan](/country/tajikistan), was a land of farmers and walled towns known to the Chinese as Dayuan. Its "heavenly horses" were so prized that the Han emperor Wu sent armies to conquer it in 104–101 BCE, an episode that helped open the overland trade routes later called the [Silk Road](/culture/silk-road). The rock of Sulaiman-Too above Osh, a sacred mountain for millennia, overlooked one of the main caravan routes between Ferghana and Kashgar.

## Turks, Tang China and the Silk Road towns

From the sixth century the region fell under the [Göktürks](/place/gokturks), whose western khagans had their headquarters at Suyab in the Chuy valley. Sogdian merchants from the region of [Samarkand](/history-of/samarkand) founded trading towns there and along the shores of Issyk-Kul, and Buddhist, Christian, Manichaean and Zoroastrian communities lived side by side; Chinese and Sogdian traces have been found at Ak-Beshim, the site of Suyab. The Chinese monk Xuanzang passed through in 629 on his way to India. The [Tang dynasty](/place/tang-dynasty) extended its protectorate this far west, making Suyab one of its Four Garrisons in 679. The Tang were checked by Arab armies of the [Abbasid Caliphate](/place/abbasid-caliphate) and their Karluk allies at the Battle of Talas in 751, fought on the Talas River near the present Kazakh–Kyrgyz border, and soon withdrew from Central Asia after the An Lushan rebellion.

The Karluk Turks then dominated the region. Their successors, the [Kara-Khanid Khanate](/place/kara-khanid-khanate), converted to [Islam](/culture/islam) in the tenth century, in part through contact with the Samanid Empire, and ruled from Balasagun in the Chuy valley and Uzgen in the south. The Burana tower near Tokmok and the mausoleums of Uzgen survive from this period, and Yusuf Balasaguni, a native of Balasagun, wrote the Kutadgu Bilig ("Wisdom that brings good fortune"), one of the earliest great works of Turkic literature. In the 1130s the Khitans, a people from northern China who had lost their Liao dynasty to the Jurchens, took Balasagun and founded the Kara-Khitai empire, which held the region as overlord of the Karakhanids.

## The Kyrgyz of the Yenisei

The people who gave the country its name originated far to the north-east. Chinese records mention a people called the Gekun or Jiankun on the upper Yenisei River in southern Siberia from about the second century BCE, and in the early Middle Ages the Yenisei Kyrgyz were subjects of the Göktürks and then of the [Uyghur Khaganate](/place/uyghur-khaganate). They left inscriptions in the Old Turkic runic script. In 840 they sacked the Uyghur capital, Ordu-Baliq, destroying the Uyghur state and ruling Mongolia for a time. Their later history and their migration into the Tian Shan are much debated: some scholars place the first Kyrgyz arrivals in the 10th century, others link the main movement to the Mongol period and after, and by the 16th century Kyrgyz tribes were clearly established in the mountains. The Epic of Manas, an oral epic of hundreds of thousands of lines recited by bards called manaschi, tells of a hero who united the scattered tribes against their enemies and is central to Kyrgyz identity.

## Mongols, Moghulistan and the Kalmyks

In 1218 the forces of [Genghis Khan](/person/genghis-khan) took the lands of the Kara-Khitai, and the region became part of the [Mongol Empire](/place/mongol-empire); the Yenisei Kyrgyz had submitted a decade earlier. After the empire divided, the Tian Shan belonged to the [Chagatai Khanate](/place/chagatai-khanate) and from the mid-14th century to its eastern successor, Moghulistan, a realm of nomadic Mongol and Turkic tribes. Christian gravestones of the Church of the East from this era have been found near Issyk-Kul. Tamerlane campaigned repeatedly against Moghulistan in the late 14th century but never brought the northern mountains under lasting control.

As Moghulistan declined in the 16th century, the Kyrgyz tribes became largely independent, divided into a "right wing", a "left wing" and southern groups and ruled by tribal leaders called manaps and biys. In the 17th and 18th centuries they faced repeated invasions by the Oirats of the [Dzungar Khanate](/place/dzungar-khanate), which drove many Kyrgyz south into Ferghana and the Pamirs. When the [Qing dynasty](/place/qing-dynasty) destroyed the Dzungars in the 1750s, Kyrgyz chiefs sent envoys to the Qing court and accepted a loose, mostly nominal Chinese overlordship.

## Kokand, Ormon Khan and the Russian conquest

In the early 19th century the Uzbek Khanate of Kokand, based in the Ferghana Valley, extended its rule over the Kyrgyz, building fortresses such as Pishpek, the site of today's Bishkek, and collecting taxes from the tribes. Kyrgyz served in Kokand's armies and at times rose high at its court, but there were also revolts. In 1842 the northern tribes met in a kurultai and proclaimed the Sarybagysh chief Ormon Khan their ruler, driving out Kokand's garrisons from the Chuy and Issyk-Kul regions; his short-lived khanate broke apart when he was killed in a war with the rival Bugu tribe in the 1850s.

The Bugu then sought protection from the [Russian Empire](/place/russian-empire), which was advancing south from the Kazakh steppe. Russian forces took Pishpek in 1862, and the Treaty of Tarbagatai of 1864 with Qing China gave Russia most of the Issyk-Kul region. In 1876 Russia abolished the Kokand Khanate and annexed the Ferghana Valley and the southern mountains. In the Alay, the female ruler Kurmanjan Datka, after resisting, negotiated her people's submission. Russian and Ukrainian peasant settlers were given much of the best land in the north, and in 1916, when the tsarist government ordered Central Asians to be conscripted for labour behind the front in World War I, the Kyrgyz and Kazakhs rose in revolt. The rebellion was crushed with great brutality; tens of thousands of Kyrgyz died in the fighting, the reprisals and the flight across the mountains to China in winter, an event remembered as the Urkun ("exodus"). Estimates of the death toll vary widely.

## Soviet Kirghizia

After the [Russian Revolution](/event/russian-revolution), Soviet power was established in the region by 1919, after civil war and the Basmachi resistance in the south. In the national delimitation of Central Asia in 1924 the Kara-Kirghiz Autonomous Oblast was formed within Soviet Russia; it became an autonomous republic in 1926, when Pishpek was renamed Frunze, and on 5 December 1936 the Kirghiz Soviet Socialist Republic, a full republic of the [Soviet Union](/place/soviet-union). The Soviet state promoted literacy, a standard written Kyrgyz language (in Arabic, then Latin and later Cyrillic script), schools and hospitals, but it also forced the nomads into collective farms in the late 1920s and 1930s, causing the loss of much of the livestock, famine and flight. Under [Joseph Stalin](/person/joseph-stalin) much of the Kyrgyz political and intellectual elite was executed in the purges of 1937–38, many of them buried in a mass grave at Chong-Tash, now the Ata-Beyit memorial.

During World War II factories and people were evacuated to Frunze from western Russia, and many Kyrgyz served at the front. The republic later mined uranium, coal and gold and supplied hydroelectric power and meat to the Soviet economy, though it remained one of the poorest republics. Turdakun Usubaliyev led its Communist Party from 1961 to 1985, and the novelist Chingiz Aitmatov became one of the best-known Soviet writers.

## Independence and the Akayev years

Under [Mikhail Gorbachev](/person/mikhail-gorbachev)'s reforms, democratic groups appeared and ethnic tensions surfaced. In June 1990 a dispute over land near Osh, a city with a large Uzbek population, led to clashes between Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in which hundreds were killed. Discredited by the violence, the party leader Absamat Masaliyev failed to win the new presidency, and in October 1990 the republic's parliament chose instead Askar Akayev, a physicist and head of the Academy of Sciences. Akayev condemned the August 1991 coup in Moscow at once, and on 31 August 1991 Kyrgyzstan declared independence; it joined the Commonwealth of Independent States in December, as the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/event/dissolution-of-the-soviet-union) was completed.

Kyrgyzstan was at first seen as the most open of the Central Asian states. It introduced its own currency, the som, in 1993, privatised land and businesses and welcomed Western aid, while many Russians and other Slavs emigrated. The economy shrank severely, and over the 1990s Akayev strengthened his powers through referendums, while elections grew less fair. Islamist militants crossed from Tajikistan into the Batken region in 1999 and 2000. After the attacks of September 2001 the United States opened an air base at Manas near Bishkek to support its war in Afghanistan, and Russia opened its own base at Kant in 2003.

## Revolutions and the return of a strong presidency

Flawed parliamentary elections in 2005 set off protests in the south that reached Bishkek in March; protesters stormed the government building, and Akayev fled to Russia, in what became known as the Tulip Revolution. His successor, Kurmanbek Bakiyev, was accused of the same corruption and nepotism, and in April 2010 protests that began in Talas were met with gunfire in Bishkek, leaving dozens dead, before Bakiyev fled to Belarus. In June 2010 violence between Kyrgyz and Uzbeks in Osh and Jalal-Abad killed more than 400 people, most of them Uzbeks, and drove hundreds of thousands from their homes. An interim government under Roza Otunbayeva, the first woman to lead a Central Asian state, put a new constitution to a referendum that created a parliamentary republic, and in 2017 Almazbek Atambayev handed power to Sooronbay Jeenbekov, the first peaceful transfer between elected presidents in the region.

That system did not last. Atambayev was arrested in 2019 after a deadly raid on his compound, and in October 2020 protests against disputed parliamentary elections freed the nationalist politician Sadyr Japarov from prison and forced Jeenbekov to resign. Japarov was elected president in January 2021, and a referendum in April 2021 adopted a new constitution concentrating power in the presidency. Rights groups and journalists have reported growing pressure on the media and civil society, including the closure of independent outlets and a 2024 law on foreign-funded organisations. Armed clashes over the disputed border with Tajikistan in 2021 and September 2022 killed scores of people, but in March 2025 the two presidents signed a treaty settling the border. Parliament dissolved itself in 2025, and in snap elections on 30 November 2025 candidates loyal to Japarov won a large majority. The country's economy depends heavily on gold mining and on remittances from Kyrgyz workers in Russia, and its relations with its neighbours [Kazakhstan](/country/kazakhstan), Uzbekistan and [China](/country/china) remain central to its future.
