Jamaica was home to the Taíno for some seven centuries before Spanish conquest in the early 1500s destroyed their society. England seized the island in 1655 and turned it into the most valuable sugar colony of the British Caribbean, worked by hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans whose resistance runs through its history: the Maroon communities that forced the British to sign treaties in 1739, the great revolts of 1760 and 1831, and, after emancipation, the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865. Labour protests in 1938 created the unions and parties that led the island to universal suffrage in 1944 and independence in 1962. Since then Jamaica has been a parliamentary democracy within the Commonwealth, with an outsized cultural influence through reggae, Rastafari and its athletes, and persistent struggles with debt, poverty and violent crime.

## Xaymaca and the Taíno

The first people to settle Jamaica arrived by canoe around 600 CE, probably from the islands to the east. Archaeologists call them the Redware people after their distinctive pottery, found at coastal sites such as Little River in St Ann and Alligator Pond in Manchester, where they lived by fishing and hunting turtles and manatees. From around 800 a second wave, sharing the culture of the Taíno of Hispaniola and Cuba, spread across the island, which they called Xaymaca, often translated as land of wood and water.

Taíno villages of round, thatched houses were ruled by caciques and set among fields of cassava, maize and sweet potatoes, which they grew in mounds of ash-enriched earth. They made cassava bread, fished from large dugout canoes, wove cotton and carved spirit figures called zemis in wood and stone; three carved wooden zemis found in a Carpenter's Mountain cave in 1792 are among the finest surviving Taíno sculptures. Petroglyphs survive at sites such as Mountain River Cave and Canoe Valley. At its height the population may have reached about 60,000. Taíno words still used in English, including hammock, canoe, tobacco, barbecue and hurricane, are a reminder of their world.

## Spanish Jamaica

[Christopher Columbus](/explorer/christopher-columbus) landed on the north coast on 5 May 1494, on his second voyage, and fought a skirmish with Taíno warriors. On his fourth voyage, in 1503, his worm-eaten ships were beached at St Ann's Bay, and he and his crew were marooned for a year, depending on Taíno food until help arrived from Hispaniola. In 1509 Juan de Esquivel founded Sevilla la Nueva near St Ann's Bay, and in 1534 the capital moved to Villa de la Vega, later Santiago de la Vega and now Spanish Town, on the southern plain.

The [Spanish Empire](/place/spanish-empire) granted the island to the Columbus family, and it remained a backwater supplying cattle, hides, lard and cassava to Spanish expeditions on the mainland. The Taíno were forced to work under the encomienda system, and violence, overwork, hunger and European diseases destroyed their society within a few decades. Some fled into the mountains, where they mixed with escaped Africans, and their descendants and cultural traces survived among later Jamaicans, but as a distinct people the Taíno were gone by the early seventeenth century. The Spanish brought enslaved Africans to replace them, and by the 1650s the island had only a few thousand inhabitants, including Spanish settlers, a community of Portuguese Jewish origin, and enslaved and free Africans.

## English conquest and the buccaneers

In 1654 [Oliver Cromwell](/person/oliver-cromwell) sent a fleet to the Caribbean under William Penn and Robert Venables, his "Western Design" against Spain. After failing to take Santo Domingo, the force of about 7,000 men landed in Jamaica in May 1655 and overwhelmed its small garrison. Spanish guerrillas fought on, helped by enslaved Africans who had fled to the hills, until the English defeated them at Rio Nuevo in 1658 and won over the African leader Juan de Bolas. Spain recognised English possession in the Treaty of Madrid of 1670.

To defend the island, the governors invited buccaneers to use Port Royal, at the tip of the Palisadoes spit guarding Kingston harbour, which became one of the richest and most notorious towns in the Americas. From there Henry Morgan, armed with commissions from Jamaica's governors, raided Spanish towns including Portobelo and, in 1671, Panama City in [Panama](/country/panama). Morgan was later knighted and served as lieutenant governor. On 7 June 1692 an earthquake sank much of Port Royal beneath the sea, killing about 2,000 people, and survivors founded Kingston across the harbour.

## Sugar and slavery

From the late seventeenth century sugar transformed Jamaica. Planters, many of whom later returned to Britain as absentee owners, built great estates with boiling houses and mills, and the island became the largest sugar producer in the [British Empire](/place/british-empire), with a planter-dominated House of Assembly. The labour was done by enslaved Africans brought through the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade), many of them Akan-speaking people from the Gold Coast, today's [Ghana](/country/ghana), known to the British as Coromantee, as well as Igbo, Kongo and other West and West Central Africans. The enslaved population grew from under 45,000 at the start of the eighteenth century to more than 300,000 by 1800, outnumbering whites by more than ten to one, yet so many died from brutal labour, disease and punishment that numbers could only be maintained by continuous imports.

Enslaved people built a culture of their own within this system. They grew much of their food on provision grounds in the hills, sold the surplus in Sunday markets, and created the Jamaican Creole language, music, festivals such as Jonkonnu, and spiritual traditions including obeah and later Myal, drawing on African practices. Christianity spread among them mainly through Black Baptist preachers and Moravian, Methodist and Baptist missionaries from the late eighteenth century.

## The Maroons

When the Spanish were driven out in 1655, many of the Africans they had enslaved escaped into the mountainous interior. They were joined over the following decades by people fleeing English plantations, including the participants in several large revolts led by newly arrived Akan captives between 1673 and 1690. Two main groups emerged: the Leeward Maroons of the Cockpit Country in the west, led in the early eighteenth century by Cudjoe, and the Windward Maroons of the Blue Mountains in the east, whose leaders included Quao and Nanny, a spiritual and military leader of Akan origin whose stronghold was Nanny Town.

In the First Maroon War of the 1720s and 1730s, Maroon fighters used ambush and their knowledge of the terrain to defeat colonial troops and militia again and again. Unable to win, the British signed a treaty with Cudjoe in 1739 and with the Windward Maroons in 1740. The Maroons received land and self-government in their towns, among them Accompong, Trelawny Town, Moore Town, Scott's Hall and Charles Town; in return they agreed to return runaways and help suppress slave revolts, which set them against the enslaved majority. In 1795 a dispute over the flogging of two Maroons led to the Second Maroon War with Trelawny Town. After surrendering on a promise of leniency, more than 500 Trelawny Maroons were deported to Nova Scotia in 1796 and in 1800 moved to [Sierra Leone](/country/sierra-leone), where some later returned. The Maroon communities still exist, and Nanny was declared a National Hero of Jamaica in 1975.

## Rebellion and emancipation

In 1760 an Akan man named Tacky led a rising in St Mary parish that spread across the island; it took colonial forces, aided by Maroons, months to suppress, and hundreds of rebels were killed or executed. At Christmas 1831 Samuel Sharpe, an enslaved Baptist deacon, organised a strike for wages that became the Baptist War, the largest slave uprising in the British Caribbean, involving perhaps 60,000 people. About 200 rebels were killed in the fighting and more than 300 executed afterwards, including Sharpe in May 1832. The rebellion and the brutality of the planters' reprisals, reported in Britain by missionaries such as William Knibb, strengthened the abolitionist cause. Britain had ended its slave trade in 1807, and the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833 freed the enslaved on 1 August 1834, though they were obliged to work unpaid for their former owners as "apprentices" until 1838. The slaveholders received compensation from the British government; the enslaved received none.

Many freed people left the estates to found free villages, often around Baptist churches, and became small farmers. Planters turned to indentured workers from India from 1845, and later from China, whose descendants form part of Jamaica's population today.

## Morant Bay and the Crown colony

Freedom brought little economic power. Land was hard to obtain, wages were low, and only a small number of propertied men could vote. In October 1865, after a scuffle at the courthouse in Morant Bay, St Thomas, the Baptist deacon Paul Bogle led several hundred people in an attack on the courthouse in which the local custos and others were killed. Governor Edward Eyre declared martial law, and soldiers and Maroon allies killed 439 Black Jamaicans, most of whom had taken no part, flogged hundreds more and burned many homes; 354 people were executed after courts martial, among them Bogle and the mixed-race politician George William Gordon, whom Eyre had arrested in Kingston and moved to the martial-law zone. The response divided Britain: a Jamaica Committee led by [John Stuart Mill](/person/john-stuart-mill) and including [Charles Darwin](/person/charles-darwin) sought to prosecute Eyre, while Thomas Carlyle and [Charles Dickens](/person/charles-dickens) were among his defenders. In 1866 the Assembly voted itself out of existence and Jamaica became a Crown colony ruled directly from London. The capital moved from Spanish Town to Kingston in 1872, and from the 1870s bananas, grown largely by small farmers, overtook sugar as the main export. An earthquake in 1907 destroyed much of Kingston.

## Garvey, Rastafari and the road to independence

Jamaicans emigrated in large numbers to build the Panama Canal, to Cuba's sugar fields and to the United States. Marcus Garvey, born in St Ann, founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association in Kingston in 1914 and from New York built the largest mass movement of people of African descent of the early twentieth century, preaching racial pride and a return to Africa. In the 1930s, inspired in part by Garvey and by the coronation of [Haile Selassie](/person/haile-selassie) as emperor of [Ethiopia](/country/ethiopia) in 1930, preachers such as Leonard Howell founded the Rastafari movement.

The [Great Depression](/event/great-depression) brought strikes and riots across the British Caribbean. In 1938 a strike at the Frome sugar estate, where police killed several workers, spread to the Kingston docks and across the island. From the unrest emerged Alexander Bustamante's Bustamante Industrial Trade Union and the People's National Party of his cousin Norman Manley, founded in 1938; Bustamante launched the Jamaica Labour Party in 1943. A new constitution of 1944 introduced universal adult suffrage and a measure of self-government. After the [Second World War](/event/world-war-ii), many Jamaicans emigrated to Britain, beginning with the passengers of the Windrush in 1948, while bauxite mining made the island one of the world's leading producers of the ore. Jamaica joined the West Indies Federation in 1958, but in a referendum in 1961 a narrow majority voted to leave, and Jamaica became independent on its own on 6 August 1962, with [Elizabeth II](/person/elizabeth-ii) as queen and Bustamante as prime minister.

## Independent Jamaica

The two parties founded in the 1940s have alternated in power ever since. Michael Manley, Norman's son, led the PNP to victory in 1972 with a programme of democratic socialism, raised taxes on the bauxite companies, and built close ties with [Fidel Castro](/person/fidel-castro)'s [Cuba](/country/cuba), alarming the United States during the [Cold War](/event/cold-war). Rival party gangs armed in the poorer neighbourhoods of Kingston, and the 1980 election campaign, won by the JLP's Edward Seaga, saw more than 800 people killed. In those years reggae, carried around the world by Bob Marley, Peter Tosh and others, made Jamaican music and Rastafari globally known. Hurricane Gilbert devastated the island in 1988.

Under P. J. Patterson, prime minister from 1992 to 2006, and then Portia Simpson-Miller, the first woman to hold the office, Jamaica struggled with heavy public debt and a murder rate among the highest in the world, fuelled by gangs and the transit of cocaine. In May 2010 an operation by soldiers and police to arrest the gang leader Christopher Coke for extradition to the United States left at least 73 civilians dead in the Tivoli Gardens area of Kingston. The athlete Usain Bolt and other sprinters became national icons from 2008. Andrew Holness of the JLP became prime minister in 2016, and in the general election of 3 September 2025 his party won a third consecutive term, with 35 of the 63 seats. On 28 October 2025 Hurricane Melissa made landfall in the southwest as a Category 5 storm, among the strongest hurricanes ever recorded at landfall in the Atlantic. It killed at least 45 people in Jamaica and damaged some 156,000 homes, and losses were estimated at close to US$10 billion, more than 40 percent of the country's annual output.
