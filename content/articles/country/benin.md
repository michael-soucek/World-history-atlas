Benin is a narrow country in West Africa, reaching 650 kilometres from the lagoons of the Atlantic coast to the Niger River. Its south was the home of Gbe-speaking kingdoms, the most powerful of which was Dahomey, a highly organised military state that grew rich and feared through the Atlantic slave trade; the north was divided among Bariba, Dendi and other peoples. [France](/country/france) conquered Dahomey in the 1890s and ruled it as a colony until independence in 1960. The modern country took the name Benin in 1975, from the Bight of Benin; it has no connection with the historic [Kingdom of Benin](/place/kingdom-of-benin), whose capital, Benin City, lies in what is now [Nigeria](/country/nigeria). In 1990 Benin became one of the first African states to move peacefully from military dictatorship to multiparty democracy.

## Early peoples and the iron age

People have lived in the region since the Stone Age, and archaeologists working on the Abomey Plateau have found traces of farming villages from the seventh century BCE onwards. Iron smelting appears by around 1000 CE and, between about 1400 and 1600, reached an industrial scale: dozens of furnace sites across southern Benin left slag heaps suggesting several thousand tonnes of iron, probably traded north towards the states of the Sahel. Markets along the coast sold salt, cloth, pottery, fish and iron long before Europeans arrived.

The peoples of the south speak the Gbe languages, including Fon, Aja, Gun and Ewe, and many of their ruling families traced their origins to the town of Tado, in modern [Togo](/country/togo). Historians now treat these traditions as political charters linking related dynasties rather than accounts of a single migration. Yoruba communities settled the south-east, linked to the Yoruba city of Ketu, while the north saw the rise of the Bariba (Baatombu) kingdoms of Borgu, centred on Nikki, which were famed for their cavalry, and Dendi traders arrived from the Niger valley who spread [Islam](/culture/islam). The Atakora mountains of the north-west were home to peoples such as the Betammaribe, known for their fortified tower houses.

## Allada, Hueda and the Slave Coast

By the sixteenth century the leading power of the coast was the kingdom of Allada, whose capital merchants of the [Portuguese Empire](/place/portuguese-empire) were visiting by the mid-1500s; captives described as "Arada" appear in records in Peru from the 1560s. In the seventeenth century the Dutch, French and English all traded there, buying slaves in exchange for cloth, cowrie shells, metal goods and guns. The small neighbouring kingdom of Hueda, with its capital at Savi and its port at Ouidah (Whydah), broke away from Allada's control in the later 1600s and became one of the busiest slave-trading ports in Africa. The shore from the Volta to [Lagos](/history-of/lagos) became known to Europeans as the Slave Coast. Rivalries among officials and European merchants weakened both kingdoms, opening the way for an inland power.

## The Kingdom of Dahomey

Dahomey emerged in the seventeenth century among the Fon people on the Abomey Plateau, about a hundred kilometres inland. According to tradition its founders were princes of Allada, and its name, meaning "in the belly of Dan", recalls a story that the king Dakodonu killed a local chief called Dan and built his palace over him, although historians suspect the legend was invented later to justify the dynasty. King Houegbadja, who reigned from around 1645, is usually regarded as the real founder and began the Royal Palaces of Abomey, which each later king extended. His grandson Agaja (1708 to 1740) built a disciplined standing army armed with muskets, conquered Allada in 1724 and Hueda in 1727, and so gained direct access to the coast.

These conquests drew the hostility of the [Oyo Empire](/place/oyo-empire), the Yoruba cavalry power to the east, which invaded Dahomey repeatedly from 1726. By 1748 Dahomey had accepted the status of a tributary of Oyo, sending annual payments while keeping its internal independence. Under Tegbesu (1740 to 1774) the king's revenue from the sale of captives grew enormously, and a royal official, the yovogan, controlled all trade with Europeans at Ouidah. The kingdom was one of the most centralised states in Africa: the king governed through ministers, a census and a system of taxes and tolls, and the queen mother (kpojito) and senior palace women held real power.

Dahomey was organised for war. Its army made annual campaigns against neighbouring peoples, especially the Mahi to the north and Yoruba communities to the east, and the captives were sold at Ouidah or put to work on royal plantations. Some were killed in the Annual Customs, great ceremonies in honour of the royal ancestors that combined the distribution of gifts, military parades and human sacrifice. A corps of women soldiers, called the Agojie or Mino and known to Europeans as the "Amazons", grew from the eighteenth century into a large and respected part of the army. The kingdom also developed a rich artistic culture of appliqué cloths, brass figures and carved thrones, and the religion of Vodun, centred on spirits such as the python deity Dangbe at Ouidah, was closely tied to royal power.

## The slave trade and its legacy

The trade through Ouidah and the other ports of the Bight of Benin was one of the largest branches of the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade). Around two million people are estimated to have been shipped from the Bight of Benin as a whole, many of them Yoruba, Fon, Mahi and other prisoners of Dahomey's wars, bound for Brazil, the French Caribbean colony of Saint-Domingue, now [Haiti](/country/haiti), and elsewhere. Their religions survive in Haitian Vodou, in the Candomblé of Bahia and in other Afro-Atlantic traditions. Merchants from [Brazil](/country/brazil), such as Francisco Félix de Souza, who became the king's agent at Ouidah, settled on the coast, and freed Afro-Brazilians who returned from Bahia in the nineteenth century formed a distinctive community of traders and builders.

Ghezo, who seized the throne from his brother Adandozan in 1818, ended tribute to Oyo as that empire collapsed in the 1820s and expanded the army, including the women's corps. When Britain campaigned to suppress the slave trade in the 1840s, he argued that his kingdom depended on it, and Dahomey's exports continued while palm oil, produced by enslaved workers on plantations, became a growing second trade. Dahomey suffered defeats against the Egba Yoruba city of Abeokuta in 1851 and 1864.

## French conquest and colonial Dahomey

The coastal kingdom of Porto-Novo, a rival of Dahomey, sought French protection in 1863 and again in 1882, and France established itself at Cotonou. Disputes over Cotonou and the Ouémé valley led to a first war with Dahomey in 1890. In 1892 France sent a column under Colonel Alfred-Amédée Dodds, a Senegalese-born officer, with modern rifles and artillery. After fierce battles in which the Agojie fought at close quarters, King Béhanzin burned Abomey and retreated north as the French entered it in November 1892. He surrendered in January 1894 and was exiled to Martinique and then Algeria, where he died in 1906. France installed a puppet king, then abolished the monarchy's powers, and campaigns in the north subdued Borgu and the Atakora by around 1900.

The colony of Dahomey became part of [French West Africa](/place/french-west-africa) in 1904. France built a port at Cotonou and a railway inland, and taxed the population and imposed forced labour on roads and plantations. Catholic and Protestant missions opened schools, and southern Dahomey produced so many educated clerks, teachers and civil servants for the French administration that it was nicknamed the "Latin Quarter of Africa". Revolts in the north and the Holli country in the 1910s were suppressed. After [World War II](/event/world-war-ii) Africans gained representation in the French parliament, and political parties formed around three regional leaders: Hubert Maga in the north, Sourou-Migan Apithy in Porto-Novo and Justin Ahomadégbé in Abomey.

## Independence, coups and Marxism

Dahomey became independent on 1 August 1960, with Maga as president, as part of the wider [decolonisation of Africa](/event/decolonisation-of-africa). Rivalry between the three regional leaders and economic hardship produced chronic instability: the army seized power in 1963, 1965, 1967 and 1969, and an experiment in 1970 with a presidential council in which the three leaders would rotate in office lasted only until 1972. On 26 October 1972 Major Mathieu Kérékou took power in a coup. In 1974 he proclaimed Marxism-Leninism as the official ideology, nationalised banks and the oil industry, and in 1975 renamed the country the People's Republic of Benin, a name meant to avoid identifying the whole nation with the Fon kingdom of Dahomey. The Party of the People's Revolution of Benin was the only legal party, and opponents were jailed.

## Democratic Benin

By the late 1980s Benin was bankrupt; civil servants and teachers went unpaid and students and workers went on strike. In February 1990 Kérékou convened a National Conference of political, religious and civic groups, which declared itself sovereign, abandoned Marxism and appointed a transitional government. A new constitution was approved, and in March 1991 Nicéphore Soglo defeated Kérékou, who became one of the first African leaders on the mainland to lose power at the ballot box and accept defeat. The national conference became a model for democratic transitions in other French-speaking African countries. Kérékou returned through elections in 1996 and 2001, and the banker Thomas Boni Yayi won in 2006 and 2011, with power changing hands peacefully each time.

The cotton magnate Patrice Talon was elected president in 2016 and re-elected in 2021. His government pursued economic reforms and infrastructure building, and in 2021 France returned 26 royal treasures looted from Abomey in 1892. Critics and international monitors reported a decline in democratic freedoms, as new electoral rules excluded the main opposition from the 2019 parliamentary elections and opposition figures were prosecuted. Jihadist groups based in the Sahel, active in neighbouring [Burkina Faso](/country/burkina-faso) and [Niger](/country/niger), attacked the northern national parks from 2021, killing soldiers and rangers. In July 2025 a new law offered citizenship to people of African descent whose ancestors were taken in the slave trade. On 7 December 2025 a group of soldiers announced on television that they had overthrown Talon, but loyal forces, supported by Nigeria and the regional bloc ECOWAS, put down the attempt within hours. In the presidential election of 12 April 2026 the finance minister Romuald Wadagni, Talon's chosen successor, won about 94 percent of the vote, in a contest from which the main opposition party was absent.
