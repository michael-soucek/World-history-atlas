Barbados is the most easterly island of the Caribbean, a low coral island that Amerindian peoples settled for well over a thousand years before it was left empty in the sixteenth century. English settlers arrived in 1627, and within two decades the island had become the first great sugar colony of the English Atlantic, where a small planter class grew rich on the forced labour of enslaved Africans who soon formed the large majority of the population. The plantation system Barbados developed, and the slave laws it wrote, spread to Jamaica, the Carolinas and beyond. After emancipation the planter elite kept its grip for another century, until the labour movement of the 1930s opened the way to universal suffrage, independence in 1966 and a republic in 2021.

## The first peoples

Shell tools found with remains dated to around 1600 BCE suggest that people visited or briefly lived on Barbados in the second millennium BCE, but the evidence is thin. Fully documented settlement began between about 350 and 650 CE, when farmers and potters of the Saladoid-Barrancoid tradition arrived from the South American mainland. Further waves of Arawak-speaking settlers followed around 800 and again in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, building villages near the coast, growing cassava and other crops, fishing the reefs and making fine painted pottery. The Kalinago, called Caribs by Europeans, visited the island by canoe, but there is no evidence that they settled there.

Amerindian life on Barbados ended in the early sixteenth century. Spanish slave raiders working out of Hispaniola carried off inhabitants of the smaller islands to labour in the mines and pearl fisheries, and those who escaped seem to have fled to more defensible, mountainous islands. By 1541 a Spanish writer described Barbados as uninhabited. Portuguese sailors bound for Brazil gave the island its name, Os Barbados, "the bearded ones", probably after its bearded fig trees, but neither Spain nor [Portugal](/country/portugal) settled it.

## The English colony

An English ship under John Powell claimed the island for James I in 1625, and on 17 February 1627 a party led by his brother Henry Powell landed near what became Holetown, with some 80 settlers and a handful of indentured labourers. The venture was financed by the London merchant William Courten, but the Earl of Carlisle soon won a rival royal grant, and his men founded a settlement on Carlisle Bay that grew into Bridgetown. A House of Assembly met from 1639, making it one of the oldest legislatures in the Americas. Shortly after the settlement began, about 40 Indigenous people were brought from the Guiana coast to teach the settlers to grow local crops, and were then held as slaves.

The first economy rested on tobacco and cotton grown by English, Scottish and Irish indentured servants, who worked for five or more years in return for passage and a small payment. Conditions were harsh and deaths far outnumbered marriages. In the 1650s Oliver Cromwell's regime shipped thousands of Irish and Scottish prisoners of war, vagrants and kidnapped people to the island to be sold as servants, a practice so notorious that it gave English the verb "to Barbados". During the English Civil War the colony's assembly declared for Charles II, and in 1651 a fleet sent by the Commonwealth of [Oliver Cromwell](/person/oliver-cromwell) blockaded the island. The royalists surrendered in January 1652 on terms set out in the Charter of Barbados, which confirmed the colony's assembly and rights of local self-government.

## The sugar revolution

In the 1640s sugar transformed Barbados. Cane and the techniques of large-scale sugar making were brought from Dutch Brazil, and merchants of the [Dutch Republic](/place/dutch-republic), including Sephardic Jews who had fled Brazil, supplied credit, mills and enslaved Africans and carried the sugar to Europe. Within a generation the island's forests were cleared and its small farms swallowed by large estates. Barbados became, for a time, the richest colony in English America: by 1660 it generated more trade than all of England's other colonies combined, and Bridgetown ranked with Boston as one of the largest towns in the English Atlantic. In 1680 over half the island's farmland belonged to some 175 great planters, each owning at least 60 enslaved people.

Sugar demanded relentless labour, and the planters met it by buying Africans. The black population, a few hundred in the early 1640s, passed 20,000 by 1655 and outnumbered white people by the 1670s. By 1684 there were about 46,500 enslaved Africans to 19,500 whites, and by the mid-eighteenth century around 65,000 to 18,000. Thousands of white smallholders and former servants, squeezed off the land, left for Jamaica, the Leeward Islands, Suriname and North America. Barbadian planters and their enslaved workers were among the founders of the colony of Carolina in 1670, carrying the Barbadian plantation model to the mainland of the future [United States](/country/united-states).

## Slavery and the slave codes

The people forced to work the Barbadian estates came through the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade), first via Dutch traders and then by the Royal African Company and private English merchants, mostly from the Gold Coast, the Bight of Biafra and other parts of West Africa. The work of cutting cane, feeding the mills and boiling the juice was gruelling and dangerous, and in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries deaths outstripped births so badly that planters counted on buying replacements every year. Enslaved people also grew provisions, tended livestock, maintained the estates and worked as artisans, and they built families, music and religious life under conditions designed to deny them any rights.

In 1661 the Barbados assembly passed an act for governing enslaved people that treated them as property, denied them legal protections and set brutal punishments, including mutilation and death, for resistance. Revised in 1676, 1682 and 1688, the Barbadian code became the template for slave laws in Jamaica, South Carolina and other English colonies. The codes deliberately separated Africans from poor whites, who kept legal rights that the enslaved were denied, building a racial hierarchy into law. Enslaved people planned revolts in 1675, 1692 and at other times, but on a small, flat, densely farmed island with a large militia, the plots were betrayed or crushed before they spread.

## Bussa's Rebellion and emancipation

By the eighteenth century Barbados had been overtaken by larger colonies such as Jamaica, and it suffered from falling sugar prices, wartime disruption of food supplies during the [American Revolution](/event/american-revolution) and a hurricane in 1780 that killed more than 4,000 people. Unlike most sugar colonies, its enslaved population began to grow naturally around 1800, so the abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 did not bring an immediate crisis to the planters.

At Easter 1816, enslaved people rose in the largest rebellion in the island's history. Encouraged by news of the abolition movement in Britain and angered by planters' refusal to reform the slave laws, perhaps 20,000 people from more than 70 plantations took part. The best-known leader was Bussa, an African-born head ranger, and a literate woman, Nanny Grigg, was among the organisers. The rebels burned cane fields and drove whites off estates, but killed very few people. The militia and British troops crushed the rising within days. About 120 rebels died in fighting or were summarily executed, another 144 were executed after trials, and most of the rest were deported. Bussa is now a National Hero of Barbados.

Parliament abolished slavery across the [British Empire](/place/british-empire) from 1 August 1834, but freed people in Barbados were forced to serve a four-year apprenticeship, unpaid for most of their working week, until full freedom came on 1 August 1838. Former owners received compensation from the British government; the formerly enslaved received nothing.

## The planters' island after slavery

Freedom brought little land. With almost every acre in cane, most freed people had to stay on the estates as tenants whose right to a house plot depended on working for the planter at low wages. Barbados kept its own assembly when other islands were turned into Crown colonies, but the vote was restricted by property qualifications, and a small white elite of planters and merchants, together with a few prosperous people of colour, continued to rule. A cholera epidemic in 1854 killed more than 20,000 people, and riots broke out in 1876 when Governor John Pope Hennessy pressed for union with the Windward Islands.

Emigration became a lifeline. Barbadians went to Trinidad, British Guiana and the United States, and between 1904 and 1914 tens of thousands of men worked on the building of the Panama Canal, sending home remittances known as "Panama money". Returning soldiers from the [First World War](/event/world-war-i), the journalist Clennell Wickham and the doctor Charles Duncan O'Neal's Democratic League began to challenge the old order in the 1920s.

## Labour, reform and independence

The [Great Depression](/event/great-depression) brought wage cuts, unemployment and rising prices, and in July 1937 the deportation of the Trinidad-born organiser Clement Payne set off riots in which 14 people were killed. A royal commission under Lord Moyne investigated the unrest across the British Caribbean. The lawyer Grantley Adams, whose evidence impressed the commission, founded the Barbados Labour Party in 1938 and led the new Barbados Workers' Union from 1941. Property qualifications for the vote were lowered and women gained the vote in 1942, universal adult suffrage was introduced in 1950, and in the 1951 election the BLP won a clear majority. Adams became premier in 1953, and in 1958 he became prime minister of the West Indies Federation, which collapsed in 1962 after [Jamaica](/country/jamaica) and [Trinidad and Tobago](/country/trinidad-and-tobago) left.

Errol Barrow, a former Royal Air Force navigator in the [Second World War](/event/world-war-ii), broke with Adams to found the Democratic Labour Party in 1955. The DLP won the 1961 election, and Barrow's government introduced free secondary education and began to industrialise. After an attempt to form a smaller federation failed, Barbados negotiated its own independence, which came on 30 November 1966, with Barrow as prime minister and [Elizabeth II](/person/elizabeth-ii) as Queen of Barbados.

## The independent state

Barbados became known for stable parliamentary government, with power alternating peacefully between the BLP and the DLP. Barrow helped found the Caribbean Free Trade Association and its successor CARICOM. Tom Adams, son of Grantley Adams, led the BLP to power in 1976 and in 1983 allowed the island to be used as a base for the American-led invasion of [Grenada](/country/grenada), which Barrow condemned. Tourism and offshore finance gradually replaced sugar as the mainstays of the economy, and Barbados reached one of the highest standards of living in the region. Owen Arthur, prime minister from 1994 to 2008, favoured becoming a republic, but a planned referendum never took place.

## The republic

Mia Mottley led the BLP to a sweeping victory in 2018, becoming the first woman to lead the country. In September 2020 her government announced that Barbados would replace the monarchy with a president. Parliament passed the necessary constitutional amendment in October 2021, the governor-general Sandra Mason was chosen as the first president with the support of both government and opposition, and Barbados became a republic on 30 November 2021, the 55th anniversary of independence. Prince Charles attended the ceremony in Bridgetown. The country remained a member of the Commonwealth.

The BLP won all 30 seats in the House of Assembly in January 2022. A Constitutional Reform Commission reported in 2024, Jeffrey Bostic succeeded Mason as president on 30 November 2025, and in the election of 11 February 2026 the BLP again took every seat, with about 70 percent of the vote, giving Mottley a third term.
