Suriname, on the north-eastern shoulder of South America, is mostly rainforest, with nearly all its people living on a narrow coastal plain around the capital, Paramaribo. Indigenous peoples lived along its rivers and coast for thousands of years before the English founded a sugar colony in 1650 and the Dutch seized it in 1667. For two centuries it was one of the harshest plantation colonies of the Americas, worked by enslaved Africans, many of whom escaped into the forest and formed Maroon nations that won their freedom by treaty a century before abolition. After 1873 indentured labourers from India, Java and China transformed its population, making it one of the most diverse societies in the world. Independent since 1975, Suriname lived through military rule under Dési Bouterse, the December murders of 1982 and a civil war in the interior before returning to elected government.

## Indigenous peoples

People have lived in the Guianas for many thousands of years. In the southern interior, in the caves of Werehpai near the modern village of Kwamalasamutu, more than 300 petroglyphs were carved by people whose charcoal remains have been dated to between about 5,000 and 4,200 years ago, the largest such site in Suriname and perhaps in all the Guianas. Over the following millennia communities of farmers and fishers settled the rivers and the swampy coast, growing manioc, the staple of the tropical lowlands, together with other crops, and making pottery, hammocks and canoes. On the coastal plain, earthen mounds raised above the swamps in pre-Columbian times hold the remains of villages.

By the time Europeans arrived, the coast was home to two main peoples. The Lokono, often called Arawaks, spoke an Arawakan language and were linked to related peoples across the Guianas and the Caribbean. The Kaliña, or Caribs, spoke a Cariban language and were known as traders and warriors who travelled by canoe along the coast and the rivers. Deeper in the forest lived the Tiriyó (Trio), the Wayana, the Akurio and others, smaller groups who moved between hunting grounds and village gardens and traded with neighbours across what are now Brazil, Guyana and French Guiana. Society was organised around villages led by headmen, and religious life centred on shamans who mediated with the spirits of forest and water. These peoples were never conquered in a single campaign; contact with the outside world reached some interior groups only in the twentieth century, and the Akurio were among the last to establish sustained contact, in the late 1960s.

## European rivals and the English colony

Spanish navigators sailed along the Guiana coast from the end of the fifteenth century during the [Age of Discovery](/event/age-of-discovery), and in the early seventeenth century Dutch, English and French traders visited to barter with Indigenous peoples. A Dutch trading post existed briefly in 1613, an English tobacco settlement around 1630 failed, and the French built an outpost in 1640. Permanent colonisation began in 1650, when Lord Francis Willoughby, the royalist governor of Barbados, sponsored an expedition that founded a plantation colony on the Suriname River. Settlers came from Barbados and England, and Jewish families, some arriving from Dutch Brazil, received religious freedom and became important planters. By 1663 the colony had about 50 sugar plantations, around 1,000 white settlers and about 3,000 enslaved Africans, as well as Indigenous labourers.

In February 1667, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War, a fleet from the Dutch province of Zeeland under Abraham Crijnssen captured the colony and renamed its fort Fort Zeelandia. The Treaty of Breda that year left Suriname to the Dutch, while England kept the former Dutch colony of New Netherland, and the Treaty of Westminster in 1674 confirmed the arrangement. Most English planters left for Jamaica in 1675.

## The Dutch plantation colony

From 1683 the colony was run by the Society of Suriname, owned jointly by the city of [Amsterdam](/history-of/amsterdam), the Dutch West India Company and the family of Cornelis van Aerssen van Sommelsdijck, who became governor. The [Dutch Republic](/place/dutch-republic) treated Suriname as a source of tropical produce: sugar first, then coffee, cocoa and cotton, all shipped to Amsterdam. Plantations spread along the Suriname, Commewijne and Cottica rivers, and along the Suriname River the Jewish planter community built its own town, Jodensavanne, with one of the oldest synagogues in the Americas. Paramaribo grew as the port and seat of government.

The colony's relations with the Indigenous peoples began in war. Colonists had enslaved Indigenous people as well as Africans, and from 1678 Kaliña and other groups attacked the plantations in what the Dutch called the Indian War. In 1686 van Aerssen made peace, and the settlement recognised that Indigenous people were free and could not be enslaved on account of their origin. Disease and displacement nonetheless reduced the coastal peoples sharply, and many withdrew inland.

The plantations depended on Africans brought through the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade). By 1713 about 13,000 enslaved people worked on some 200 plantations, and over the colonial period Suriname absorbed a large share of the more than 600,000 Africans whom Dutch traders transported to the Americas. Death rates were extremely high, and the colony became notorious even among contemporaries for the brutality of its punishments, including mutilation and execution for those who resisted or ran away. Enslaved people preserved and reshaped African languages, music and religious practice, and from their speech grew Sranan Tongo, the English-based creole that remains Suriname's common language.

## The Maroon nations

The forest offered escape. From the late seventeenth century enslaved Africans fled the plantations, alone, in small groups or in mass uprisings, and formed communities deep in the interior, sometimes joining or learning from Indigenous peoples. They came from many West and Central African peoples, among them Akan, Fon and Kongo, and built new societies with their own creole languages, political institutions headed by paramount chiefs, and religious traditions drawing on African, Indigenous and European elements. Maroon raids on the plantations, to free relatives, seize goods and weaken the colony, kept the colonists in constant fear, and expensive military expeditions repeatedly failed to destroy them.

In the end the colony made peace. In 1760 the Ndyuka signed a treaty that recognised their freedom and territory, followed in 1762 by the Saramaka and in 1767 by the Matawai. The treaties also obliged the Maroons to return future runaways, and the colony paid them regular tribute in goods. The Boni, or Aluku, who formed later, were refused peace; the colonists fought a long war against them from the 1760s, building a military cordon path to protect the plantations, and the Aluku eventually withdrew across the Marowijne River into what became French Guiana. Today the six Maroon peoples, the Saramaka, Ndyuka, Matawai, Kwinti, Aluku and Paramaka, make up roughly 15 percent of Suriname's population, and their eighteenth-century treaties remain central to their claims to the land.

## British interlude and the end of slavery

During the wars of the French Revolution and the [Napoleonic Wars](/event/napoleonic-wars), Britain occupied Suriname from 1799 to 1802 and again from 1804 to 1816, before returning it to the Netherlands. The British abolition of the slave trade in 1807 cut off new arrivals, and the Dutch followed in 1814. Moravian missionaries spread [Christianity](/culture/christianity) among the enslaved population in the nineteenth century. Slavery itself was abolished on 1 July 1863, later than in the British and French colonies. The Dutch government paid owners 300 guilders for each enslaved person, while those freed received nothing and were required to keep working on the plantations under state supervision for another ten years. The anniversary is celebrated as Keti Koti, "the chains are broken". Many freed people left the plantations for Paramaribo when the supervision ended in 1873.

## Indentured labour and a new society

To replace them, planters turned to contract labour from Asia. Chinese workers had already arrived from the 1850s. From 1873, under an agreement with Britain, indentured labourers from British [India](/country/india), mostly from today's Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, were shipped to Suriname; conditions were harsh, and critics called the system a new form of slavery, but many stayed when their contracts ended and became farmers. From 1890 the colonial government also recruited about 33,000 contract labourers from Java in the [Dutch East Indies](/place/dutch-east-indies), a migration that continued until 1939. The descendants of these migrants, together with the Afro-Surinamese, Maroons, Indigenous peoples and smaller groups of Chinese, Lebanese and European origin, created a society in which no single group forms a majority and politics long followed ethnic lines.

The plantation economy declined, but the twentieth century brought gold, rubber and above all bauxite, the ore of aluminium, mined from 1916 by a subsidiary of the American company Alcoa. During the [Second World War](/event/world-war-ii), with the Netherlands under German occupation, [United States](/country/united-states) troops were stationed in Suriname from 1941 to protect the bauxite mines, which supplied much of the aluminium for Allied aircraft. In the 1960s the Afobaka dam, built to power an aluminium smelter, flooded about half of the Saramaka homeland and forced thousands of people to move.

## Autonomy and independence

Suriname held its first elections under universal suffrage in 1949, and in 1954 the Charter for the Kingdom of the Netherlands made it a self-governing country within the kingdom, with the [Netherlands](/country/netherlands) retaining control of defence and foreign affairs. Political parties were organised largely along ethnic lines, chiefly the National Party of Suriname, rooted in the Creole population, and the Progressive Reform Party (VHP), representing Hindustanis. In 1974 the government of Henck Arron announced its aim of independence, and despite the misgivings of many Hindustanis, Suriname became independent on 25 November 1975, with Johan Ferrier as president and Arron as prime minister. Fearing instability, about a third of the population emigrated to the Netherlands in the years around independence, creating a large diaspora that has remained closely connected to the country.

## Military rule, the December murders and the Interior War

On 25 February 1980 sixteen non-commissioned officers led by Sergeant-Major Dési Bouterse overthrew the Arron government in the Sergeants' Coup. Bouterse ruled as chairman of the National Military Council, and the regime moved towards the left and towards [Cuba](/country/cuba) during the [Cold War](/event/cold-war), suppressing opposition as it went. On the night of 7 to 8 December 1982, fifteen prominent critics of the regime, among them lawyers, journalists, a university lecturer, businessmen, soldiers and a trade union leader, were taken from their homes or prison to Fort Zeelandia, Bouterse's headquarters, where they were tortured and shot. The regime claimed they had been killed trying to escape. The Netherlands froze its development aid, and many more Surinamese fled abroad.

In July 1986 a rebel group, the Jungle Commando, led by Ronnie Brunswijk, a former bodyguard of Bouterse, began an insurgency in the east, drawing its fighters largely from the Maroons. The army responded with reprisals against Maroon communities. On 29 November 1986 soldiers surrounded the Ndyuka village of Moiwana and killed at least 39 people, mainly women and children, and burned the village; survivors fled to French Guiana, where thousands of Maroon refugees eventually lived in camps. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights later held the state responsible, and in 2006 President Ronald Venetiaan apologised to the Ndyuka. An Indigenous armed group, the Tucayana Amazonas, also emerged in 1989. Civilian government was restored after elections in 1987, but Bouterse overthrew it in the so-called Telephone Coup of December 1990. A peace agreement signed in August 1992 by the army, the Jungle Commando and the Tucayana ended the Interior War, in which several hundred civilians died.

## Democracy and the Bouterse question

Elections in 1991 brought Ronald Venetiaan to the presidency, and the country alternated between coalitions led by Venetiaan and by Bouterse's National Democratic Party (NDP). In 1999 a Dutch court convicted Bouterse in absentia of cocaine trafficking. Indigenous and Maroon communities pressed for recognition of their collective land rights, winning judgments from the Inter-American Court of Human Rights for the Saramaka in 2007 and for the Kaliña and Lokono in 2015, as logging and gold mining spread into the interior.

In 2010 Bouterse returned to power as an elected president, and in 2012 parliament passed an amnesty law to halt the trial over the December murders, which had opened in 2007; the courts allowed the trial to continue. Re-elected in 2015, he lost the 2020 election as the economy slid into crisis. In 2019 a military court sentenced him to 20 years in prison for the December murders, a verdict upheld on appeal and confirmed by the Supreme Court on 20 December 2023. Bouterse failed to report to prison, went into hiding and died in December 2024.

Chan Santokhi of the VHP, president from 2020, governed during the COVID-19 pandemic and a severe debt crisis, with protests in 2023 that included the storming of parliament. In the Netherlands, Prime Minister Mark Rutte formally apologised in December 2022 for the Dutch state's role in slavery, and King Willem-Alexander apologised personally at the Keti Koti commemoration in Amsterdam on 1 July 2023, the 150th anniversary of the end of state supervision. Offshore oil discoveries promise a major change to the economy: in October 2024 TotalEnergies approved a $10.5 billion project to develop fields about 150 kilometres off the coast, with first oil targeted for 2028. In the election of May 2025 the NDP won the most seats, and on 6 July 2025 parliament elected its leader, Jennifer Geerlings-Simons, as Suriname's first woman president, at the head of a coalition that brought together parties representing Creole, Maroon, Javanese and other communities.
