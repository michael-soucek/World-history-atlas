Akbar, the third Mughal emperor, ruled from 1556 to 1605 and turned a fragile kingdom into an empire covering most of northern and central India. He is remembered for his efficient government, his alliances with Hindu rulers, and his remarkable openness to other religions.

## A boy king

Akbar was born in 1542 at Umarkot, in Sindh, while his father, the emperor Humayun, was in exile after losing his throne to the Afghan ruler Sher Shah Suri. Humayun regained Delhi in 1555 but died a few months later after falling down the stairs of his library, and Akbar became emperor at thirteen. That same year his regent, Bairam Khan, defeated the Hindu general Hemu at the second Battle of Panipat, securing the dynasty. In 1560 Akbar dismissed Bairam Khan and, after a period dominated by his foster mother's family, took power himself.

## Building an empire

Akbar conquered Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal, Kashmir, Sindh and much of the Deccan. He brought most of the Rajput kingdoms of Rajasthan into alliance, marrying Rajput princesses, such as the mother of his heir, and appointing Rajput nobles, such as Man Singh of Amber, to high positions. Those who resisted were crushed or fought him for years: he took the great fortress of Chittor in 1568, after a long siege, killing thousands, and Rana Pratap of Mewar fought him, notably at Haldighati in 1576.

## Government

Akbar built a system of ranks, the mansabdari, in which every official held a numerical rank that set his pay and the number of cavalry he had to maintain, paid through assignments of land revenue that were regularly rotated so that no noble could build a local power base. His finance minister, Todar Mal, reformed land taxation, based on careful surveys of land and average yields over ten years. The empire was divided into provinces, subahs, with a standard administration. Persian was the language of government.

## Religion

Akbar abolished the pilgrim tax on Hindus in 1563 and the jizya, the tax on non-Muslims, in 1564. At Fatehpur Sikri, the red sandstone city he built near Agra in the 1570s, he held debates in his House of Worship, the Ibadat Khana, at first among Muslim scholars, then among Sunni and Shia Muslims, Hindus, Jains, Zoroastrians and Jesuit priests from Goa. In 1579 he claimed the authority to decide religious disputes. He developed a personal religious doctrine, later called Din-i Ilahi, "divine faith", followed by a small circle of courtiers, and a policy of tolerance known as sulh-i kul, "universal peace". Orthodox Muslim scholars opposed him, and some rebelled.

## Culture

Although he probably never learned to read, perhaps because of dyslexia, Akbar had books read to him daily, gathered a large library and sponsored translations of Sanskrit works, such as the Mahabharata and Ramayana, into Persian. Mughal painting flourished in his workshops, blending Persian, Indian and European styles in illustrated manuscripts such as the Hamzanama. His court historian, Abu'l-Fazl, wrote the Akbarnama, a history of his reign, and the Ain-i-Akbari, a detailed account of his government. His court included the "nine jewels", among them the musician Tansen and the witty minister Birbal, hero of many later stories.

## Later years

Akbar's later years were troubled by his son Salim, who rebelled in 1600 and had Abu'l-Fazl murdered in 1602. They were reconciled before Akbar died in October 1605. He was buried at Sikandra, near Agra, in a tomb completed by Salim, who reigned as Jahangir. Akbar is widely regarded as the greatest Mughal ruler.
