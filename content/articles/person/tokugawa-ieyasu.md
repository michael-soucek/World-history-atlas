Tokugawa Ieyasu was the founder of the Tokugawa shogunate, which ruled Japan from 1603 to 1868. The last of the three great unifiers of Japan, after Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, he won the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and established a government that gave Japan more than 250 years of peace.

## Hostage

Ieyasu was born Matsudaira Takechiyo on 31 January 1543, in Okazaki Castle, in Mikawa province, the son of a minor warlord, a daimyo, during the Sengoku period, the "Warring States" era of civil war. As a child, he was sent as a hostage to the powerful Imagawa clan, and spent years in their capital, Sunpu, where he received a good education.

## Ally of Nobunaga

In 1560 Imagawa Yoshimoto was killed by Oda Nobunaga at the Battle of Okehazama, and Ieyasu returned to Okazaki, became independent and allied with Nobunaga. He took the name Tokugawa in 1567. He fought alongside Nobunaga in campaigns, and in 1572 suffered a heavy defeat by Takeda Shingen at Mikatagahara. In 1575, at Nagashino, Nobunaga and Ieyasu defeated the Takeda cavalry with massed firearms. In 1579, under pressure from Nobunaga, Ieyasu ordered his eldest son and wife executed on suspicion of conspiracy.

## Under Hideyoshi

After Nobunaga was killed in 1582, Toyotomi Hideyoshi took power. Ieyasu fought Hideyoshi to a draw in 1584, then submitted. In 1590, after Hideyoshi conquered the Hōjō, he gave Ieyasu the Kantō region, around the small castle town of Edo, in exchange for his home provinces. Ieyasu developed Edo, today's Tokyo, into his capital. He avoided taking part in Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea. When Hideyoshi died in 1598, Ieyasu was one of five regents for his young son, Hideyori.

## Sekigahara

Ieyasu soon became the most powerful regent, and his opponents, led by Ishida Mitsunari, formed a coalition. On 21 October 1600, at Sekigahara, in a valley in central Japan, Ieyasu's Eastern Army defeated the Western Army, helped by defections in the enemy ranks during the battle. Ieyasu redistributed lands, rewarding his allies and punishing his enemies.

## Shogun

In 1603 the emperor appointed Ieyasu shogun. In 1605 he passed the title to his son Hidetada, to establish the succession, but kept real power as retired shogun, ōgosho, from Sunpu. He organised the daimyo, issued regulations for them and for the imperial court, and controlled trade. He employed the English navigator William Adams, the first Englishman in Japan, whose story inspired the novel Shōgun, as an adviser on ships and foreign affairs, and initially tolerated Christianity, before banning it in 1614.

## Osaka

Hideyori, son of Hideyoshi, remained in Osaka Castle, a potential rival. In the sieges of Osaka, in 1614 and 1615, Ieyasu destroyed the Toyotomi; Hideyori and his mother committed suicide.

## Death

Ieyasu died on 1 June 1616, in Sunpu. He was deified as Tōshō Daigongen, and his shrine at Nikkō, the Tōshō-gū, richly decorated, is a World Heritage Site.
