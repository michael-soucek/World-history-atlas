Ashoka was the third emperor of the Maurya dynasty, ruling most of the Indian subcontinent from about 268 to 232 BCE. After a bloody war of conquest, he turned to Buddhism and governed according to dhamma, a moral code he proclaimed in inscriptions carved on rocks and pillars across his empire.

## The Maurya Empire

Ashoka was the grandson of Chandragupta Maurya, who founded the empire around 321 BCE, and the son of Bindusara, who expanded it into southern India. Born around 304 BCE, he served as a governor, at Ujjain and possibly Taxila, under his father. According to Buddhist tradition, after Bindusara's death he fought his brothers for the throne, and his coronation took place around 268 BCE, about four years after his accession. Later legends portray the young Ashoka as cruel, but these stories, told by Buddhists to emphasise his conversion, may be exaggerated.

## The Kalinga war

Around 261 BCE, Ashoka conquered Kalinga, a kingdom on the east coast, in today's Odisha. In his Thirteenth Rock Edict, he records that 150,000 people were deported, 100,000 were killed, and many more died, and expresses his deep remorse. He declared that conquest by dhamma, rather than by force, was the true conquest.

## Dhamma

Ashoka's dhamma, the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit dharma, was a code of ethical conduct based on Buddhist values but addressed to all his subjects, whatever their religion. It stressed non-violence, respect for parents, elders and teachers, generosity to Brahmins and ascetics, kindness to slaves and servants, truthfulness and tolerance of all religious sects. He restricted the killing of animals, reduced meat in the royal kitchens and banned certain sacrifices. He appointed officials, dhamma-mahamattas, to promote dhamma and look after the welfare of the people, and made tours of his empire to teach.

## Public works

Ashoka's edicts record that he had wells dug, trees planted along roads for shade, rest houses built and medical care provided for people and animals, with medicinal herbs planted, both in his empire and in neighbouring lands.

## The edicts

Ashoka's edicts are the earliest substantial written records in Indian history. They were carved on rocks and on polished sandstone pillars, many crowned with animal capitals, at over thirty sites, from Afghanistan to Karnataka. Most are written in Prakrit, in the Brahmi script, but in the north-west some use Kharosthi, and near Kandahar, Greek and Aramaic. Brahmi was deciphered by James Prinsep in 1837, allowing them to be read again. The Lion Capital from the pillar at Sarnath, with four lions, is now the national emblem of India, and the wheel, chakra, on its base appears on India's flag.

## Spreading Buddhism

According to Buddhist tradition, Ashoka built 84,000 stupas, including at Sanchi, and supported the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra. He sent envoys to Greek kings in the west, whom he names in his edicts, including Antiochus II of Syria and Ptolemy II of Egypt. According to Sri Lankan chronicles, his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta took Buddhism to Sri Lanka, bringing a cutting of the Bodhi tree.

## Later years and legacy

Ashoka died around 232 BCE. The Maurya Empire declined within fifty years of his death. He was largely forgotten in India, though remembered in Buddhist texts, until his edicts were deciphered in the nineteenth century. Today he is admired as one of the great rulers of history, a model of a ruler who renounced war, though historians note that he did not disband his army or give up Kalinga.
