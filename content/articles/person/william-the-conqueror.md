William the Conqueror was the first Norman king of England, ruling from 1066 to 1087, and Duke of Normandy from 1035. His victory at the Battle of Hastings and the Norman conquest of England transformed English society, language, law and architecture, and tied England closely to continental Europe.

## William the Bastard

William was born around 1028 in Falaise, Normandy, the illegitimate son of Robert I, Duke of Normandy, and Herleva, the daughter of a tanner. Before his contemporaries called him "the Conqueror", he was known as "the Bastard". His father died on pilgrimage in 1035, and William became duke at about seven or eight. His childhood was violent, as Norman nobles fought for power; several of his guardians were murdered. With the help of King Henry I of France, he defeated rebel barons at Val-ès-Dunes in 1047 and gradually took control of Normandy. Around 1051 he married Matilda of Flanders.

## The English succession

Edward the Confessor, king of England, was childless. He had spent years in exile in Normandy and, according to Norman sources, promised the throne to William, his cousin, around 1051. They also claimed that Harold Godwinson, the most powerful English earl, who visited Normandy around 1064, swore an oath to support William's claim, a scene shown on the Bayeux Tapestry. When Edward died in January 1066, the English council chose Harold as king.

## Hastings

William gathered an army and fleet, with the pope's blessing. Meanwhile, the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada invaded northern England, and Harold defeated and killed him at Stamford Bridge on 25 September 1066. Three days later, William landed at Pevensey, on the south coast. Harold marched south. On 14 October 1066, near Hastings, the two armies fought all day. The English shield wall held against Norman attacks, until feigned retreats drew English troops out. Harold was killed, according to tradition by an arrow in the eye. William was crowned king in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066.

## Conquest

The conquest was not complete. William faced rebellions in the following years. In the winter of 1069–1070, he crushed a rebellion in the north with the "Harrying of the North", in which villages, crops and livestock were destroyed, causing a famine in which many thousands died. The English aristocracy was almost entirely replaced by Norman and French nobles, who received lands in return for military service. The Normans built castles across England, such as the Tower of London, and rebuilt cathedrals and churches in the Romanesque style. Normans also took over the leadership of the church.

## The Domesday Book

In 1085 William ordered a survey of England, recording land, landholders, resources and value, for taxation and to know his kingdom. Completed in 1086, the Domesday Book is a unique record of medieval England.

## Death

William spent much of his later reign in Normandy, fighting the French king and his own rebellious eldest son, Robert. In 1087, while attacking the town of Mantes, he was injured, perhaps when his horse stumbled, and died on 9 September 1087 in Rouen. At his funeral in Caen, his body, swollen, burst when forced into the tomb. Normandy went to Robert and England to his son William Rufus.

## Legacy

The Norman conquest brought thousands of French words into English and introduced a feudal order.
