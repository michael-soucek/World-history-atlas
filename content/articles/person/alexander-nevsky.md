Alexander Yaroslavich, known as Alexander Nevsky, was a thirteenth-century Rus' prince who ruled Novgorod at intervals from 1236 and became grand prince of Vladimir in 1252. Celebrated for victories over Swedish and German crusaders in 1240 and 1242, he also bowed to the Mongol overlords of Rus' and enforced their tribute. Later church writers, tsars and Soviet propagandists made him a national hero, and historians still debate how far his reputation matches the record.

## A prince of Vladimir-Suzdal

Alexander was born in Pereslavl-Zalessky on 13 May 1221, the second son of Yaroslav Vsevolodovich and a grandson of Vsevolod the Big Nest, grand prince of Vladimir; his mother's identity is uncertain. His father often held the princely seat of Novgorod, a city that chose its princes through its assembly, the veche, and Alexander was left there as a boy to represent him. In 1236 he became prince of the [Novgorod Republic](/place/novgorod-republic) in his own right.

The [Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus'](/event/mongol-invasion-of-kievan-rus) of 1237 and 1238 devastated the north-east and killed Alexander's uncle, Grand Prince Yuri II. His father succeeded to Vladimir with the khan's permission, and left Novgorod in Alexander's hands.

## The Neva

In 1240 a Swedish expedition led by Birger Jarl, part of a crusade into the eastern Baltic, landed where the Izhora joins the Neva. On 15 July Alexander attacked with a small force and drove them off. The battle is known only from two Russian accounts and is absent from Swedish sources; the historian John Fennell saw it as one more clash in a long Russo-Swedish contest over Finland and Karelia rather than a coordinated assault on Rus'. The surname "Nevsky", "of the Neva", was only attached to him in the fifteenth century.

## The Battle on the Ice

Later in 1240 the Novgorodians, wary of his power, sent him away, while forces of the bishopric of Dorpat and the Livonian Order seized Pskov. Recalled in 1241, Alexander retook Koporye and Pskov and pushed into Estonian territory. On 5 April 1242, on the frozen Lake Peipus, he defeated the Livonian knights, checking the eastward advance of the [Teutonic Order](/place/state-of-the-teutonic-order), and the Germans soon agreed to give up occupied Russian land and exchange prisoners. Soviet historians presented the victory as having saved Russia from German enslavement; Fennell and others doubt it was a turning point, noting that one chronicle gives most of the credit to his brother Andrey.

## Serving the khans

Rus' princes now had to travel to the khans of the [Golden Horde](/place/golden-horde) for patents to rule. After his father died in 1246, Alexander and his brother Andrey journeyed to the Mongols; in 1248 Andrey became grand prince of Vladimir and Alexander was given Kiev. When Andrey moved against the Mongols, a punitive force was sent and he fled into exile, and in 1252 Alexander took the grand princely title. In 1259 he led troops to Novgorod and forced the reluctant city to submit to a Mongol census and tribute. Fennell judged that the "Tatar yoke" truly began with this betrayal of his brothers; others, such as Orlando Figes, explain his collaboration by a deeper distrust of the Catholic West.

## Death and sainthood

Returning from a visit to the khan Berke at Sarai, Alexander fell ill and died at Gorodets on the Volga on 14 November 1263, reportedly after taking monastic vows on his deathbed. He was buried in Vladimir. The Russian Orthodox Church canonised him in 1547, and in 1724 Peter the Great moved his relics to his new capital, Saint Petersburg. The 1938 film Alexander Nevsky fixed his image as the saviour of [Russia](/country/russia), and his cult remains politically charged: in 2024 the Orthodox Church of Ukraine removed his feast from its calendar.
