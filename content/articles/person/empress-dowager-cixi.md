Empress Dowager Cixi effectively controlled the Qing government of China for nearly fifty years, from 1861 until her death in 1908. A concubine who rose to supreme power, she ruled as regent for her son and nephew during a period of rebellion, foreign invasion and attempts at reform. Long portrayed as a reactionary villain, she has been reassessed by some historians.

## Concubine

Cixi was born on 29 November 1835 into a Manchu family of the Yehe Nara clan. In 1851 she was selected as a concubine of the Xianfeng emperor. In 1856 she gave birth to his only surviving son, Zaichun, raising her status. During the Second Opium War, in 1860, British and French forces took Beijing and burned the Summer Palace, and the court fled to Rehe. The emperor died there in 1861.

## The coup

Xianfeng's five-year-old son became the Tongzhi emperor, with a council of eight regents. Cixi, his mother, and Ci'an, the late emperor's empress, allied with Prince Gong, the emperor's brother, and in November 1861 overthrew the regents, executing or forcing some to commit suicide. The two empresses dowager ruled as co-regents, "behind the curtain", as women could not be seen at court audiences.

## The Tongzhi Restoration

During the 1860s, Qing forces, led by officials such as Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang, defeated the Taiping Rebellion and other revolts. Under the Self-Strengthening Movement, the government built arsenals, shipyards and a foreign office, the Zongli Yamen, and sent students abroad, while keeping Confucian institutions.

## Guangxu

Tongzhi died in 1875, aged eighteen. Cixi had her three-year-old nephew, Zaitian, made emperor as Guangxu, against the rules of succession, keeping her as regent. Ci'an died in 1881. Guangxu took power formally in 1889, and Cixi retired to the Summer Palace, which she rebuilt, using, according to a disputed account, funds intended for the navy, but she continued to influence government. China's defeat by Japan in 1894–1895 humiliated the court.

## The Hundred Days' Reform

In 1898 Guangxu, influenced by the reformers Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, issued decrees for sweeping reforms of education, the army and government. In September, fearing a plot against her, Cixi staged a coup, placed Guangxu under house arrest on an island in the palace lake and executed six reformers. Kang and Liang fled.

## The Boxers

In 1900 the Boxers, a movement attacking foreigners and Christians, spread across northern China. Cixi supported them and declared war on the foreign powers. The Boxers besieged the foreign legations in Beijing. An international force of eight nations relieved the legations in August 1900, and Cixi fled to Xi'an. The Boxer Protocol of 1901 imposed a huge indemnity.

## Late reforms

After her return in 1902, Cixi supported reforms she had opposed: the civil-service examinations were abolished in 1905, a modern army was built and a constitution was promised. She met foreign diplomats' wives and was photographed. Guangxu died on 14 November 1908, poisoned with arsenic according to a 2008 forensic study, and Cixi died the next day, after naming the two-year-old Puyi as emperor. The dynasty fell three years later.
