Nzinga Mbande was queen of the Mbundu kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba, in what is now Angola, from the 1620s until her death in 1663. A skilled diplomat and military leader, she resisted Portuguese colonial expansion and the slave trade's encroachment for decades, and is honoured in Angola as a national heroine.

## Ndongo and the Portuguese

Nzinga was born around 1583, the daughter of Mbande a Ngola, king of Ndongo, whose title, ngola, gave Angola its name. The Portuguese had founded the colony of Luanda on the coast in 1575, and their wars against Ndongo, fought with the help of Imbangala mercenaries, sought to capture people for the Atlantic slave trade, which sent huge numbers of Mbundu people to Brazil. Nzinga was trained in military and political affairs.

## The embassy to Luanda

In 1617 her brother, Ngola Mbande, became king. In 1622 he sent Nzinga to Luanda to negotiate peace with the Portuguese governor, João Correia de Sousa. According to a famous story, when she found that the governor had a chair while only a mat was provided for her, a servant knelt, and she sat on the servant's back, to negotiate as an equal. She agreed a treaty and was baptised as a Christian, taking the name Ana de Sousa, with the governor as godfather. The Portuguese did not honour the treaty.

## Queen

When her brother died in 1624, possibly poisoned, Nzinga took power, first as regent for his young son, who soon died, perhaps on her orders, then as ruler. Her right to rule as a woman was challenged, and the Portuguese supported a rival, Hari a Kiluanje, then Ngola Hari, as king of Ndongo. Nzinga sometimes adopted male titles and dressed as a man, and kept male concubines who dressed as women.

## Matamba

Driven from Ndongo by the Portuguese, Nzinga allied with Imbangala bands, adopting some of their customs, and around 1631 conquered the kingdom of Matamba, to the east, which became her base. She welcomed escaped slaves and soldiers who deserted from the Portuguese, and controlled trade routes to the interior, including the slave trade, selling captives to the Dutch.

## The Dutch alliance

In 1641 the Dutch West India Company captured Luanda. Nzinga allied with the Dutch against the Portuguese, and her forces won several victories, including at Kombi in 1647. But in 1648 a Portuguese fleet from Brazil retook Luanda, and the Dutch left Angola. Nzinga's sister Kambu was captured, and her sister Funji was killed by the Portuguese, reportedly for sending secret information to Nzinga.

## Peace

In her final years, Nzinga negotiated peace with Portugal, signed in 1656, recovering her sister Kambu. She returned to Christianity, welcomed Capuchin missionaries and tried to establish Matamba as a Christian kingdom with Portuguese recognition. She died on 17 December 1663, aged about eighty. Her sister Kambu, known as Barbara, succeeded her.

## Legacy

Nzinga's reputation has varied. European writers depicted her as a cruel and exotic figure, while in the twentieth century she became a symbol of resistance to colonialism. In independent Angola, she is a national heroine; a statue stands in Luanda, and a street and a film bear her name.
