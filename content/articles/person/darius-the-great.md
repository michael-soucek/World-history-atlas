Darius I, known as Darius the Great, was king of the Achaemenid Persian Empire from 522 to 486 BCE. He seized the throne in a disputed succession, crushed widespread revolts and then reorganised the empire, which reached its greatest extent under him, from the Indus to the Balkans and Egypt.

## Seizing the throne

Darius was born around 550 BCE, the son of Hystaspes, a Persian noble and satrap, and belonged to a branch of the Achaemenid family. He served as a spear-bearer to Cambyses II, son of Cyrus the Great, during the conquest of Egypt. In 522 BCE, while Cambyses was in Egypt, a man claiming to be his brother Bardiya seized the throne. Cambyses died on the way home. According to Darius's own account, the man was an impostor, a Magian priest named Gaumata, and Cambyses had secretly killed the real Bardiya. With six other nobles, Darius killed the usurper in September 522 BCE and became king. Many historians suspect that the "impostor" was the real Bardiya and that Darius's story justified a coup.

## Behistun

Revolts broke out across the empire, in Persia, Media, Babylon, Elam and elsewhere. Darius crushed them in about a year, fighting nineteen battles. He recorded his victories in a huge inscription carved on a cliff at Behistun, in western Iran, in three languages, Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian, with a relief showing him triumphing over the rebel kings. Copied by Henry Rawlinson in the 1830s and 1840s, the inscription was the key to deciphering cuneiform.

## Organising the empire

Darius organised the empire into about twenty satrapies, each paying a fixed annual tribute in silver or goods. He introduced a gold coin, the daric, and standardised weights and measures. He built the Royal Road, about 2,700 kilometres long, from Sardis to Susa, with stations where messengers could change horses, so that royal messages could cross the empire in about a week. Herodotus wrote that nothing mortal travels faster than these messengers. Aramaic was used as a common administrative language. Darius completed a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea.

## Persepolis

Darius moved the capital to Susa, where he built a palace, and began the great ceremonial city of Persepolis, in Fars, with its vast Apadana audience hall. Reliefs on its staircases show delegations from the subject peoples, Medes, Babylonians, Ethiopians, Indians and others, bringing gifts to the king. Thousands of clay tablets found there, the Persepolis Fortification Tablets, record payments of food rations to workers, including women, showing a sophisticated administration.

## Campaigns

Darius conquered parts of the Indus valley around 515 BCE, and campaigned against the Scythians across the Danube around 513 BCE, without success, though he took Thrace. The Ionian Greek cities of Asia Minor revolted in 499 BCE, with help from Athens and Eretria; Darius crushed the revolt by 494 BCE, sacking Miletus.

## Marathon

To punish Athens and Eretria, Darius sent an expedition across the Aegean in 490 BCE. It destroyed Eretria but was defeated by the Athenians at Marathon. Darius planned another invasion, but a revolt in Egypt intervened, and he died in 486 BCE. He was buried in a rock-cut tomb at Naqsh-e Rostam, near Persepolis. His son Xerxes succeeded him.
