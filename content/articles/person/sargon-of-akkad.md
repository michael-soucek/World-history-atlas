Sargon of Akkad founded the Akkadian Empire, often described as the world's first empire, ruling from about 2334 to 2279 BCE. From his new capital of Akkad, he conquered the Sumerian city-states of southern Mesopotamia and extended his power to Syria and Iran, and his legend inspired Mesopotamian rulers for two thousand years.

## Legendary origins

Sargon's name in Akkadian, Sharru-ukin, means "the true king" or "the king is legitimate", probably a name he adopted. A later text, the Legend of Sargon, written centuries after his death, tells that his mother was a priestess who gave birth to him in secret, placed him in a basket of reeds sealed with bitumen and set him in the Euphrates river. He was found and raised by Akki, a gardener, and the goddess Ishtar loved him. The story resembles that of Moses. According to the Sumerian King List, Sargon was a cupbearer to Ur-Zababa, king of Kish, before seizing power.

## Conquest of Sumer

Southern Mesopotamia was divided among Sumerian city-states, such as Ur, Uruk, Lagash and Umma. The most powerful ruler was Lugalzagesi of Uruk, who had united much of Sumer. Sargon defeated him, according to inscriptions, in battle, and brought him in a neck-stock to the gate of the temple of Enlil at Nippur. He then conquered Ur, Lagash and Umma, and "washed his weapons in the sea", the Persian Gulf, a sign of total victory. His inscriptions claim thirty-four battles.

## Akkad

Sargon built a new capital, Akkad, or Agade, whose location has never been found; it was probably near the Tigris or Euphrates in central Iraq. His people, the Akkadians, spoke a Semitic language, the ancestor of Babylonian and Assyrian, which became the language of government, written in the cuneiform script borrowed from the Sumerians. Sumerian continued to be used for religion and literature.

## An empire

Sargon campaigned beyond Mesopotamia, west to Mari and Ebla, in Syria, the "Cedar Forest" and the "Silver Mountains", the Amanus and Taurus mountains, and east into Elam, in south-western Iran. He boasted that 5,400 men ate daily before him. He placed Akkadian governors over conquered cities and controlled trade, claiming that ships from Dilmun, Magan and Meluhha, probably Bahrain, Oman and the Indus valley, docked at Akkad.

## Enheduanna

Sargon appointed his daughter Enheduanna as high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur, an important political and religious post. She wrote hymns to the goddess Inanna, and she is the earliest author in history known by name.

## Revolts and death

Later traditions say that in his old age, all the lands revolted against Sargon and besieged him in Akkad, but he defeated them. He died around 2279 BCE. His sons Rimush and Manishtushu faced revolts.

## The dynasty

Sargon's grandson Naram-Sin expanded the empire and took the title "king of the four quarters" and declared himself a god. His victory stele, now in the Louvre, shows him climbing a mountain. The empire collapsed around 2150 BCE, attacked by the Gutians, a people from the Zagros mountains, and possibly affected by drought. A later poem, The Curse of Akkad, blamed its fall on Naram-Sin's offence against the god Enlil.

## Legacy

Later Mesopotamian kings remembered Sargon as the model of a great king. Two Assyrian kings took his name, including Sargon II in the eighth century BCE.

## On our maps

Sargon's empire falls between our 3000 and 2000 BCE maps. On the 3000 BCE map, Mesopotamia is divided among the Sumerian cities, and [Elam](/place/elam) lies to the east.
