Simón Bolívar was a Venezuelan military and political leader who led the struggle for independence from Spain in much of South America. Known as El Libertador, "the Liberator", he helped free the territories that became Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia and Panama, and dreamed of uniting Spanish America.

## Early life

Bolívar was born on 24 July 1783 in Caracas, into a wealthy creole family, of Spanish descent born in the Americas, who owned plantations and mines. His parents died when he was young. His tutor, Simón Rodríguez, introduced him to the ideas of Rousseau and the Enlightenment. In 1799 he went to Spain, where he married María Teresa del Toro; she died of yellow fever in 1803, months after they returned to Venezuela. He never remarried. In 1805, in Rome, on the Monte Sacro, he vowed to free his country from Spain.

## The First and Second Republics

After Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808, a junta in Caracas took power in 1810, and Venezuela declared independence in 1811. Bolívar served under Francisco de Miranda. In 1812 an earthquake destroyed Caracas, and royalist forces reconquered Venezuela. Bolívar escaped to New Granada. In 1813, in his "Admirable Campaign", he retook Caracas, where he was proclaimed Liberator. He declared "war to the death" against Spaniards who did not support independence. But in 1814 royalist cavalry, the llaneros under José Tomás Boves, crushed the Second Republic, and Bolívar fled to Jamaica, where in 1815 he wrote the Jamaica Letter, setting out his vision for Latin America.

## Victory

With help from Haiti, whose president Alexandre Pétion asked him to free enslaved people, Bolívar returned to Venezuela in 1816. He allied with the llaneros under José Antonio Páez. In 1819 he made a daring crossing of the Andes into New Granada and defeated the royalists at Boyacá on 7 August 1819. He was proclaimed president of the Republic of Colombia, now called Gran Colombia, uniting Venezuela, New Granada and later Ecuador. In 1821 he won at Carabobo, securing Venezuela. His general Antonio José de Sucre won at Pichincha in 1822, freeing Quito.

## Peru and Bolivia

In 1822 Bolívar met José de San Martín, the liberator of Argentina and Chile, at Guayaquil; San Martín withdrew, leaving Bolívar to complete the liberation of Peru. Bolívar won at Junín in 1824, and Sucre at Ayacucho in December 1824. Upper Peru became a new republic in 1825, named Bolivia after him. He wrote its constitution, which provided for a president for life.

## Disillusion

Bolívar's dream of uniting Spanish America failed. The Congress of Panama of 1826 achieved little. Gran Colombia was torn by regional rivalries. Bolívar took dictatorial powers in 1828 and survived an assassination attempt that year, saved by his companion Manuela Sáenz. Venezuela and Ecuador broke away in 1830. Bolívar resigned and, ill with tuberculosis, set out for exile, but died at Santa Marta, Colombia, on 17 December 1830, saying that those who serve a revolution plough the sea.

## Legacy

Bolívar is revered across Latin America; Venezuela's currency and official name honour him.
