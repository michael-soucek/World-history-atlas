Isaac Newton was an English mathematician, physicist and astronomer, one of the most influential scientists in history. His Principia, published in 1687, set out the laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, which explained the movements of planets and objects on earth with the same principles. He also made fundamental discoveries in optics and invented calculus.

## Early life

Newton was born on 25 December 1642, by the calendar then used in England, at Woolsthorpe, in Lincolnshire, the posthumous son of a farmer. His mother remarried and left him with his grandmother. He was a solitary child who built mechanical models. In 1661 he went to Trinity College, Cambridge, where he studied the new philosophy of Descartes, Galileo and Kepler.

## The plague years

When plague closed Cambridge in 1665–1666, Newton returned to Woolsthorpe. In these two years he developed the foundations of calculus, which he called the method of fluxions, did experiments with prisms on light, and began thinking about gravity. Later he told a story of seeing an apple fall in the garden, which led him to wonder if the same force reached the moon.

## Optics

Back at Cambridge, Newton became Lucasian professor of mathematics in 1669. Using prisms, he showed that white light is a mixture of all the colours of the spectrum, which are bent by different amounts. To avoid the colour distortions of lens telescopes, he built the first practical reflecting telescope, using a mirror, in 1668, which led to his election to the Royal Society. His criticisms and disputes, especially with Robert Hooke, made him reluctant to publish. His Opticks appeared in 1704.

## The Principia

In 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton and asked what path a planet would follow under a force decreasing with the square of distance. Newton replied that it would be an ellipse, and he had calculated it. Halley encouraged him to write it up, and paid for the publication. The Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, published in 1687, set out three laws of motion: an object stays at rest or in uniform motion unless acted on by a force; force equals mass times acceleration; and every action has an equal and opposite reaction. It stated the law of universal gravitation: every mass attracts every other with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. With these, Newton explained Kepler's laws of planetary motion, the tides, the paths of comets and the precession of the equinoxes.

## The calculus dispute

The German philosopher Gottfried Leibniz developed calculus independently and published first, in 1684. A bitter dispute followed over who had invented it first; a Royal Society committee, secretly guided by Newton, its president, ruled for him in 1712. Historians now credit both.

## Public life

Newton was elected to Parliament for Cambridge University. In 1696 he became warden and in 1699 master of the Royal Mint in London, where he reformed the coinage and prosecuted counterfeiters. He was president of the Royal Society from 1703 and was knighted in 1705. He also devoted much time to alchemy and biblical study, holding unorthodox religious views, rejecting the Trinity, which he kept private.

## Death

Newton died on 20 March 1727 by the calendar then used in England (31 March by the modern calendar) and was buried in Westminster Abbey. The poet Alexander Pope wrote that God said "Let Newton be!" and all was light.
