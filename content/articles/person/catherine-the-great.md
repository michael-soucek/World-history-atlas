Catherine II, known as Catherine the Great, was empress of Russia from 1762 to 1796, the country's longest-ruling female leader. A German princess who seized the throne from her husband, she expanded Russia to the Black Sea and deep into central Europe, and presented herself as an enlightened ruler, while serfdom reached its height.

## A German princess

She was born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst on 2 May 1729 in Stettin, Prussia, today's Szczecin in Poland, the daughter of a minor German prince. In 1744 she was chosen as a bride for Peter, the heir of Empress Elizabeth of Russia. She converted to Orthodoxy, took the name Catherine, learned Russian and married Peter in 1745. The marriage was unhappy. She read widely, including Voltaire and Montesquieu, and built connections at court and among the guards regiments.

## The coup

Peter became emperor as Peter III in January 1762. He was unpopular: he made peace with Prussia, giving back conquests from the Seven Years' War, and planned a war with Denmark, alienating the army and the church. In June 1762 Catherine, supported by the guards and her lover Grigory Orlov and his brothers, overthrew him. Peter was killed days later at Ropsha, probably by Alexei Orlov; Catherine's role is unclear.

## The enlightened empress

Catherine corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot, whose library she bought, letting him keep it and paying him as librarian. In 1767 she called a Legislative Commission, of representatives of nobles, townspeople, state peasants and others, to draft a new law code, giving it her Nakaz, "Instruction", drawing heavily on Montesquieu and Beccaria; the commission discussed but never produced a code. She founded the Smolny Institute, the first state school for girls, reorganised local government in 1775, granted a charter to the nobility in 1785 and promoted smallpox inoculation, having herself inoculated in 1768. She collected art on a huge scale, founding the Hermitage collection.

## Serfdom and Pugachev

Catherine's reign strengthened the power of nobles over their serfs, who could be sold, and she gave hundreds of thousands of state peasants to her favourites. In 1773–1775 Yemelyan Pugachev, a Don Cossack claiming to be Peter III, led a huge rebellion of Cossacks, peasants, Bashkirs and workers in the Volga and Urals, which was crushed; Pugachev was executed in Moscow.

## Expansion

Catherine's wars with the Ottoman Empire, in 1768–1774 and 1787–1792, gave Russia the northern Black Sea coast. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 gave Russia access to the Black Sea and a role as protector of Orthodox Christians in the Ottoman Empire. Russia annexed the Crimean Khanate in 1783. Her favourite and partner, Grigory Potemkin, governed the new southern lands, called New Russia, founding cities such as Kherson, Sevastopol, Mykolaiv and Yekaterinoslav. The story that he built fake "Potemkin villages" to impress her on her 1787 tour is largely a myth. Odessa was founded in 1794.

## The partitions of Poland

With Prussia and Austria, Catherine partitioned Poland-Lithuania three times, in 1772, 1793 and 1795, after which Poland disappeared from the map. Russia gained most of Lithuania, Belarus and western Ukraine. In 1794 Russian troops under Alexander Suvorov crushed the Kościuszko Uprising.

## Later years

The French Revolution turned Catherine against the radical ideas of the Enlightenment. She had the writer Alexander Radishchev, who criticised serfdom, exiled to Siberia. Catherine had many lovers, whom she rewarded generously, and her private life was the subject of malicious rumours, many invented after her death. She died on 17 November 1796 after a stroke and was succeeded by her son Paul, who resented her.
