Aristotle was a Greek philosopher and scientist of the fourth century BCE. A student of Plato and teacher of Alexander the Great, he wrote on almost every subject studied in his time, from logic, physics and biology to ethics, politics, poetry and rhetoric, and his ideas shaped the thought of Europe and the Islamic world for nearly two thousand years.

## Early life

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, a small town in Chalcidice, in northern Greece. His father, Nicomachus, was the court physician to King Amyntas III of Macedon, the grandfather of Alexander. Orphaned young, he was raised by a guardian.

## At the Academy

At about seventeen, Aristotle went to Athens and joined Plato's Academy, where he stayed for about twenty years, as student and teacher, until Plato's death in 347 BCE. He admired Plato but came to disagree with his theory of Forms, the idea that true reality consists of perfect, unchanging ideas, separate from the physical world. For Aristotle, the forms of things exist in the things themselves, and knowledge begins with observation of the world.

## Travels and Alexander

After Plato's death, Aristotle left Athens. He lived at Assos, in Asia Minor, at the court of the ruler Hermias, whose niece Pythias he married, and on the island of Lesbos, where he studied marine life in the lagoon of Pyrrha. In 343 BCE Philip II of Macedon invited him to tutor his son Alexander, then thirteen. He taught him for several years, though how much he influenced the future conqueror is unclear.

## The Lyceum

In 335 BCE Aristotle returned to Athens and founded his own school at the Lyceum, a gymnasium sacred to Apollo Lykeios. His followers were called Peripatetics, perhaps because he taught while walking in the covered walkways, peripatoi. The school collected books, maps and specimens, and organised research, such as a collection of 158 constitutions of Greek cities, of which only the Constitution of the Athenians survives.

## Logic and science

Aristotle created the first formal system of logic, the syllogism, a form of reasoning in which a conclusion follows from two premises: all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal. His logic remained the standard until the nineteenth century. He studied hundreds of animal species, dissecting many, and classified them, founding biology as a science; some of his observations, such as on the reproduction of dogfish, were confirmed only in modern times. His physics and astronomy, describing an earth at the centre of the universe surrounded by heavenly spheres, and his theory of four elements, earth, water, air and fire, were later overturned.

## Ethics and politics

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle argued that the goal of human life is eudaimonia, flourishing or happiness, achieved by living according to virtue and reason. Virtues are habits that lie in a mean between extremes; courage, for example, lies between cowardice and recklessness. In the Politics, he called man a "political animal", meant to live in a city, compared forms of government, and favoured a mixed constitution. He defended slavery as natural for some people and considered women inferior to men, views criticised today.

## Poetics and rhetoric

The Poetics analyses tragedy, introducing ideas such as catharsis, the purging of emotions, and the unity of plot. The Rhetoric studies the art of persuasion through character, emotion and argument.

## Death and influence

After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens led to a charge of impiety against Aristotle, who left, reportedly saying he would not let Athens sin twice against philosophy, a reference to Socrates. He died in Chalcis in 322 BCE. His works, mostly lecture notes, were preserved and translated into Arabic, where scholars such as Avicenna and Averroes commented on them. Through Latin translations, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, he became "the Philosopher" for medieval scholars such as Thomas Aquinas.
