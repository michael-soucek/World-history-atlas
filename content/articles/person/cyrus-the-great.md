Cyrus II, known as Cyrus the Great, founded the Achaemenid Persian Empire, the largest empire the world had seen. From around 559 to 530 BCE, he conquered the Medes, Lydia and Babylon, uniting much of western Asia, and was remembered by his subjects and later writers as a just and tolerant ruler.

## King of Anshan

Cyrus was born around 600 BCE, the son of Cambyses I, king of Anshan, a small Persian kingdom in today's Fars province, in south-western Iran, subject to the Medes. According to the Greek historian Herodotus, his mother was the daughter of the Median king Astyages, who, after a dream, ordered the infant killed, but a herdsman raised him; this story resembles other foundation legends.

## Conquering the Medes

Around 553 BCE Cyrus rebelled against Astyages. In 550 BCE Astyages's army mutinied and handed him over to Cyrus, who took the Median capital, Ecbatana. The Median Empire, which stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia, became the core of Cyrus's empire.

## Lydia and Croesus

Croesus, the wealthy king of Lydia in western Anatolia, attacked Cyrus around 547 BCE. According to Herodotus, the oracle of Delphi told Croesus that if he attacked Persia he would destroy a great empire; he did, his own. Cyrus defeated Croesus and took his capital, Sardis. His generals then conquered the Greek cities of Ionia, on the Aegean coast.

## Babylon

In 539 BCE Cyrus attacked the Neo-Babylonian Empire. After defeating the Babylonian army at Opis, his forces entered Babylon, apparently without a battle. The Babylonian king Nabonidus, who was unpopular with the priests of Marduk, was captured. Cyrus presented himself as a restorer of order, chosen by Marduk.

## The Cyrus Cylinder

The Cyrus Cylinder, a clay cylinder inscribed in Babylonian cuneiform, discovered in 1879 and now in the British Museum, records Cyrus's conquest of Babylon, his restoration of temples and his return of displaced peoples and their gods to their homes. It has sometimes been called the first charter of human rights, a description historians consider anachronistic; it follows the traditions of Mesopotamian royal inscriptions.

## The return of the Jews

According to the Hebrew Bible, in the books of Ezra and Isaiah, Cyrus allowed the Jews exiled in Babylon to return to Judah and rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem. Isaiah calls him God's anointed, messiah, the only non-Jew so named. His policy of allowing conquered peoples to keep their religions and customs helped him govern his vast empire.

## Ruling the empire

Cyrus divided the empire into provinces, satrapies, governed by satraps, and kept local officials and customs. He built a new capital at Pasargadae, with palaces and gardens, the earliest known Persian gardens, or paradises.

## Death

Cyrus died in 530 BCE, probably in battle on the north-eastern frontier, against the Massagetae, a nomadic people of Central Asia; Herodotus says their queen, Tomyris, dipped his head in a bag of blood. His tomb, a simple stone building on a stepped platform, still stands at Pasargadae. His son Cambyses II conquered Egypt.

## Legacy

Greek writers admired Cyrus; Xenophon's Cyropaedia presented him as an ideal ruler. He remains a national hero in Iran.
