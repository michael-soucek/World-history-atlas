Joan of Arc was a French peasant girl who, believing she was guided by the voices of saints, led French forces to lift the siege of Orléans in 1429, during the Hundred Years' War, and helped Charles VII to be crowned. Captured by the Burgundians and handed to the English, she was tried for heresy and burned at the stake in 1431, aged about nineteen. She was later declared innocent and made a saint.

## A divided France

Joan was born around 1412 in Domrémy, a village in north-eastern France, to a farming family. France was in a desperate situation. In the Hundred Years' War, England had won a great victory at Agincourt in 1415, and by the Treaty of Troyes in 1420, Charles VI of France had disinherited his son, the dauphin Charles, in favour of Henry V of England. England and its allies, the dukes of Burgundy, controlled northern France, including Paris and Reims, where French kings were traditionally crowned.

## The voices

From the age of about thirteen, Joan said she heard the voices of Saint Michael, Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret, who told her to help the dauphin drive out the English and have him crowned at Reims. In 1429, aged about seventeen, she persuaded a local commander, Robert de Baudricourt, to send her, dressed in men's clothes, to the dauphin's court at Chinon. After theologians examined her at Poitiers, Charles allowed her to join an army going to relieve Orléans, which the English had besieged since October 1428.

## Orléans

Joan arrived at Orléans at the end of April 1429, carrying a banner. She inspired the soldiers and townspeople, joined attacks, and was wounded by an arrow. On 8 May 1429, the English lifted the siege. The French then won a series of victories in the Loire valley, notably at Patay, in June.

## The coronation

Joan persuaded Charles to march through enemy territory to Reims, where he was crowned Charles VII on 17 July 1429, with Joan standing near him with her banner. The coronation strengthened his claim to the throne. In September, an attack on Paris failed, and Joan was wounded.

## Capture and trial

In May 1430, defending Compiègne, Joan was captured by Burgundian soldiers, who sold her to the English. Charles VII made no attempt to rescue her. In Rouen, from January to May 1431, she was tried by a church court led by Pierre Cauchon, bishop of Beauvais, a supporter of the English. She was questioned for months about her voices and her wearing of men's clothing. The records show her answering skilfully; asked whether she was in God's grace, a trap question, she replied that if she was not, she prayed God would put her there, and if she was, she prayed God would keep her there. She signed a recantation, under threat of execution, but resumed wearing men's clothes days later, possibly to protect herself from assault in prison. She was condemned as a relapsed heretic and burned in the Old Market square in Rouen on 30 May 1431.

## Rehabilitation and sainthood

The war turned in France's favour, and in 1453 the English were driven from all of France except Calais. In 1456 a retrial, ordered by the pope at the request of Joan's mother, declared her innocent. She became a symbol of France, invoked by both sides in political debates, and inspired many works of art, literature and film. She was canonised by the Catholic Church in 1920.
