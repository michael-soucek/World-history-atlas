Constantine I, called Constantine the Great, was Roman emperor from 306 to 337. He was the first emperor to convert to Christianity, ended the persecution of Christians, and founded Constantinople as a new capital, which became the centre of the Eastern Roman Empire for over a thousand years.

## The tetrarchy

Constantine was born around 272 in Naissus, today's Niš in Serbia, the son of Constantius Chlorus, an army officer, and Helena, a woman of humble origins. To manage the empire, the emperor Diocletian had created the tetrarchy, a system of two senior emperors, augusti, and two junior emperors, caesars. Constantius became caesar in the west in 293. Constantine served at the court of Diocletian in the east, effectively as a hostage, and fought on the Danube and in Persia.

## Rise to power

In 305 Diocletian and his colleague abdicated, and Constantius became augustus. Constantine joined his father in Britain, and when Constantius died at York in 306, his troops proclaimed Constantine emperor. Over the following years, the tetrarchy broke down into civil war among several rivals.

## The Milvian Bridge

In 312 Constantine invaded Italy against Maxentius, who controlled Rome. According to the Christian writers Lactantius and Eusebius, before the battle Constantine saw a sign in the sky, or in a dream, and was told "in this sign, conquer"; he ordered his soldiers to paint a Christian symbol, the chi-rho, on their shields. On 28 October 312, he defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome; Maxentius drowned in the Tiber. Constantine attributed his victory to the Christian God.

## Toleration

In 313 Constantine and the eastern emperor Licinius issued the Edict of Milan, granting freedom of religion to all, ending the persecution of Christians, which had been severe under Diocletian, and returning confiscated church property. Constantine gave churches money and privileges and built great churches, such as the original St Peter's Basilica and the Lateran Basilica in Rome and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, whose site his mother Helena helped identify. He made Sunday a day of rest. He remained Pontifex Maximus, the head of the traditional Roman religion, and did not ban pagan worship.

## The Council of Nicaea

Constantine sought unity in the church. In 325 he called the first ecumenical council at Nicaea, which condemned Arianism, the teaching of the priest Arius that the Son was created by and subordinate to the Father, and adopted the Nicene Creed. The dispute nevertheless continued for decades.

## Sole emperor

In 324 Constantine defeated Licinius and became sole ruler of the empire. He reformed the army, creating mobile field armies, and the currency, introducing the gold solidus, which remained stable for centuries.

## Constantinople

Constantine refounded the old Greek city of Byzantium, on the Bosporus, as his new capital, dedicated on 11 May 330 and named Constantinople. Its location, between Europe and Asia and near the eastern frontiers, was strategic. He built a palace, a hippodrome, a forum and a senate, and brought statues from across the empire.

## Family and death

Constantine's family life was violent: in 326 he had his eldest son, Crispus, and his wife, Fausta, executed for reasons that remain unclear. He was baptised on his deathbed, by the Arian bishop Eusebius of Nicomedia, and died on 22 May 337. His three sons divided the empire. The Orthodox Church venerates him as a saint.
