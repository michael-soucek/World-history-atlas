Kublai Khan was the fifth Great Khan of the Mongol Empire, from 1260 to 1294, and the founder of the Yuan dynasty, which ruled all of China. A grandson of Genghis Khan, he completed the Mongol conquest of China, built a new capital at what is now Beijing and presided over a cosmopolitan court described by Marco Polo.

## Grandson of Genghis

Kublai was born on 23 September 1215, the son of Tolui, Genghis Khan's youngest son, and Sorghaghtani Beki, a Kerait princess and Nestorian Christian, who was a skilled politician and promoted her sons' careers. She ensured that Kublai learned about Chinese culture. In 1251 his brother Möngke became Great Khan and gave Kublai responsibility for northern China. Kublai gathered Chinese advisers, Buddhist monks and Confucian scholars, and built a capital, Shangdu, the Xanadu of Coleridge's poem.

## Struggle for the throne

When Möngke died in 1259 while campaigning in China, Kublai and his younger brother Ariq Böke both claimed the title of Great Khan. Kublai was proclaimed in 1260. After four years of civil war, Ariq Böke surrendered. But the Mongol Empire was fragmenting into separate khanates: the Golden Horde, the Ilkhanate and the Chagatai Khanate, whose rulers did not fully recognise Kublai's authority. His cousin Kaidu fought him for decades in Central Asia.

## The Yuan dynasty

In 1271 Kublai proclaimed the Yuan dynasty, taking a Chinese dynastic name, and began building a new capital, Dadu, called Khanbaliq, on the site of today's Beijing. He conquered the Southern Song dynasty after a long war, taking the great city of Xiangyang in 1273, after a five-year siege, using counterweight trebuchets built by Persian engineers, and the capital Hangzhou in 1276. In 1279 the last Song loyalists were defeated at sea, and all of China was united under Mongol rule.

## Ruling China

Kublai adopted Chinese forms of government, but kept the Mongols in a privileged position, dividing the population into classes. He employed foreigners in high positions, such as the Muslim minister Ahmad Fanakati and, according to his own account, Marco Polo. He issued paper money, extended the Grand Canal, built roads and postal stations and promoted trade. He favoured Tibetan Buddhism, making the lama Phagpa his imperial preceptor, who created a script for writing Mongolian and Chinese, but tolerated all religions.

## Failed campaigns

Kublai's attempts to expand overseas failed. Two invasions of Japan, in 1274 and 1281, were defeated by Japanese resistance and by typhoons, which the Japanese called kamikaze, "divine wind". Campaigns in Vietnam, Champa, Burma and Java were costly and largely unsuccessful.

## Marco Polo

The Venetian Marco Polo claimed to have served Kublai for seventeen years. His book described the splendour of Kublai's court, his palaces, his hunting parties, the paper money and the postal system, and introduced Europe to China.

## Later years

Kublai was deeply affected by the deaths of his favourite wife, Chabi, in 1281 and his chosen heir, Zhenjin, in 1285. He grew obese, suffered from gout and drank heavily. He died on 18 February 1294 and was buried in Mongolia. His grandson Temür succeeded him.
