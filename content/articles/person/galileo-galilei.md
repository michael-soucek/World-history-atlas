Galileo Galilei was an Italian astronomer, physicist and mathematician who played a central role in the Scientific Revolution. He improved the telescope and made discoveries that supported the theory that the earth moves around the sun, and he studied motion through experiments and mathematics. His conflict with the Catholic Church became one of the most famous episodes in the history of science.

## Early life

Galileo was born in Pisa on 15 February 1564, the son of Vincenzo Galilei, a musician and music theorist. He studied medicine at the University of Pisa but turned to mathematics. He became professor of mathematics at Pisa in 1589 and at Padua, in the Republic of Venice, in 1592, where he taught for eighteen years, which he called the happiest of his life. He never married but had three children with Marina Gamba; his elder daughter, Virginia, became a nun, Sister Maria Celeste, and their letters survive.

## Motion

Galileo studied falling bodies and motion. Contrary to Aristotle's teaching that heavier objects fall faster, he showed, using balls rolled down inclined planes, that all objects fall with the same acceleration, if air resistance is ignored, and that distance fallen increases with the square of time. The story that he dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa may be a legend. He also studied pendulums, developed the principle of inertia and showed that projectiles follow a parabola.

## The telescope

In 1609, hearing of a Dutch invention, Galileo built his own telescopes, improving them to magnify about twenty times. He turned them to the sky and, in 1610, published his discoveries in The Starry Messenger: mountains and craters on the moon, countless stars invisible to the naked eye in the Milky Way, and four moons orbiting Jupiter, which he named the Medicean stars after the Medici family. He became court mathematician and philosopher to the Grand Duke of Tuscany in Florence. He later observed the phases of Venus, which showed that Venus orbits the sun, sunspots and the strange shape of Saturn.

## Copernicus

Galileo's discoveries supported the theory of Nicolaus Copernicus, published in 1543, that the earth and planets orbit the sun, contradicting the traditional view, based on Aristotle and Ptolemy, of an earth-centred universe. Some theologians argued that the theory contradicted the Bible. In 1616 the Church declared heliocentrism heretical or erroneous, and Cardinal Bellarmine instructed Galileo not to hold or defend it.

## The Dialogue and the trial

In 1632, after his friend Cardinal Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII, Galileo published the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, which presented arguments for and against Copernicus in a conversation between three characters, in Italian rather than Latin. The book clearly favoured Copernicus, and the pope felt mocked by the character Simplicio, who defended the traditional view. In 1633 the Roman Inquisition tried Galileo, aged sixty-nine, found him "vehemently suspect of heresy" and forced him to recant. The legend that he muttered "and yet it moves" is probably apocryphal. He was sentenced to house arrest.

## Last years

At his villa in Arcetri, near Florence, Galileo wrote Two New Sciences, published in the Netherlands in 1638, summarising his work on motion and the strength of materials. He became blind in 1638 and died on 8 January 1642. In 1992 Pope John Paul II acknowledged that the Church had erred in condemning him.
