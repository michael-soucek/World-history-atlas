Ramesses II, known as Ramesses the Great, was a pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty, ruling for sixty-six years, from 1279 to 1213 BCE. He led military campaigns in the Levant and Nubia, signed the earliest known peace treaty, with the Hittites, and built more monuments than any other pharaoh, including the temples of Abu Simbel.

## Prince

Ramesses was born around 1303 BCE, the son of Seti I and Queen Tuya. His grandfather, Ramesses I, a military officer from the Nile Delta, had founded the dynasty. Seti made Ramesses prince regent as a teenager, and he accompanied his father on campaigns. He became pharaoh around 1279 BCE, in his mid-twenties.

## Kadesh

Ramesses sought to recover Egyptian territory in Syria from the Hittite Empire. In his fifth year, around 1274 BCE, he led an army of four divisions north to the city of Kadesh, on the Orontes river. Misled by two captured spies, he camped near the city without knowing that the Hittite king Muwatalli II's army was hidden nearby. The Hittite chariots attacked, routing one Egyptian division and surrounding Ramesses's camp. According to his own account, Ramesses fought almost alone, with the help of the god Amun, until reinforcements arrived. The battle, one of the largest chariot battles in history, ended in a stalemate, but Ramesses celebrated it as a great victory on temple walls throughout Egypt.

## The peace treaty

After years of further conflict, around 1259 BCE Ramesses and the Hittite king Hattusili III signed a peace treaty, the earliest known peace treaty in history. Copies survive in Egyptian hieroglyphics at Karnak and in Akkadian cuneiform on clay tablets found at Hattusa, the Hittite capital. It agreed mutual non-aggression, an alliance and the return of fugitives. A copy hangs at the United Nations. Ramesses later married a Hittite princess.

## Builder

Ramesses built or enlarged temples across Egypt and Nubia. At Abu Simbel, in Nubia, he carved two temples into the rock: the Great Temple, fronted by four colossal seated statues of himself, about 20 metres tall, and a smaller temple for his chief wife, Nefertari, and the goddess Hathor. The Great Temple is aligned so that twice a year the rising sun lights the statues in the inner sanctuary. In the 1960s, both temples were cut into blocks and moved to higher ground to save them from the waters of Lake Nasser, created by the Aswan High Dam. He built his mortuary temple, the Ramesseum, at Thebes, and added to Karnak and Luxor. He founded a new capital, Pi-Ramesses, in the eastern Delta. He also put his name on many earlier monuments.

## Family

Ramesses had many wives, including Nefertari, whose tomb in the Valley of the Queens is famous for its paintings, and Isetnofret. He had more than a hundred children, many of whom died before him; his thirteenth son, Merneptah, succeeded him.

## Death and legacy

Ramesses died around 1213 BCE, aged about ninety. His mummy was moved by priests to protect it from robbers and was found in a cache at Deir el-Bahari in 1881. In 1976 it was flown to Paris for conservation, with an Egyptian passport. Ramesses is often identified with the pharaoh of the Exodus in popular culture, though there is no historical evidence for this. The poem "Ozymandias" by Percy Bysshe Shelley was inspired by him.
