Attila was the ruler of the Huns from 434 to 453 CE. From his base on the Hungarian plain, he led a vast empire of nomadic and subject peoples, terrorised both the Eastern and Western Roman Empires, and became, in European memory, the archetype of the barbarian conqueror, the "Scourge of God".

## The Huns

The Huns were a nomadic people who appeared north of the Black Sea around 370 CE, possibly from Central Asia; their origins, and any link with the Xiongnu of Mongolia, are debated. Skilled horse archers using powerful composite bows, they defeated the Alans and the Goths, driving many Goths into the Roman Empire. By the early fifth century they had settled on the Hungarian plain and ruled over many peoples, including Goths, Gepids, Alans and Slavs.

## Joint rule with Bleda

Attila was born around 406, a nephew of King Rugila. When Rugila died in 434, Attila and his elder brother Bleda inherited the kingdom. They negotiated the Treaty of Margus with the Eastern Roman Empire in 435, doubling the annual tribute to 700 pounds of gold and securing the return of Hun fugitives. Around 445 Bleda died, probably murdered by Attila, who became sole ruler.

## Attacking the Eastern Empire

Attila invaded the Balkans repeatedly, in 441–443 and 447, sacking cities such as Naissus, Serdica and Marcianopolis, and defeating Roman armies. After each campaign, the Eastern emperor Theodosius II paid more tribute; in 447 it rose to 2,100 pounds of gold a year, with a lump sum of 6,000 pounds.

## Priscus at Attila's court

In 449 the Roman diplomat Priscus accompanied an embassy to Attila's court, near the Tisza river, and left the most vivid account of him. He describes a wooden palace, a banquet at which Attila, unlike his guests, ate from a wooden plate and dressed simply, and a Greek merchant who had chosen to live among the Huns, praising their fairness compared with Roman corruption. Priscus describes Attila as short and broad-chested, with small eyes and a flat nose.

## Invasion of Gaul

In 450 Honoria, sister of the Western emperor Valentinian III, who was being forced into marriage, sent Attila a ring and asked for help. Attila claimed her as his bride and half of the Western Empire as dowry. In 451 he invaded Gaul with a large army. The Roman general Aetius, who had lived among the Huns as a hostage, formed an alliance with the Visigoths under King Theodoric I and others. At the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains, in north-eastern France, the allies stopped Attila in a bloody battle in which Theodoric was killed. Attila withdrew.

## Italy

In 452 Attila invaded Italy, besieging and destroying Aquileia; refugees fleeing to the lagoons are traditionally said to have helped found Venice. He sacked Milan and Padua. Near Mantua, he met an embassy that included Pope Leo I, and withdrew. Later tradition credited the pope's persuasion, but famine, disease in his army and an Eastern Roman attack on the Hun homeland probably mattered more.

## Death and collapse

In 453, after his marriage to a young woman named Ildico, Attila was found dead, apparently of a severe nosebleed or haemorrhage, after a night of feasting. He was buried in secret, according to the Gothic historian Jordanes, in coffins of gold, silver and iron. His sons fought over the empire, and in 454 the Gepids and other subject peoples defeated them at the Nedao river. The Hun empire collapsed.

## Legacy

Attila appears in Germanic legend as Etzel, in the Nibelungenlied, and Atli, in Norse sagas, and in Hungarian tradition, where Attila is a popular name, as an ancestor-figure.
