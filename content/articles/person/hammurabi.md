Hammurabi was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon, ruling from about 1792 to 1750 BCE. He turned Babylon from a minor city-state into the capital of an empire covering most of Mesopotamia, and he is best known for his law code, carved on a stone stele, one of the longest and best-preserved legal texts of the ancient world.

## Mesopotamia in his time

In the early second millennium BCE, Mesopotamia was divided among competing kingdoms, many ruled by Amorite dynasties, such as Larsa in the south, Eshnunna, Mari on the Euphrates and the kingdom of Shamshi-Adad in the north. Babylon was a small kingdom. Hammurabi inherited the throne from his father, Sin-Muballit. Thousands of letters from the archives of Mari show the complex diplomacy of the period; one says that no king is strong on his own, but that ten or fifteen kings follow Hammurabi of Babylon.

## Building an empire

For most of his reign, Hammurabi built canals and temples and strengthened his city's walls. In the last decade or so of his reign, he went to war. Around 1763 BCE he conquered Larsa, ruled by Rim-Sin, after a long siege, partly by damming the river. He defeated Eshnunna and Elam, and around 1761 BCE conquered Mari, his former ally, whose palace was destroyed. He extended his rule north to Assyria. Babylon became the political centre of Mesopotamia, and remained its cultural capital for over a thousand years.

## Government

Hammurabi's letters show him closely involved in administration, dealing with canals, taxes, temple estates and disputes. He appointed governors and officials and maintained the irrigation system on which agriculture depended.

## The code

Towards the end of his reign, Hammurabi had his laws inscribed on stone stelae set up in temples. The best-known stele, made of black basalt, over two metres tall, was found in 1901 at Susa, in Iran, where the Elamites had carried it as plunder in the twelfth century BCE; it is now in the Louvre. At the top, a relief shows Hammurabi standing before Shamash, the god of the sun and justice. Below, in Akkadian cuneiform, a prologue, 282 laws and an epilogue are inscribed.

## The laws

The laws cover trade, property, loans, wages, marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, theft, assault, medical malpractice, building and slavery. They follow a pattern: "if a man does this, then this shall be the penalty". Some apply the principle of retaliation, "an eye for an eye": if a man destroys another's eye, his eye shall be destroyed. Punishments depended on social class: injuries to a free man, awilum, were punished more severely than injuries to a commoner, mushkenum, or a slave. Some laws protected the weak, such as widows. The epilogue says that the laws were set up so "that the strong might not oppress the weak, to give justice to the orphan and the widow".

## A code?

Scholars debate whether Hammurabi's laws were a legal code applied in courts, as legal documents of the time rarely cite them, or rather a royal statement of justice and model judgements, showing the king as a just ruler. Earlier law collections, such as that of Ur-Nammu, existed, but Hammurabi's was copied by scribes for centuries.

## Legacy

Hammurabi died around 1750 BCE. His successors lost much of the empire, and Babylon was sacked by the Hittites in 1595 BCE. His image appears among the lawgivers in the US House of Representatives.
