Abraham Lincoln was the sixteenth president of the United States, from 1861 until his assassination in 1865. He led the Union through the Civil War, preserved the United States as one nation and issued the Emancipation Proclamation, which set the country on the path to abolishing slavery.

## Frontier boyhood

Lincoln was born on 12 February 1809 in a log cabin in Kentucky, to a poor farming family. The family moved to Indiana and then Illinois. His mother died when he was nine. He had less than a year of formal schooling but read widely, borrowing books such as the Bible, Aesop's Fables and the works of Shakespeare. He worked as a rail-splitter, a flatboatman taking cargo down the Mississippi to New Orleans, a storekeeper and a postmaster in New Salem, Illinois, and served briefly as a militia captain in the Black Hawk War of 1832.

## Lawyer and politician

Lincoln taught himself law and became a lawyer in Springfield in 1837, riding the circuit across Illinois. He served four terms in the Illinois legislature as a Whig and one term in the US House of Representatives, from 1847 to 1849, where he opposed the Mexican–American War. In 1842 he married Mary Todd; of their four sons, only one, Robert, survived to adulthood.

## Against the spread of slavery

The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, which allowed new territories to choose slavery, brought Lincoln back into politics. He joined the new Republican Party, which opposed the expansion of slavery. In 1858, running for the Senate against Stephen Douglas, he declared that "a house divided against itself cannot stand", and the two held seven famous debates across Illinois. Lincoln lost the election but became nationally known.

## Election and secession

In 1860 Lincoln won the presidency with less than 40 percent of the popular vote, carrying no southern state. Seven southern states seceded before he took office and formed the Confederate States of America. In his inaugural address, he appealed to "the better angels of our nature". In April 1861 Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter, and the Civil War began; four more states joined the Confederacy.

## War leader

Lincoln struggled to find effective generals, replacing several commanders of the Army of the Potomac, until he gave command of all Union armies to Ulysses S. Grant in 1864. He suspended habeas corpus in some areas, a controversial step. On 1 January 1863 he issued the Emancipation Proclamation, declaring free the enslaved people in areas in rebellion and allowing Black men to enlist; about 180,000 served in the Union army. In November 1863 he delivered the Gettysburg Address, a speech of about 270 words dedicating a cemetery, which redefined the war as a struggle for "a new birth of freedom" and government "of the people, by the people, for the people".

## Re-election and victory

Lincoln won re-election in 1864, helped by Union victories, especially Sherman's capture of Atlanta. He pushed Congress to pass the Thirteenth Amendment, abolishing slavery throughout the country, which was ratified in December 1865. In his second inaugural address, in March 1865, he called for "malice toward none" and "charity for all". General Robert E. Lee surrendered at Appomattox on 9 April 1865.

## Assassination

On 14 April 1865, Good Friday, Lincoln was shot by the actor John Wilkes Booth, a Confederate sympathiser, at Ford's Theatre in Washington, and died the next morning. He was the first American president to be assassinated. His funeral train carried his body to Springfield.

## Legacy

Lincoln is consistently ranked by historians among the greatest American presidents. Historians continue to discuss his views on race, which changed over time, his early support for colonising freed slaves abroad and his wartime use of executive power. The Lincoln Memorial in Washington was dedicated in 1922.
