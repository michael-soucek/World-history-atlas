Mansa Musa was the ruler of the Mali Empire in West Africa from about 1312 to 1337. Under him, Mali reached its height, and his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, with a vast caravan and huge amounts of gold, made him famous across the Islamic world and Europe as one of the richest rulers in history.

## The Mali Empire

The Mali Empire was founded in the thirteenth century by Sundiata Keita, who defeated the Sosso kingdom around 1235. Mali controlled the gold fields of Bambuk and Bure and the trade routes across the Sahara, where gold was exchanged for salt, copper, cloth and horses from North Africa. Its rulers took the title mansa, "king". Musa, a grandnephew of Sundiata, came to the throne around 1312. According to his own account, reported by the Syrian writer al-Umari, his predecessor, Abubakari, had sailed into the Atlantic with a fleet of ships to discover what lay beyond the ocean and never returned.

## The pilgrimage

In 1324 Mansa Musa set out on the hajj to Mecca, crossing the Sahara with a huge caravan. Accounts say it included thousands of people, courtiers, soldiers and enslaved servants, and camels carrying large amounts of gold. In Cairo, he stayed for three months, and he and his followers spent and gave away so much gold that its value in Egypt fell for years afterwards, according to al-Umari, who visited Cairo twelve years later. He reportedly ran out of money on the way back and had to borrow from Cairo merchants. His pilgrimage made Mali famous: on the Catalan Atlas of 1375, made in Majorca, Mansa Musa is shown seated on a throne, holding a gold nugget and wearing a gold crown.

## Expansion

During or after his pilgrimage, Mali's general Saghmandia took the city of Gao, on the Niger river, capital of the Songhai, extending Mali's rule east. At its height, Mali stretched from the Atlantic coast, in today's Senegal and Gambia, to beyond the Niger bend, including much of today's Mali, Guinea, Mauritania and Niger, with dozens of provinces and vassal kingdoms.

## Timbuktu and learning

Mansa Musa brought back from Mecca scholars and architects, including the Andalusian poet and architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, who is credited with building the Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu, made of mud brick and wood, and a palace. Timbuktu became a centre of Islamic learning, with mosques and schools, and later, under the Songhai, a famous centre with many manuscripts. Mansa Musa promoted Islam, building mosques, sending students to study in Fez, in Morocco, and establishing Friday prayers, while most of his subjects continued to follow traditional religions.

## Government

Mali was governed through a network of provinces and vassals, with royal governors. The traveller Ibn Battuta, who visited Mali in 1352–1353, under Mansa Musa's brother Sulayman, described the safety of travel, the justice of the rulers and the ceremonies of the court, where people threw dust on their heads before the king.

## Death and legacy

Mansa Musa died around 1337. His son Maghan succeeded him. Mali declined in the fifteenth century, and the Songhai Empire took over its eastern lands. Mansa Musa is often described in modern lists as the richest person ever, but such comparisons cannot be measured; his wealth was based on control of the gold trade.
