Steve Biko was the leading thinker of the Black Consciousness Movement, which reshaped resistance to apartheid in [South Africa](/country/south-africa) in the late 1960s and 1970s. He argued that black South Africans had to free their own minds and lead their own struggle. His death in police custody in 1977, at the age of 30, made him one of the best-known martyrs of the anti-apartheid cause.

## Early life

Bantu Stephen Biko was born in Tarkastad on 18 December 1946, in the Eastern Cape, and grew up in Ginsberg, a poor township outside King William's Town. His father, a former policeman turned clerk, died when Biko was four, leaving the family to live on his mother's wages as a domestic worker and hospital cook. A gifted pupil, he won a place at the boarding school of Lovedale in 1964, but was expelled within months after police questioned him over his brother's alleged links to Poqo, the armed wing of the banned Pan Africanist Congress.

## Black Consciousness

In 1966 Biko began studying medicine at the University of Natal and joined the multiracial but white-led National Union of South African Students. A 1967 conference at which black African delegates were told to sleep in a church while others had dormitory rooms convinced him that black students needed their own organisation. He was the driving force behind the South African Students' Organisation, launched in 1969, and became its first president.

Drawing on Frantz Fanon and other writers, Biko and his colleagues developed Black Consciousness: the idea that black people must reject the sense of inferiority instilled by white rule and act for themselves. He rejected the official term "non-white" and used "black" for all those oppressed under apartheid, including Coloured and Indian South Africans. He popularised the phrase "black is beautiful" and wrote a column under the pen name Frank Talk. The movement also ran Black Community Programmes, such as a rural clinic near King William's Town and literacy and craft schemes. Barred from further study in 1972, he went to work for these programmes.

## Banned

In March 1973 the government served Biko with a banning order that confined him to the King William's Town district, forbade him to speak to more than one person at a time and barred the press from quoting him. He remained active, helping to found local community projects and a fund for political prisoners' families. He was detained four times, once for 101 days, and received threats. In these years he befriended Donald Woods, the white editor of the Daily Dispatch, and quietly sought contact with the ANC and the PAC in the hope of uniting the liberation movements.

## Death in custody

On 18 August 1977 Biko was stopped at a roadblock near Grahamstown, returning from Cape Town in breach of his ban, and arrested. In Port Elizabeth he was held naked and shackled, and during an interrogation in early September he suffered severe head injuries. On 11 September he was driven, naked and in manacles, in the back of a Land Rover to a prison hospital in Pretoria, where he died on 12 September 1977. About 20,000 people attended his funeral, among them an Anglican delegation led by Bishop [Desmond Tutu](/person/desmond-tutu). The police minister, Jimmy Kruger, at first implied that Biko had been on hunger strike; police later said he had hit his head in a scuffle. An inquest accepted the police account and no one was charged.

## Legacy

Biko's death drew worldwide condemnation of apartheid, and within weeks the government banned all Black Consciousness organisations. Woods's biography inspired the 1987 film Cry Freedom. After apartheid ended, five former security policemen applied to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission for amnesty; it was refused in 1998, but prosecutors decided in 2003 not to charge them. The government reopened the case in 2025, and a new inquest began in August 2026. Long before the [end of apartheid](/event/end-of-apartheid) he had become an icon of the struggle, often called the father of Black Consciousness, though his political legacy is still argued over.
