Qin Shi Huang was the founder of the Qin dynasty and the first emperor of a unified China, ruling as king of Qin from 246 BCE and as emperor from 221 BCE until his death in 210 BCE. He conquered the rival states, standardised writing, coins and measures, began the Great Wall and was buried with the Terracotta Army.

## King of Qin

He was born Ying Zheng in 259 BCE in Handan, capital of the state of Zhao, where his father, a prince of Qin, was a hostage. According to the historian Sima Qian, his real father may have been the merchant Lü Buwei, who helped his father become king, though historians doubt this story. He became king of Qin at thirteen, in 246 BCE, with Lü Buwei as chancellor. In 238 BCE he took power himself, crushing a revolt by his mother's lover, Lao Ai, and dismissing Lü Buwei, who later poisoned himself.

## Unification

Qin, in the west, had been reformed in the fourth century BCE by the minister Shang Yang along Legalist lines: strict laws, harsh punishments, rewards for farming and military service, and a centralised bureaucracy. With the advice of the Legalist minister Li Si, Ying Zheng conquered the six other Warring States in a decade: Han in 230 BCE, Zhao in 228, Wei in 225, Chu in 223, Yan in 222 and Qi in 221 BCE. In 227 BCE he survived an assassination attempt by Jing Ke, sent by the prince of Yan, who attacked him with a poisoned dagger.

## The first emperor

In 221 BCE Ying Zheng took the title Shi Huangdi, "first emperor", combining words for divine rulers. He abolished the feudal states and divided the empire into commanderies and counties governed by appointed officials. He standardised the writing system, the coinage, weights and measures and the width of cart axles, built roads and canals, and connected and extended walls on the northern frontier against the Xiongnu, a forerunner of the Great Wall, built with forced labour.

## Burning books

According to Sima Qian, in 213 BCE, on Li Si's advice, the emperor ordered the burning of books, except those on medicine, agriculture and divination and the state's own history, to suppress criticism based on the past, and in 212 BCE had 460 scholars buried alive. Some historians think these accounts were exaggerated by later Confucian writers hostile to Qin.

## The search for immortality

The emperor became obsessed with immortality. He sent expeditions, such as that of Xu Fu, who sailed into the eastern sea to find the island of the immortals and never returned. He may have taken elixirs containing mercury. He made inspection tours across the empire, setting up inscribed stones praising his achievements.

## Death and the Terracotta Army

He died in 210 BCE, during a tour, aged forty-nine. Li Si and the eunuch Zhao Gao hid his death and forged orders forcing his heir, Fusu, to commit suicide, placing a younger son on the throne. The dynasty collapsed in 206 BCE. His tomb, near Xi'an, has not been opened, but in 1974 farmers digging a well discovered the Terracotta Army, thousands of life-size clay soldiers, with horses and chariots, buried to guard him in the afterlife. It is a World Heritage Site.
