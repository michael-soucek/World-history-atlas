Marie Curie was a Polish-born French physicist and chemist who pioneered research on radioactivity. She discovered the elements polonium and radium, was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and remains the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, physics and chemistry.

## Warsaw

She was born Maria Skłodowska on 7 November 1867 in Warsaw, then part of the Russian Empire, the youngest of five children of teachers. Her mother died of tuberculosis when she was ten. Women could not attend university in Russian-ruled Poland, so she studied secretly at the "Flying University" and worked as a governess to pay for her sister Bronisława's medical studies in Paris, on the understanding that her sister would later help her.

## Paris

In 1891 she moved to Paris and studied physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne, living in poverty in an attic. She graduated first in physics in 1893. In 1894 she met Pierre Curie, a physicist known for his work on magnetism and crystals, and they married in 1895. They had two daughters, Irène, who would also win a Nobel Prize, and Ève, who became a writer.

## Radioactivity

In 1896 Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium salts emit rays. Marie chose these rays as the subject of her doctoral research. Using an electrometer developed by Pierre, she found that the strength of the rays depended only on the amount of uranium, suggesting that the radiation came from the atom itself, a key insight. She coined the term "radioactivity". She found that the mineral pitchblende was more radioactive than its uranium content could explain, and concluded that it contained unknown elements.

## Polonium and radium

Working in a leaky shed, Marie and Pierre processed tonnes of pitchblende. In July 1898 they announced the discovery of polonium, named after Poland, and in December 1898 radium. Isolating pure radium took years of exhausting work. In 1903 the Curies and Becquerel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics; the committee had initially intended to honour only the men, until Pierre insisted that Marie be included.

## Tragedy and second Nobel

On 19 April 1906, Pierre was killed when he was struck by a horse-drawn carriage in a Paris street. Marie took over his professorship, becoming the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for isolating radium and studying its properties. That year she faced a press scandal over her relationship with the physicist Paul Langevin, a married man, and xenophobic attacks.

## War work

During the First World War, Marie developed mobile X-ray units, called "petites Curies", to help surgeons locate bullets and shrapnel in wounded soldiers. She learned to drive and operated them herself near the front, with Irène, and trained technicians. About a million soldiers were examined.

## Later life and death

Marie Curie directed the Radium Institute in Paris, which became a centre of research, and founded one in Warsaw. Years of exposure to radiation, not well understood at the time, damaged her health; her notebooks remain radioactive today. She died on 4 July 1934 of aplastic anaemia. In 1995 her remains and Pierre's were moved to the Panthéon in Paris; she was the first woman honoured there for her own achievements.
