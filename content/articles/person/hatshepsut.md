Hatshepsut was a pharaoh of Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty who ruled from about 1479 to 1458 BCE, during the New Kingdom. One of the few women to rule Egypt as pharaoh in her own right, she presided over a long, peaceful and prosperous reign, sponsored great building projects and an expedition to the land of Punt.

## Royal daughter and queen

Hatshepsut was the daughter of Thutmose I, a successful warrior king, and his principal wife, Ahmose. She married her half-brother, Thutmose II, and became his great royal wife; they had a daughter, Neferure. When Thutmose II died around 1479 BCE, his heir was a young son by a secondary wife, Thutmose III. Hatshepsut became regent for him.

## Pharaoh

Within a few years, by about 1473 BCE, Hatshepsut had herself crowned as pharaoh, taking the full royal titles, while Thutmose III remained co-ruler in name. As kingship was traditionally male, she was often depicted in statues and reliefs with the male royal regalia, including the false beard, and a male body, though some images show her as a woman, and inscriptions often use feminine grammar. She justified her rule by claiming that the god Amun was her true father, a story carved in her temple, and that Thutmose I had chosen her as his successor. Her chief steward and architect, Senenmut, a man of humble origins, was a powerful figure and tutor to Neferure; some suggest they were lovers, but evidence is lacking.

## Deir el-Bahari

Hatshepsut's greatest monument is her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, on the west bank of the Nile at Thebes, called Djeser-Djeseru, "holy of holies". Built against the cliffs, it rises in three colonnaded terraces connected by ramps, once with gardens of trees. Its reliefs record her divine birth and the Punt expedition. She also built at the temple of Karnak, setting up two great obelisks of red granite, one of which still stands, nearly thirty metres tall, and the Red Chapel. She restored temples damaged in the Hyksos period.

## The expedition to Punt

Around 1470 BCE, Hatshepsut sent a trading expedition of five ships down the Red Sea to Punt, a land probably on the coast of today's Eritrea, Sudan or Somalia. The reliefs at Deir el-Bahari show the Egyptians bringing back myrrh trees in baskets, incense, ebony, ivory, gold, exotic animals, such as baboons, and leopard skins, and show the houses of Punt, built on stilts, and its ruler and his wife.

## Military campaigns

Although her reign was mainly peaceful, inscriptions suggest campaigns in Nubia and the Levant, probably led by Thutmose III, who became a great warrior king.

## Death and erasure

Hatshepsut died around 1458 BCE, and Thutmose III ruled alone for over thirty years. Late in his reign, about twenty years after her death, her images and names were removed from many monuments, her statues were smashed and her name was left out of later king lists. Historians once saw this as revenge, but its late date suggests it was a political move to secure the succession for Thutmose's son and remove the precedent of a female king.

## Rediscovery

Hatshepsut was rediscovered by Egyptologists in the nineteenth century. In 2007 a mummy found in a minor tomb, KV60, in the Valley of the Kings, was identified as hers, based on a tooth found in a box bearing her name, though some scholars question the identification.
