Diponegoro was a Javanese prince of the Yogyakarta court who led the great revolt against Dutch colonial power known as the [Java War](/event/java-war), fought from 1825 to 1830. Seized during truce talks and exiled to Sulawesi for the rest of his life, he became one of the founding heroes of Indonesian nationalism and was declared a National Hero of Indonesia in 1973.

## A prince passed over

Born in Yogyakarta on 11 November 1785, he was the eldest son of the future Sultan Hamengkubuwono III. He grew up in turbulent years: the Dutch East India Company was dissolved, the British occupied Java in 1811 and sacked the Yogyakarta palace in 1812, and Dutch rule returned in 1816. He had helped the British and was offered a senior royal position, but declined it, and when his father died in 1814 a younger half-brother became sultan with Dutch backing. A devout Muslim given to retreat and mystical experience, Diponegoro disliked the laxity and pro-Dutch politics of the court, and he wrote of visions, including one of the legendary sea queen Nyai Roro Kidul.

## Grievances and prophecy

When the next sultan died in 1822 leaving an infant heir, Diponegoro was made one of the guardians but again denied power. Meanwhile peasants suffered from new taxes and failed harvests, and nobles resented Dutch interference in their land leases. Cholera, an eruption of Mount Merapi in 1822 and a millenarian mood convinced many that disaster was coming, and that Diponegoro might be the Ratu Adil, the just king foretold in Javanese prophecy. The spark came in mid-1825, when the Dutch resident had a road laid past his estate at Tegalrejo. On 20 July troops were sent to arrest him; he escaped to the cave of Selarong and declared a holy war.

## The Java War

At first the rebels swept the countryside and besieged Yogyakarta, and some seventy members of the royal family joined Diponegoro. Dutch relief forces lifted the siege in 1825, and in October 1826 he was defeated near Gawok, west of Surakarta. The war then became a guerrilla struggle that disease and hostile villages made very costly for the colonial army. From 1827 General Hendrik Merkus de Kock answered with a network of small forts linked by mobile columns, steadily squeezing the rebel zone. Defections, the surrender of the religious leader Kyai Maja in 1828 and a decisive defeat at Siluk in September 1829 left Diponegoro with only a small band. His forces also massacred Chinese communities, leaving lasting bitterness.

## Capture and exile

In March 1830 he came to Magelang to negotiate with de Kock, demanding recognition as sultan and Muslim leader of Java. On 28 March de Kock had him arrested. Diponegoro regarded it as treachery under a flag of truce; the Dutch presented it as a surrender, and two famous paintings, by the Javanese artist Raden Saleh and the Dutch painter Nicolaas Pieneman, show the scene in opposite ways. He was shipped to Manado and in 1833 moved to the stronger fortress town of Makassar, where he was held at Fort Rotterdam and where he wrote his autobiographical chronicle, the Babad Diponegoro. He died there on 8 January 1855.

## Cost and legacy

The war devastated central Java. At least 200,000 Javanese died from violence, hunger and disease, while the Dutch lost about 15,000 soldiers, some 8,000 of them Dutch. The princely states lost most of their remaining land, and to recover its costs the colonial government introduced the forced Cultivation System. No comparable armed challenge to Dutch rule in Java came until the [Indonesian National Revolution](/event/indonesian-national-revolution). Diponegoro's name is now borne by an army command, a university and countless streets, and in 2020 the Dutch king returned to [Indonesia](/country/indonesia) a kris believed to be his.
