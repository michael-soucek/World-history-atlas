Harun al-Rashid was the fifth Abbasid caliph, ruling from 786 to 809. His reign, at the height of Abbasid power and wealth, is remembered as a golden age of Baghdad, partly because he appears as a character in the One Thousand and One Nights.

## Prince

Harun was born in Rey, near today's Tehran, around 763 or 766, the son of the caliph al-Mahdi and al-Khayzuran, a former slave from Yemen who became a powerful political figure. As a young man, he led campaigns against the Byzantine Empire, reaching the Bosporus in 782, where the Byzantine empress Irene agreed to pay tribute. His father gave him the title al-Rashid, "the rightly guided". His brother al-Hadi became caliph in 785 but died the next year, possibly murdered on their mother's orders, and Harun became caliph.

## The Barmakids

For the first seventeen years of his reign, government was largely run by the Barmakid family, of Persian Buddhist origin from Balkh: Yahya ibn Khalid, Harun's former tutor, as vizier, and his sons, al-Fadl and Ja'far, Harun's close friend. They were patrons of scholars and poets. In 803 Harun suddenly destroyed the family: Ja'far was executed, and Yahya and al-Fadl imprisoned, where they died. The reasons are unclear; the family's wealth and power may have alarmed him.

## Baghdad

Baghdad, founded by Harun's grandfather al-Mansur in 762, became under Harun one of the largest and richest cities in the world, with perhaps several hundred thousand people. Its markets traded goods from China, India, Africa and Europe. Paper making, learned from China, spread, and Harun's reign saw the beginnings of the translation movement, in which Greek, Persian and Indian works were translated into Arabic. The House of Wisdom developed from a library associated with him, expanded by his son al-Ma'mun. Poets, such as Abu Nuwas, musicians, such as Ibrahim al-Mawsili, and scholars flourished at his court. Harun made the pilgrimage to Mecca several times.

## Wars and diplomacy

Harun fought the Byzantines almost every year, leading campaigns himself. In 806 he took Heraclea, in Anatolia, forcing the emperor Nikephoros I to pay tribute. He exchanged embassies with Charlemagne, sending him gifts, including an elephant named Abul-Abbas and a water clock. He faced revolts in North Africa, Syria, Yemen and Khurasan. In 800 he recognised the Aghlabid dynasty in Ifriqiya, today's Tunisia, as hereditary governors, an early step in the empire's fragmentation.

## Succession and death

Harun divided the succession between his sons: al-Amin, son of the Arab princess Zubayda, would be caliph, and al-Ma'mun, son of a Persian concubine, would govern Khurasan and succeed al-Amin. Harun died in 809 at Tus, in Khurasan, while on campaign against a revolt. His sons fought a civil war, which al-Ma'mun won in 813.

## The Arabian Nights

Harun appears in many stories of the One Thousand and One Nights, often wandering in disguise through Baghdad at night with Ja'far and his executioner Masrur, encountering adventures and dispensing justice. These stories, written later, created the image of Harun as a magnificent, generous, sometimes capricious ruler of a magical Baghdad, which remains popular.
