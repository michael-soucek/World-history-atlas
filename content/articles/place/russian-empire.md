The Russian Empire was proclaimed in 1721, when Peter the Great took the title of emperor after defeating Sweden in the Great Northern War. It lasted until 1917, and at its height in the late nineteenth century it covered about a sixth of the world's land, from Poland and Finland to Alaska, which it sold to the United States in 1867.

## Peter and Catherine

Peter the Great set out to modernise Russia on western European lines. He built a navy, reorganised the army and government, made nobles shave their beards and serve the state, and built a new capital, Saint Petersburg, on land taken from Sweden. Catherine the Great (1762–1796) extended the empire to the Black Sea, annexed Crimea in 1783 and took the largest share of Poland-Lithuania in its partitions.

## The autocracy

The Russian emperors ruled as autocrats, with no legal limits on their power until 1905. Their authority rested on the nobility, the army, the bureaucracy and the Orthodox Church. Under Nicholas I (1825–1855), the government's official ideology was summed up as "Orthodoxy, autocracy and nationality". The secret police, the Third Section, watched for dissent, and censorship was strict.

## Serfdom

Most Russians were peasants, and over a third were serfs, owned by nobles, bound to the land and liable to be bought and sold, flogged or sent into the army for 25 years. Others were state peasants. The Emancipation Edict of 1861, issued by Alexander II, freed over 20 million serfs, but they had to pay for the land they received over 49 years, and many remained poor.

## The Decembrists and revolutionaries

In December 1825, army officers influenced by liberal ideas rebelled in Saint Petersburg, demanding a constitution. The Decembrist revolt was crushed, and its leaders were hanged or exiled to Siberia. In the later nineteenth century, radical movements grew: the narodniks went to the countryside to educate peasants, and the terrorist group People's Will assassinated Alexander II in 1881. Marxist parties formed in the 1890s.

## Expansion

The empire expanded throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It took the Baltic provinces from Sweden, Crimea and the Black Sea coast from the Ottomans, most of Poland-Lithuania, Finland from Sweden in 1809, and Bessarabia in 1812. The Caucasus was conquered in long wars, including the deportation of the Circassians in the 1860s. Central Asia was conquered between the 1860s and 1880s. Russia sold Alaska to the United States in 1867. The Trans-Siberian Railway, built from 1891 to 1916, linked Moscow with Vladivostok.

## Wars

Russia defeated Napoleon in 1812. It fought the Ottoman Empire many times, and lost the Crimean War of 1853–1856 to Britain, France and the Ottomans, which exposed its backwardness. The Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878 helped Bulgaria, Serbia and Romania gain independence. Defeat by Japan in 1904–1905 led to the revolution of 1905.

## Industrialisation

From the 1890s, under finance minister Sergei Witte, Russia industrialised rapidly, with foreign investment in railways, coal, iron and oil, especially in Baku. Industrial workers, concentrated in large factories in Saint Petersburg and Moscow, faced harsh conditions. On Bloody Sunday in January 1905, troops fired on peaceful marchers in Saint Petersburg, sparking a revolution. Nicholas II agreed to create the Duma, an elected parliament, and Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin introduced land reforms but also executed many revolutionaries.

## Society and culture

The nineteenth century was the golden age of Russian culture, with writers such as Pushkin, Lermontov, Gogol, Turgenev, Dostoevsky and Tolstoy, and composers such as Glinka and Tchaikovsky. Russia's Jews, confined to the Pale of Settlement in the west, faced restrictions and pogroms, and millions emigrated, many to the United States.

## The end

The First World War brought defeats, millions of casualties and shortages. Nicholas II took personal command of the army in 1915, leaving the government to Empress Alexandra and Rasputin. Revolution in February 1917 forced his abdication; in October the Bolsheviks took power, and in July 1918 the imperial family was shot in Yekaterinburg.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire from 1783 to 1900. It is largest on the 1900 map at about 21.7 million square kilometres, three-quarters of it in today's Russia and much of the rest in Kazakhstan and Central Asia. Kyiv, Riga, Tallinn and Odesa are inside it from 1783, Warsaw from 1815, and Tashkent and Samarkand from 1878, after the conquest of Central Asia. Before it, our maps show the Tsardom of Muscovy; on the 1920 map the region belongs to the Soviet state.
