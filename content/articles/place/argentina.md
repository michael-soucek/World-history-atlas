Argentina takes its name from the Latin word for silver, after the River Plate, the Río de la Plata, whose estuary early Spanish explorers hoped would lead to silver mines. It became independent from Spain in the early nineteenth century and grew, by the early twentieth, into one of the richest countries in the world.

## Indigenous peoples and colonisation

Before the Spanish arrived, Argentina was home to diverse peoples: the Diaguita in the north-west, part of the Inca Empire in the fifteenth century, the Guaraní in the north-east, and hunting peoples such as the Tehuelche and Mapuche in the Pampas and Patagonia. Spanish settlers founded Buenos Aires twice, in 1536 and permanently in 1580, and cities such as Córdoba and Tucumán. For two centuries, the region was a remote part of the Viceroyalty of Peru.

## Independence

The May Revolution of 1810 created a junta in Buenos Aires. José de San Martín, an officer trained in Spain, organised an army in Mendoza and crossed the Andes in 1817, freeing Chile and then Peru. Argentina declared independence on 9 July 1816 at the Congress of Tucumán. Manuel Belgrano designed the national flag.

## Civil wars and Rosas

After independence, Unitarians, who wanted a centralised state led by Buenos Aires, fought Federalists, who wanted provincial autonomy, led by regional strongmen, caudillos. Juan Manuel de Rosas, governor of Buenos Aires, ruled as a dictator from 1829 to 1852, using terror against opponents. The writer Domingo Faustino Sarmiento, later president, criticised caudillo rule in his book Facundo, framing Argentina's struggle as one of "civilisation and barbarism".

## Building the nation

After Rosas's defeat in 1852, the Constitution of 1853 created a federal republic. Argentina fought with Brazil and Uruguay against Paraguay from 1864 to 1870. The Conquest of the Desert, led by General Julio Argentino Roca in the late 1870s, took the Pampas and Patagonia from Indigenous peoples, killing thousands. Millions of European immigrants arrived, especially from Italy and Spain, and by the early twentieth century Argentina was one of the richest countries in the world, exporting beef and grain.

## Radicals and the Infamous Decade

The Sáenz Peña Law of 1912 introduced secret, compulsory male suffrage, and the Radical Civic Union governed from 1916. A military coup in 1930, during the Great Depression, began the "Infamous Decade" of electoral fraud.

## Peronism

Colonel Juan Domingo Perón rose to power through his support among workers and was elected president in 1946. With his wife, Eva Perón, "Evita", who led charitable work and campaigned for women's suffrage, granted in 1947, he expanded workers' rights, nationalised industries and built a powerful political movement. Evita died in 1952, and Perón was overthrown in 1955. He returned in 1973 and died in 1974, succeeded by his third wife, Isabel.

## Dictatorship and democracy

The military seized power in 1976. During its "Dirty War", around 30,000 people, by human-rights groups' estimates, "disappeared". The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo marched to demand news of their children. After defeat in the Falklands War of 1982, democracy returned in 1983 under Raúl Alfonsín, and military leaders were tried. Argentina has faced repeated economic crises, including a default in 2001. Argentines such as Jorge Luis Borges, Che Guevara, Diego Maradona, Lionel Messi and Pope Francis are known worldwide.
