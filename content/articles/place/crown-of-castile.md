Castile was the kingdom at the heart of medieval Spain. Its name comes from the many castles along its frontier with the Muslim south, and it grew from a small county north of Burgos into the largest and most powerful of the Christian kingdoms of Iberia, leading the conquest of al-Andalus and, under Isabella, sponsoring Christopher Columbus.

## From county to kingdom

Castile began in the ninth century as a frontier county of the Kingdom of León, on the upper Ebro and Duero rivers, facing raids from Muslim al-Andalus. Count Fernán González made it largely independent in the tenth century. In 1035 Ferdinand I became the first king of Castile, and in 1037 he also took León. The epic hero Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, El Cid, a Castilian noble who served both Christian and Muslim rulers, took Valencia in 1094.

## Toledo and the frontier

In 1085 Alfonso VI of León and Castile captured Toledo, the old Visigothic capital, a great blow to al-Andalus. Toledo became a centre of translation where Christian, Muslim and Jewish scholars translated Arabic works of philosophy, science and medicine into Latin, bringing Aristotle and Arab learning to Europe. Castile and León separated and reunited several times, until Ferdinand III united them permanently in 1230, forming the Crown of Castile.

## The great conquests

After the victory of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, Ferdinand III conquered Córdoba in 1236, Jaén in 1246 and Seville in 1248. Only the Emirate of Granada remained in Muslim hands, as a vassal of Castile. Ferdinand was later canonised as Saint Ferdinand. The conquered lands were divided among nobles, the church and the military orders of Santiago, Calatrava and Alcántara.

## Alfonso the Wise

Ferdinand's son Alfonso X, "the Wise", who ruled from 1252 to 1284, was a great patron of learning. His court produced the Siete Partidas, a comprehensive law code; a history of Spain and a world history; astronomical tables, the Alfonsine Tables, used across Europe for centuries; and the Cantigas de Santa María, songs to the Virgin. He made Castilian, rather than Latin, the language of government and scholarship, laying the foundations of modern Spanish.

## Wool and society

Castile's economy depended on sheep farming. The Mesta, a powerful association of sheep owners, moved huge flocks of merino sheep between summer and winter pastures, and Castilian wool was exported to Flanders and Italy. The Cortes, the parliament, represented nobles, clergy and towns. Castile's society included Christians, Jews and Muslims, the Mudéjares, living side by side, but tensions grew. In 1391 anti-Jewish riots spread across Castile, killing thousands and forcing many to convert; converts, conversos, were later suspected of secretly practising Judaism.

## Civil war and the Trastámaras

In the fourteenth century, King Peter I, "the Cruel", fought his half-brother Henry of Trastámara, who killed him in 1369 with French help. The Trastámara dynasty ruled Castile and, from 1412, Aragon.

## Isabella and Ferdinand

In 1469 Isabella, half-sister of King Henry IV, married Ferdinand, heir of Aragon. After winning a civil war against her niece Joanna, supported by Portugal, Isabella became queen in 1474, and Ferdinand king of Aragon in 1479. They ruled their kingdoms jointly but separately. In 1478 they set up the Spanish Inquisition. In 1492 they conquered Granada, expelled the Jews and sponsored Columbus's voyage. The Americas were incorporated into the Crown of Castile, which bore the main burden and benefits of the Spanish Empire.

## Into Spain

After Isabella's death in 1504, Castile passed to her daughter Joanna and then to Joanna's son Charles, who also inherited Aragon in 1516. The Revolt of the Comuneros in 1520–1521, a rising of Castilian towns against Charles's foreign advisers and taxes, was crushed. Castile became the core of the Spanish monarchy, and Madrid its capital from 1561.
