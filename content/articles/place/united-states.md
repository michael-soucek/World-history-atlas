The United States began as thirteen British colonies along the Atlantic coast of North America. In 1776 they declared independence, and after winning the Revolutionary War they were recognised by Britain in 1783. In the following century the country expanded across the continent to the Pacific, and in the twentieth century it became the world's leading power.

## A new republic

The Constitution, written in 1787, created a federal republic with a president, a two-chamber Congress and a Supreme Court, and the Bill of Rights added guarantees of individual liberties in 1791. George Washington was the first president. The new nation's founding ideals of liberty and equality sat beside the reality of slavery, which was legal in all thirteen original states at independence and became central to the economy of the South.

## The early republic

Under the Constitution, the first political parties formed: Alexander Hamilton's Federalists, who favoured a strong federal government, and Thomas Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans. The War of 1812 with Britain ended in a stalemate. Andrew Jackson, president from 1829 to 1837, expanded democracy for white men but forced Native Americans west in the Indian Removal Act of 1830. In the 1840s, the idea of "Manifest Destiny" justified expansion to the Pacific.

## Westward expansion

The Louisiana Purchase from France in 1803 doubled the country's size. Texas was annexed in 1845, the Oregon country was divided with Britain in 1846, and the war with Mexico of 1846–1848 brought California and the south-west. Alaska was bought from Russia in 1867 and Hawaii annexed in 1898. Expansion came at the expense of Native American nations, who were pushed off their lands by war, treaty and forced removal, as in the Trail of Tears of the 1830s.

## Slavery and abolition

Slavery was central to the economy of the South, where enslaved people grew cotton, the country's main export. By 1860 there were about four million enslaved people. Abolitionists, such as Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery, and Harriet Beecher Stowe, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin, campaigned against it. The Underground Railroad helped enslaved people escape, with guides such as Harriet Tubman. Disputes over slavery in new territories led to secession and the Civil War.

## Reconstruction and the Gilded Age

After the war, the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments abolished slavery, guaranteed citizenship and equal protection, and gave Black men the vote. But after 1877, southern states imposed segregation, the "Jim Crow" laws, and denied Black people the vote, enforced by violence, including lynching. In the late nineteenth century, the United States industrialised rapidly, with railroads, steel and oil, and industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller became enormously rich. The transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869. On the Great Plains, the US Army fought the Plains peoples, and the massacre at Wounded Knee in 1890 marked the end of armed Native resistance.

## Immigration

Tens of millions of immigrants arrived between 1840 and 1920, from Ireland, Germany, Italy, Scandinavia, Eastern Europe and Asia, many passing through Ellis Island in New York. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the Immigration Act of 1924 restricted immigration from Asia and southern and eastern Europe.

## World power

The United States took Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines from Spain in 1898, and annexed Hawaii. It entered the First World War in 1917. The 1920s brought prosperity, jazz and Prohibition, and women won the vote in 1920. The Great Depression from 1929 led to Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal. After Pearl Harbor in 1941, the United States became the "arsenal of democracy" and, in 1945, used atomic bombs against Japan.

## The Cold War and civil rights

After 1945, the United States led the Western alliance in the Cold War, fought wars in Korea and Vietnam and raced the Soviet Union to the Moon. The civil rights movement, led by Martin Luther King Jr. and others, ended legal segregation with the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. The women's movement and other movements followed.

## Recent history

After the Soviet collapse, the United States was the world's sole superpower. The attacks of 11 September 2001 led to wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. Barack Obama, elected in 2008, became the first African American president. Today the United States has the world's largest economy and third-largest population.
