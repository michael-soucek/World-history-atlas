The Grand Principality of Moscow, or Muscovy, grew from a minor town in the forests of north-eastern Rus' into the core of the Russian state. In about two centuries, from the late thirteenth to the early sixteenth, its princes gathered the Russian lands under their rule and threw off the overlordship of the Golden Horde.

## A small town

Moscow first appears in the chronicles in 1147, when Prince Yuri Dolgoruky of Suzdal invited an ally to a feast there. It was a minor settlement in the principality of Vladimir-Suzdal, which the Mongols sacked in 1238. In the 1260s Moscow became a separate principality under Daniel, the youngest son of Alexander Nevsky, founding the line of Moscow princes.

## Rising under the Horde

Moscow's princes rose by cooperating with the khans of the Golden Horde, who granted them the title of grand prince of Vladimir. Ivan I, known as Kalita, "money bag", who ruled from 1325 to 1340, collected tribute from other Russian princes for the Horde, enriching Moscow, and bought land and villages. In 1325 the metropolitan, the head of the Russian Church, moved his seat to Moscow, making it the religious centre of Rus'. Moscow's rival, Tver, rebelled against the Horde in 1327, and Ivan helped crush it.

## Dmitry Donskoy

In the 1370s, Grand Prince Dmitry built the first stone walls of the Kremlin. In 1380 he led a coalition of Russian princes against the Horde general Mamai and won at Kulikovo, on the upper Don, earning the name Donskoy. The victory became a symbol of Russian resistance, though in 1382 Khan Tokhtamysh burned Moscow and restored the tribute. Saint Sergius of Radonezh, founder of the Trinity Monastery, blessed Dmitry before the battle. The icon painter Andrei Rublev, who painted the Trinity icon, worked in this era.

## Civil war

In the fifteenth century, a long civil war, from 1425 to 1453, between Vasily II and his uncle and cousins over the succession, saw Vasily captured and blinded, but he won. The victory confirmed succession from father to son. After the Council of Florence in 1439, where the Greek church accepted union with Rome, the Russian Church rejected the union and in 1448 chose its own metropolitan without Constantinople's approval, becoming effectively independent.

## Ivan III

Ivan III, "the Great", who ruled from 1462 to 1505, tripled Moscow's territory. He conquered Novgorod in 1478, taking its vast northern lands and removing its veche bell, and annexed Tver in 1485. In 1480, at the Ugra River, the armies of Ivan and Khan Akhmat of the Great Horde faced each other for weeks before the Horde withdrew, an event traditionally taken as the end of the "Tatar yoke". Ivan married Sophia Palaiologina, niece of the last Byzantine emperor, in 1472, adopted the double-headed eagle and the title "tsar" in diplomacy, and issued a law code, the Sudebnik, in 1497. Italian architects, such as Aristotele Fioravanti, rebuilt the Kremlin's cathedrals and walls.

## The Third Rome

After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, some Russian churchmen saw Moscow as the heir of Rome and Byzantium. Around 1510 the monk Filofei of Pskov wrote that "two Romes have fallen, the third stands, and a fourth there shall not be". The idea of Moscow as the "Third Rome" later became important to Russian identity.

## Vasily III and the tsardom

Ivan's son Vasily III completed the "gathering of the Russian lands", annexing Pskov in 1510, Smolensk from Lithuania in 1514 and Ryazan around 1521. In 1547 his son Ivan IV was crowned tsar of all Russia, and the Grand Principality became the Tsardom of Russia.
