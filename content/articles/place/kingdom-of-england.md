The Kingdom of England was formed in the tenth century, when the kings of Wessex brought the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms under their rule. Æthelstan, Alfred the Great's grandson, is usually counted the first king of all England from 927. The kingdom lasted until 1707, when it joined with Scotland to form Great Britain.

## Anglo-Saxon England

After the Romans left Britain, Angles, Saxons and Jutes settled much of the east and south in the fifth and sixth centuries, forming several kingdoms, including Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia and Wessex. Augustine's mission from Rome in 597 began the conversion of the English. The Northumbrian monk Bede wrote the Ecclesiastical History of the English People in 731. The Sutton Hoo ship burial, from the early seventh century, revealed the wealth of an East Anglian king.

## Alfred and the Danes

In 865 a Danish "Great Army" invaded and conquered every English kingdom except Wessex. King Alfred of Wessex defeated the Danes at Edington in 878 and divided England with them; the Danish area became the Danelaw. Alfred built fortified towns, burhs, created a navy, issued laws and promoted learning in English, sponsoring translations and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. His son Edward and grandson Æthelstan conquered the Danelaw, and Æthelstan became the first king of all England.

## The Norman Conquest

After 1066, William the Conqueror and his followers replaced the English nobility, built castles and recorded the kingdom's wealth in the Domesday Book of 1086. The Anglo-Norman kings ruled lands on both sides of the Channel. Under Henry II (1154–1189), the first Plantagenet king, the Angevin Empire stretched from Scotland's border to the Pyrenees. Henry developed the common law, with royal judges travelling the country. His quarrel with Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury, ended with Becket's murder in the cathedral in 1170.

## Magna Carta and Parliament

King John lost Normandy to France in 1204 and quarrelled with the pope and his barons. In 1215, at Runnymede, the barons forced him to accept Magna Carta, which established that the king was subject to law and guaranteed rights such as the right to a trial. Under his son Henry III, Simon de Montfort called a parliament in 1265 that included representatives of towns. Edward I conquered Wales in the 1280s and tried to conquer Scotland. Parliament, with the Lords and Commons, became a regular part of government.

## Wars and plague

The Hundred Years' War with France, the Black Death, which killed perhaps a third or more of the population, and the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 transformed English society. The Wars of the Roses, from 1455 to 1485, between the houses of Lancaster and York, ended when Henry Tudor defeated Richard III at Bosworth.

## The Tudors

Henry VII restored stability. Henry VIII broke with Rome in the 1530s, dissolved the monasteries and made himself head of the Church of England. His son Edward VI introduced Protestant reforms, his daughter Mary I restored Catholicism and burned Protestants, and Elizabeth I created a Protestant settlement and defeated the Spanish Armada.

## The Stuarts and the Civil War

James I, king of Scotland, inherited the English throne in 1603. His son Charles I clashed with Parliament over taxes and religion, leading to civil war from 1642. Parliament's New Model Army, led by Oliver Cromwell, won, and Charles was executed in 1649. England became a republic, the Commonwealth, until 1660, when Charles II was restored. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 established parliamentary supremacy, and in 1707 England and Scotland united.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Kingdom of England at several dates between 1000 and 1700. Its largest outline, on the 1650 map, covers about 320,000 square kilometres, but only about half of that is in today's United Kingdom: the basemap also counts Ireland, then under English rule, and the early English colonies along the North American coast. London is English throughout, Dublin from 1279 on several maps. After 1707 our maps show the United Kingdom.
