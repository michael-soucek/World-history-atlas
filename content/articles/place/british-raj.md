The British Raj was the period of direct rule over India by the British Crown, from 1858 to 1947. It covered what are now India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and until 1937 Burma, governed either directly as British India or through some six hundred princely states that accepted British supremacy.

## From Company to Crown

The British East India Company had conquered most of India between 1757 and 1856. In May 1857, Indian soldiers, sepoys, at Meerut rebelled, partly over rumours that new rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindus and Muslims. The rebellion spread across northern India, with rebels proclaiming the old Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader, and major fighting at Delhi, Kanpur and Lucknow. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi became a famous rebel leader. The British crushed the uprising with great brutality. In 1858 Parliament abolished the Company's rule, and India was governed by a secretary of state in London and a viceroy in India. In 1877 Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.

## Governing India

British India was governed by the Indian Civil Service, an elite of a few thousand mostly British officials, chosen by competitive examination. The princely states, such as Hyderabad, Kashmir and Mysore, were ruled by their own princes under British supervision. The Indian Army, recruited heavily from groups the British regarded as "martial races", such as Sikhs and Gurkhas, served across the empire. Calcutta was the capital until 1911, when it moved to Delhi; the new city of New Delhi was inaugurated in 1931.

## Economy

The Raj built one of the world's largest railway networks, about 65,000 kilometres by 1947, as well as canals, ports and telegraphs. India exported raw cotton, jute, tea, indigo and opium, and imported British manufactured goods, while its own handloom industry declined. Nationalist economists, such as Dadabhai Naoroji, argued that Britain drained India's wealth. Famines killed tens of millions, including the Great Famine of 1876–1878 and the Bengal famine of 1943, made worse by government policy.

## Society and reform

The Raj introduced Western-style education and universities in Bombay, Calcutta and Madras in 1857, creating an English-educated middle class. Social and religious reform movements, such as the Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj, and Muslim reformers like Sayyid Ahmad Khan, who founded what became Aligarh Muslim University, flourished. Census operations classified the population by caste and religion, which some historians argue hardened these identities.

## Nationalism

The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885. The partition of Bengal in 1905 sparked the Swadeshi movement, boycotting British goods. The Muslim League was founded in 1906. After the First World War, in which over a million Indians served, the Rowlatt Acts extended wartime repression, and on 13 April 1919 at Amritsar, General Reginald Dyer's troops fired on an unarmed crowd in the Jallianwala Bagh, killing hundreds. Gandhi turned the Congress into a mass movement with campaigns of non-cooperation in 1920–1922, the Salt March of 1930 and Quit India in 1942.

## The road to independence

The Government of India Act of 1935 gave provinces elected governments. During the Second World War, India provided over two million soldiers, while Subhas Chandra Bose led the Indian National Army with Japanese support. After the war, Britain's Labour government decided to leave. Negotiations between the Congress and the Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, failed, and communal violence broke out. On 14 and 15 August 1947, British India was partitioned into Pakistan and India, amid violence that killed hundreds of thousands and displaced around fifteen million people.
