The Tang dynasty ruled China from 618 to 907 and is often seen as a high point of Chinese civilisation. Its capital, Chang'an, was perhaps the largest city in the world, home to merchants, monks and envoys from across Asia, and Tang poetry is still learned by heart in China today.

## Taizong's empire

Li Shimin, the Taizong emperor, seized power in 626 by killing two of his brothers at the Xuanwu Gate and forcing his father to abdicate. He became one of the most admired emperors in Chinese history, known for listening to criticism from ministers such as Wei Zheng. Taizong defeated the Eastern Turks in 630, and the Turkic tribes gave him the title "Heavenly Khagan". He extended Tang control over the oasis states of the Tarim basin and established protectorates in Central Asia.

## Government

The Tang inherited and refined the institutions of the Sui. The central government was organised into three departments and six ministries, a structure that lasted for centuries. A law code, the Tang Code of 653, was one of the most influential legal texts in East Asia. The equal-field system allocated land to peasant households, who paid taxes in grain, cloth and labour. The examination system expanded, though aristocratic families still dominated high office.

## Chang'an

Chang'an, the capital, was a planned city of about a million people, laid out on a grid of wide avenues, with walled wards that were locked at night. Its two great markets sold goods from across Asia. Persians, Sogdians, Arabs, Turks, Koreans and Japanese lived there, and the city had Buddhist temples, Daoist monasteries, Zoroastrian fire temples, Manichaean shrines and a Nestorian Christian church, recorded on a stele erected in 781.

## Wu Zetian and Xuanzong

Empress Wu Zetian ruled from the 660s and declared her own Zhou dynasty in 690, promoting Buddhism and expanding the examinations. After the Tang restoration, the Xuanzong emperor's long reign, from 712 to 756, was a high point of culture and prosperity. He founded the Pear Garden academy for music and theatre, and his court was home to the poets Li Bai and Wang Wei and the painter Wu Daozi. In his later years, his infatuation with his consort Yang Guifei and reliance on ministers and frontier generals led to disaster.

## Buddhism and its persecution

Buddhism flourished under the Tang. The monk Xuanzang travelled to India from 629 to 645 and returned with hundreds of scriptures, which he translated. Chan Buddhism, later known in Japan as Zen, developed. But the wealth of the monasteries alarmed the state, and in 845 the Wuzong emperor ordered the destruction of thousands of monasteries and forced hundreds of thousands of monks and nuns to return to lay life.

## Poetry and printing

The Tang is the golden age of Chinese poetry. The Complete Tang Poems, compiled later, contains about 48,000 poems by over 2,000 poets. Li Bai wrote of wine, moonlight and friendship; Du Fu wrote of war and suffering; Bai Juyi wrote in simple language that ordinary people could understand. Woodblock printing developed, and the oldest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868, was found in the caves of Dunhuang.

## The An Lushan rebellion

In 755 the general An Lushan, who commanded huge frontier armies, rebelled. The war lasted eight years and devastated northern China. The Tang survived, but frontier governors became semi-independent and the Central Asian territories were lost, partly to the Tibetan Empire.

## Defeat in the west

In 751 a Tang army was defeated by Abbasid forces at the Talas river, in Central Asia. Tradition says that Chinese prisoners taught papermaking to the Arabs at Samarkand, though paper may have spread earlier. Soon after, the An Lushan rebellion forced the Tang to withdraw troops from Central Asia, and Tibet took much of the region.

## The end

A great peasant revolt led by Huang Chao in the 870s and 880s shattered what was left of the dynasty's authority. In 907 a former rebel general deposed the last Tang emperor, and China split into the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms.

## Influence

Tang China was a model for its neighbours. Japan sent embassies to study Tang institutions, and built its capitals at Nara and Kyoto on the plan of Chang'an. Korea's Silla and Vietnam also borrowed Tang government, writing and Buddhism. Chinese communities abroad still sometimes call themselves "people of Tang".
