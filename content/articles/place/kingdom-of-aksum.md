The Kingdom of Aksum ruled the highlands of today's Eritrea and northern Ethiopia from around the first century CE, and at its height controlled trade across the Red Sea. A third-century Persian religious teacher, Mani, counted it among the four great kingdoms of the world, alongside Rome, Persia and China.

## Origins

Before Aksum, the highlands of Eritrea and Tigray were home to D'mt, a kingdom of the first millennium BCE with strong links to South Arabia, known from inscriptions and the temple at Yeha. Aksum emerged as a kingdom by the first century CE. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek merchant's guide from around that time, describes Adulis as a port and names a king, Zoskales, as a ruler who knew Greek.

## Kings and stelae

Aksum's kings called themselves "king of kings" and recorded their deeds on stone inscriptions in Ge'ez, Greek and South Arabian. Their capital was marked by monumental stelae, tall granite monoliths carved to look like multi-storey buildings with false doors and windows, which marked royal tombs. The largest, now fallen, was about 33 metres high and weighed over 500 tonnes. Another, about 24 metres tall, was taken to Rome by Italian troops in 1937 and returned to Aksum in 2005.

## Coins and trade

From the late third century, Aksum issued its own coins in gold, silver and bronze, a sign of its importance in international trade; Aksumite coins have been found in Arabia and India. The kingdom exported ivory, rhinoceros horn, tortoiseshell, gold and spices, and imported cloth, glass, wine, olive oil and metal goods. Ge'ez, the language of Aksum, developed its own script, which later added vowel signs and is still used for Amharic and Tigrinya.

## Ezana and Christianity

According to tradition, a Syrian Christian named Frumentius, shipwrecked on the Red Sea coast as a boy, became tutor to the young king Ezana, and was consecrated in Alexandria as the first bishop of Aksum. Ezana's conversion around 330 is recorded on his coins and inscriptions, which shift from the old gods to the Christian God. Ezana also campaigned against Kush, on the Nile. In the late fifth century, missionaries known as the Nine Saints spread Christianity further and founded monasteries, such as Debre Damo.

## Wars across the Red Sea

In 525 King Kaleb invaded Himyar in Yemen, after its Jewish king persecuted Christians, and Aksumite rule there lasted until the Persians took over around 570. The Byzantine emperor Justinian sent embassies to Aksum, hoping to use it against Persia in the silk trade.

## Islam and decline

According to Islamic tradition, the early Muslims of Mecca, persecuted by the Quraysh, sought refuge with the Christian king of Aksum, the Najashi, around 615, and he protected them. Relations between Aksum and the early Muslims were therefore friendly. But the Arab conquests shifted trade routes, Aksum lost its access to the sea, and environmental degradation may have weakened its farmlands. The capital declined, though the town of Aksum remained a religious centre.

## Legacy

Aksum's Christian tradition, script and political ideas passed to later Ethiopian states, and Ethiopia's emperors claimed descent from its kings. The Church of St Mary of Zion in Aksum remains one of the holiest sites of Ethiopian Christianity, and the ruins of the city are a World Heritage Site.

## On our maps

Our maps show Aksum on every map from 1 BCE to 900 CE. It is largest on the 500 map, at about a million square kilometres, of which about half lies in today's Sudan. The basemap stretches its outline far west into the Nile valley; the kingdom's core was always the Tigray and Eritrean highlands, and its control of the lowlands was much looser.
