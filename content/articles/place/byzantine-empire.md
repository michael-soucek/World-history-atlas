The Byzantine Empire is the name historians give to the eastern half of the Roman Empire after the west fell. Its people never used it: they called themselves Romans and their state the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople, the city Constantine founded in 330 on the site of ancient Byzantium. It lasted over a thousand years, until 1453.

## Romans who spoke Greek

The eastern empire was Roman in law, government and identity, Greek in language and culture, and Christian in religion. Latin remained the official language until the seventh century, when Greek replaced it. The emperor was seen as God's representative on earth, and the Church, led by the patriarch of Constantinople, was closely tied to the state. Constantinople, with its land walls built under Theodosius II in the 410s and its harbours, was one of the best-defended cities in the world.

## Justinian and after

The empire's greatest reach came under Justinian I (527–565), whose generals reconquered North Africa, Italy and part of southern Spain. He also codified Roman law and built the church of Hagia Sophia. But plague, long wars with Persia and, from the 630s, the Arab conquests cost the empire Syria, Palestine, Egypt and North Africa within a few decades. Byzantium became a smaller, Greek-speaking state centred on Anatolia and the Balkans.

## The Arab conquests and survival

In the 630s and 640s the Arabs took Syria, Palestine and Egypt, and Arab fleets besieged Constantinople in 674–678 and 717–718. The city survived, helped by "Greek fire", an incendiary weapon sprayed from ships. The empire reorganised its remaining lands into military districts called themes, whose soldiers held land in return for service. In the Balkans, Slavs settled across much of the peninsula, and the Bulgars founded a state south of the Danube.

## Iconoclasm

From the 720s, emperors such as Leo III and Constantine V banned religious images, or icons, which they saw as idolatry. The resulting controversy, iconoclasm, divided the empire for over a century. Icons were restored in 787, banned again in 815, and finally restored in 843, a victory still celebrated in the Orthodox Church as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

## The Macedonian renaissance

Under the Macedonian dynasty, from 867 to 1056, Byzantium recovered. Its armies retook territory in Syria and Armenia, and Basil II, known as the Bulgar-slayer, conquered Bulgaria in 1018 after decades of war; tradition says he blinded thousands of prisoners after a victory in 1014. Scholars revived the study of classical Greek texts. The missionaries Cyril and Methodius, in the ninth century, created an alphabet for Slavic languages, the basis of Cyrillic. In 988 Vladimir of Kyiv converted, bringing the Rus' into the Orthodox world.

## The schism

The churches of Rome and Constantinople had drifted apart over language, authority and doctrine, including the papal claim to supremacy and the wording of the creed. In 1054 the papal legate and the patriarch excommunicated each other, a break often called the Great Schism.

## Manzikert and the Crusades

The defeat by the Seljuks at Manzikert in 1071 and civil wars that followed cost Byzantium most of Anatolia. Alexios I Komnenos, emperor from 1081, restored the empire with western help; his daughter Anna Komnene wrote the Alexiad, a history of his reign. The Komnenian emperors regained parts of Anatolia, but in 1176 Manuel I was defeated by the Seljuks at Myriokephalon.

## 1204 and after

In 1204 the Fourth Crusade, diverted by Venetian interests and a Byzantine claimant, sacked Constantinople and set up a Latin Empire. Byzantine successor states survived at Nicaea, Trebizond and Epirus, and Nicaea retook Constantinople in 1261 under Michael VIII Palaiologos. The restored empire was weak, fought civil wars, and lost Anatolia to the Ottomans and the Balkans to the Serbs and Ottomans. By the 1400s it paid tribute to the Ottomans.

## The fall

On 29 May 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople after a siege of nearly two months. The last emperor, Constantine XI, died defending the walls. On our 1492 map the city is Ottoman.

## Legacy

Byzantium preserved Roman law, most of the surviving ancient Greek literature, and a rich tradition of art: mosaics, icons and domed churches. Its Orthodox Christianity shaped Greece, Bulgaria, Serbia, Romania, Ukraine, Belarus and Russia. Greek scholars who fled to Italy before and after 1453 helped inspire the Renaissance.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on every map from 400 to 1400. It is largest on the 600 map at about 2.7 million square kilometres, still holding Egypt, the Levant, North Africa and Italian territory from Justinian's conquests; Turkey makes up about a quarter of that area and Egypt a fifth. After 700 Jerusalem, Alexandria and Damascus are gone from it on our maps, and by 1400 it is little more than Constantinople and parts of Greece.
