Babylonia was the ancient state of southern Mesopotamia, named after its capital, Babylon, on the Euphrates south of modern Baghdad. For much of two thousand years Babylon was the cultural heart of the Mesopotamian world, and its name still evokes splendour, learning and, in the Bible, pride.

## The Old Babylonian period

Babylon, a small town in the third millennium, became a capital under the Amorite dynasty founded around 1894 BCE. Hammurabi's conquests made it the centre of southern Mesopotamia. The Old Babylonian period was a golden age of Akkadian literature: the Epic of Gilgamesh was assembled from earlier Sumerian stories, and scribes copied and studied Sumerian texts. Mathematical tablets show advanced knowledge, including a value for the square root of two accurate to six decimal places and tables of what we call Pythagorean triples, such as the tablet Plimpton 322.

## Hammurabi

Babylon was a small city until an Amorite dynasty made it a power in the early second millennium BCE. Its most famous king, Hammurabi (around 1792–1750 BCE), united southern Mesopotamia through war and diplomacy. His law code, carved on a stone stele, is one of the best-known legal texts of the ancient world, with nearly three hundred laws covering trade, family, property and punishment. Later, the Kassites ruled Babylonia for over four centuries, a stable period in which Babylonian became the language of international diplomacy across the Near East.

## The Kassites

After the Hittite sack of Babylon in 1595 BCE, the Kassites, a people from the Zagros mountains, took power and ruled Babylonia for over four centuries, longer than any other dynasty. They adopted Babylonian culture and religion, and Babylon became a centre of international diplomacy: Kassite kings corresponded with the pharaohs, as the Amarna letters show, and Akkadian was the language of diplomacy. The Kassites set up boundary stones, kudurrus, recording land grants and decorated with symbols of the gods.

## Marduk

Babylon's god Marduk rose to become the king of the gods. The Enuma Elish, the Babylonian creation epic, tells how Marduk defeated the sea monster Tiamat and created the world from her body, and was proclaimed king by the gods. It was recited at the New Year festival, the Akitu, when the king took the hand of Marduk's statue to confirm his rule. When enemies carried off the statue, as the Hittites, Assyrians and Elamites did, it was a disaster.

## Under Assyria

From the ninth century BCE, Assyria dominated Babylonia, sometimes ruling it directly. Babylonians, along with Chaldean tribes of the south, such as Merodach-Baladan, repeatedly rebelled. Sennacherib destroyed Babylon in 689 BCE, flooding the city, but his son Esarhaddon rebuilt it. Ashurbanipal's brother Shamash-shum-ukin, king of Babylon, rebelled against him and died in 648 BCE when Babylon fell after a siege.

## The Neo-Babylonian Empire

Nabopolassar, a Chaldean, freed Babylon from Assyria in 626 BCE and, with the Medes, destroyed the Assyrian Empire. His son Nebuchadnezzar II built an empire from the Persian Gulf to Egypt's border and made Babylon the largest city in the world, with walls, the Ishtar Gate, the Processional Way, palaces and the ziggurat Etemenanki. The last king, Nabonidus, devoted himself to the moon god Sin and spent ten years at the oasis of Tayma in Arabia, leaving his son Belshazzar in charge, which angered Marduk's priests.

## Astronomy

Babylonian astronomers kept astronomical diaries recording the positions of the moon and planets, eclipses and weather for centuries, from about 750 BCE to the first century BCE. They developed mathematical methods to predict planetary positions and eclipses. The zodiac of twelve signs, the division of the circle into 360 degrees and the sexagesimal system we use for time all come from Babylonian traditions.

## Persian and later Babylon

Cyrus the Great took Babylon in 539 BCE. Alexander the Great planned to make it his capital and died there in 323 BCE. Under the Seleucids, the new city of Seleucia on the Tigris replaced it, and Babylon slowly declined. Its ruins near Hillah, in Iraq, became a World Heritage Site in 2019.

## On our maps

Our maps show Babylonia on the 1500, 1000 and 700 BCE maps. Its outline, at most about 130,000 square kilometres, is drawn half in today's Iraq and half in Iran, which places more of it east of the Tigris than most historical maps would. Baghdad's site lies inside it on all three. On the 500 BCE map it is part of the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire).
