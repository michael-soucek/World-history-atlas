The German Empire was created on 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, at the end of the Franco-Prussian War. King William I of Prussia was proclaimed German emperor, and the states of Germany, except Austria, were united in a single state for the first time. It lasted until the revolution of November 1918.

## The constitution

The German Empire was a federation of 25 states: the kingdoms of Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony and Württemberg, grand duchies, duchies, principalities and the free cities of Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck, plus the imperial territory of Alsace-Lorraine. The Bundesrat, representing the states' governments, and the Reichstag, elected by all men over 25, made laws. But the emperor appointed the chancellor and controlled the army and foreign policy. Prussia, with two-thirds of the population, had a three-class voting system that gave the wealthy most power.

## The Kulturkampf and socialism

In the 1870s Bismarck fought the Catholic Church in the Kulturkampf, "culture struggle", expelling the Jesuits, introducing civil marriage and imprisoning bishops. The Catholic Centre Party grew stronger, and Bismarck eventually abandoned the campaign. He then turned against the Social Democratic Party, banning socialist organisations from 1878 to 1890, while introducing health insurance, accident insurance and pensions to win over workers. Despite the ban, the Social Democrats became the largest party in the Reichstag by 1912.

## An industrial giant

Germany industrialised rapidly. By 1914 it produced more steel than Britain, led the world in the chemical and electrical industries, with companies such as Bayer, BASF, Siemens and AEG, and had a dense railway network. German universities and research institutes led in science: Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895, Robert Koch identified the bacteria causing tuberculosis and cholera, and Max Planck founded quantum theory. Karl Benz and Gottlieb Daimler built early cars. Germany's population grew from 41 million in 1871 to 65 million in 1910.

## Colonies

From 1884, Germany acquired colonies: German South West Africa, now Namibia; Cameroon; Togo; German East Africa, now mainland Tanzania, Rwanda and Burundi; New Guinea and Pacific islands; and Qingdao in China. Colonial rule was often brutal. The Herero and Nama genocide of 1904–1908 in South West Africa, and the suppression of the Maji Maji rebellion in East Africa in 1905–1907, which killed tens of thousands, are remembered as crimes.

## Wilhelm II and world policy

Wilhelm II, emperor from 1888, wanted Germany to be a world power. He dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and let the alliance with Russia lapse, pushing Russia towards France. Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz built a battle fleet to challenge Britain, starting a naval arms race. Germany provoked crises over Morocco in 1905 and 1911. Its alliances with Austria-Hungary and Italy faced the Triple Entente of France, Russia and Britain.

## War and revolution

In July 1914, Germany gave Austria-Hungary unconditional support against Serbia and declared war on Russia and France, invading Belgium. The war turned into a stalemate. The Allied blockade caused severe food shortages in Germany. In 1918, after the failure of the spring offensive, the army's leaders, Hindenburg and Ludendorff, told the emperor the war was lost. Sailors mutinied at Kiel, revolution spread, and on 9 November the Social Democrat Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a republic. Wilhelm went into exile in the Netherlands, where he died in 1941.

## Legacy

The Treaty of Versailles took territory from Germany, including Alsace-Lorraine and lands to Poland, and all its colonies. The myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back" by civilians at home poisoned the politics of the Weimar Republic that followed.

## On our maps

Our maps show the German Empire from 1878 to 1914. Its largest outline, about 1.5 million square kilometres on the 1914 map, is mostly in Africa, because the basemap includes German East Africa, now mainland Tanzania, under the same name. In Europe, its territory stretched from Strasbourg, then German, to Kaliningrad, then Königsberg, and included Gdańsk and Wrocław, now in Poland.
