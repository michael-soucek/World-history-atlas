The Tokugawa shogunate governed Japan from 1603 to 1868, a period known as the Edo period after its capital, Edo, today's Tokyo. After a century and a half of civil war, it gave Japan more than 250 years of internal peace, during which the country largely closed itself to the outside world.

## Unification

Tokugawa Ieyasu was the last of the three great unifiers of Japan, after Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi. After Hideyoshi's death, Ieyasu defeated his rivals at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, and in 1603 the emperor appointed him shogun. He destroyed the remaining Toyotomi family at the sieges of Osaka in 1614–1615. He and his son Hidetada and grandson Iemitsu built the shogunate's institutions.

## The bakuhan system

The shogunate governed through about 250 to 300 regional lords, the daimyo, each ruling a domain, han, with their own samurai, while the shogun ruled about a quarter of Japan directly, including the major cities and mines. Daimyo were classified by their loyalty: the shogun's relatives, the fudai, long-time allies, and the tozama, "outside lords", who had submitted after Sekigahara and were kept away from power. Under the system of alternate attendance, sankin-kōtai, each daimyo spent every other year in Edo with a large retinue, and his wife and heir lived there permanently, as hostages. The cost of these processions and residences drained the daimyo's wealth and helped develop roads, such as the Tōkaidō.

## A hierarchical society

Society was divided into four ranks: samurai, farmers, craftsmen and merchants, with outcast groups below. Samurai, about 6 percent of the population, received stipends in rice and were expected to live in castle towns. During the long peace, many became administrators, scholars and teachers. Neo-Confucianism became the official ideology. Peasants paid heavy taxes in rice, and peasant uprisings, ikki, were frequent, especially in times of famine.

## Closing the country

The shogunate saw Christianity, which had spread in the sixteenth century, as a threat. It banned it in 1614, persecuted Christians and executed many. After the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638, a revolt of Christian peasants, the shogunate expelled the Portuguese and restricted foreign contact, a policy later called sakoku, "closed country". The Dutch were allowed to trade at Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbour, along with Chinese merchants, and Japan traded with Korea through Tsushima and with the Ryukyu kingdom. Japanese were forbidden to travel abroad. Through Dutch books, some Japanese scholars studied Western medicine and science, rangaku, "Dutch learning".

## Edo culture

Peace brought economic growth. Edo grew into one of the largest cities in the world, with perhaps a million people in the eighteenth century, and Osaka became a commercial centre. Merchants prospered, and the urban "floating world", ukiyo, produced kabuki theatre, bunraku puppet plays, the plays of Chikamatsu Monzaemon, the haiku of Matsuo Bashō, the novels of Ihara Saikaku and the woodblock prints, ukiyo-e, of artists such as Hokusai, whose Great Wave was made around 1831, and Hiroshige. Literacy spread through temple schools.

## Crisis and fall

In the nineteenth century, the shogunate faced financial problems, famines, such as the Tenpō famine of the 1830s, and foreign ships. In 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry arrived with American warships, and in 1854 the shogunate signed the Treaty of Kanagawa, opening ports. Unequal treaties with Western powers followed. Opponents rallied around the emperor, and domains such as Satsuma and Chōshū turned against the shogunate. In 1867 the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, resigned, and after the Boshin War of 1868–1869, the Meiji Restoration ended the shogunate.
