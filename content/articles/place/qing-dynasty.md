The Qing was the last imperial dynasty of China, ruling from 1644 to 1912. Its rulers were not Han Chinese but Manchus, a people from the north-east, and under them China reached its greatest size, taking in Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet and Taiwan.

## The Manchus

The Manchus were descendants of the Jurchens, who had ruled northern China as the Jin dynasty. Nurhaci unified the Jurchen tribes in the late sixteenth century, organised them into military units called banners, and in 1616 founded the Later Jin. His son Hong Taiji renamed the people Manchus and the dynasty Qing in 1636, and incorporated Mongol and Chinese banners.

## The conquest

In 1644 the rebel Li Zicheng took Beijing, and the last Ming emperor took his own life. The Ming general Wu Sangui, guarding the Shanhai pass, allowed the Qing army into China to fight Li. The Qing took Beijing and, over four decades, conquered the south, often brutally; at Yangzhou in 1645, Qing forces massacred much of the population. Ming loyalists, such as Koxinga, who took Taiwan from the Dutch in 1662, resisted. The Kangxi emperor crushed the Revolt of the Three Feudatories in 1681 and took Taiwan in 1683.

## Governing China

The Qing kept much of the Ming system of government, using the examinations and Chinese officials, while Manchus held key posts, often in pairs with Chinese officials. Manchu bannermen were stationed in garrisons across the empire. The queue hairstyle was required of Han men. The Yongzheng emperor created a Grand Council to make decisions quickly. Qing emperors sponsored huge scholarly projects, such as the Kangxi Dictionary and the Complete Library of the Four Treasuries, but also censored and destroyed works considered anti-Manchu.

## A multi-ethnic empire

The Qing ruled a vast empire of different peoples. In China, the emperors presented themselves as Confucian sons of heaven; to Mongols, they were khans; to Tibetans, patrons of Buddhism and incarnations of the bodhisattva Manjushri. The Qianlong emperor completed the conquest of the Dzungar Khanate in the 1750s and made Xinjiang part of the empire. Tibet came under Qing protection, and Qing officials, the ambans, were stationed in Lhasa.

## The high Qing

In the eighteenth century, the Qing economy prospered, and the population grew from about 150 million to over 300 million, helped by new crops from the Americas. Chinese tea, silk and porcelain were in great demand in Europe, and foreign trade was restricted to Guangzhou under the Canton system. In 1793 the British envoy Lord Macartney visited the Qianlong emperor, who wrote to King George III that China had no need of British goods. The novel Dream of the Red Chamber was written in this period.

## Crisis

From the late eighteenth century, problems grew: population pressure, corruption, the White Lotus Rebellion of 1796–1804, and the opium trade. The Opium Wars forced China to open ports and accept unequal treaties. The Taiping Rebellion killed tens of millions. The Self-Strengthening Movement built arsenals and shipyards, but China was defeated by France in 1884–1885 and by Japan in 1894–1895, losing Taiwan.

## The last years

After the Boxer Uprising of 1900, the Qing introduced reforms: the examinations were abolished in 1905, and provincial assemblies were created. But revolutionaries, led by Sun Yat-sen, called for the overthrow of the Manchus. After the Wuchang Uprising of 1911, the six-year-old Puyi abdicated in 1912. Puyi later became the puppet emperor of Japanese-controlled Manchukuo, and ended his life as an ordinary citizen of the People's Republic.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Qing from 1650 to 1900. It is largest on the 1800 map at about 12 million square kilometres: 75 percent of it in today's China, 13 percent in Mongolia and most of the rest in Russia's Far East, which the Qing held until it was ceded to Russia in 1858–1860. Beijing, Shanghai and Nanjing are Qing on our maps throughout; Lhasa from 1783 and Kashgar from 1815.
