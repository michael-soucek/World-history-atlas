Majapahit was a Javanese kingdom that ruled from eastern Java between 1293 and the early sixteenth century and is remembered in Indonesia as the greatest of its pre-colonial states. At its height in the fourteenth century it claimed overlordship over much of what is now Indonesia and parts of the Malay peninsula.

## Raden Wijaya

Raden Wijaya was the son-in-law of Kertanegara, the last king of Singhasari, a Javanese kingdom that had expanded its influence over Sumatra and Bali. When Kertanegara was killed in 1292 by Jayakatwang, a rebel vassal from Kediri, Wijaya fled and opened land near the Brantas river, at a place called Majapahit, after the bitter maja fruit found there. He used the arrival of a Mongol fleet sent by Kublai Khan to defeat Jayakatwang, and then attacked the Mongols, who withdrew. He was crowned in 1293 as Kertarajasa Jayawardhana.

## Gajah Mada

Gajah Mada rose from the royal guard to become chief minister, mahapatih, in 1334, under Queen Tribhuwana Wijayatunggadewi. According to the Pararaton, a Javanese chronicle, he swore the Palapa oath, vowing not to taste palapa, perhaps a spice, until he had united the archipelago. He led campaigns against Bali and other islands. In 1357, the Bubat incident, in which the Sundanese royal party, invited to a royal wedding, was killed in a clash with Majapahit troops, damaged his reputation. He died in 1364.

## The Nagarakretagama

The court poet Mpu Prapanca wrote the Nagarakretagama in 1365, an Old Javanese poem praising King Hayam Wuruk. It describes the capital, the king's royal tours, ceremonies, religious foundations, and a list of tributary lands across Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, the Malay peninsula and eastern islands. Historians treat it as a statement of Majapahit's claims rather than a precise map of its rule; many places on the list probably had trade and diplomatic relations rather than direct rule.

## The capital at Trowulan

Archaeologists have explored the site of the capital at Trowulan, in East Java, uncovering brick foundations, gates such as the Bajang Ratu and Wringin Lawang, canals, reservoirs, terracotta figurines, Chinese ceramics and coins. The city was a large, densely populated centre with royal compounds, temples and markets.

## Religion and culture

Majapahit's court followed both Shaivite Hinduism and Buddhism, often blended, with kings honoured as incarnations of gods. Temples, candi, built of brick, and literature in Old Javanese, such as the kakawin poems, flourished. The poem Sutasoma, written in the fourteenth century, contains the phrase bhinneka tunggal ika, "unity in diversity", adopted as Indonesia's national motto.

## Trade

Majapahit's economy combined rice farming in the fertile valleys of East Java with control of the maritime trade in spices, sandalwood and other goods between the eastern islands, Malacca, China and India. Chinese copper coins were widely used as currency. Ming envoys, including Zheng He's fleets, visited Java.

## Decline

After Hayam Wuruk's death in 1389, a civil war, the Paregreg war, broke out between rival branches of the royal family in 1404–1406. Islam spread through the trading ports of Java's north coast, which grew independent. In the early sixteenth century, the Sultanate of Demak became the dominant power, and Majapahit disappeared around 1527. Many Hindu nobles, priests and artists went to Bali, which preserved a Hindu-Javanese culture.
