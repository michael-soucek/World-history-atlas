The Sokoto Caliphate was one of the largest states in nineteenth-century Africa. Founded through an Islamic reform movement in 1804, it united dozens of Hausa and other emirates in what is now northern Nigeria and parts of Niger, Cameroon and Burkina Faso, under a caliph based at Sokoto, until the British conquered it in 1903.

## The Hausa kingdoms

The Hausa people lived in city-states, such as Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Gobir and Zamfara, walled cities that were centres of trade and crafts, especially leather and dyed cloth, linked to the trans-Saharan trade. Islam had spread among their rulers from the fourteenth century, but many people followed traditional religion, and Muslim scholars criticised the rulers for mixing Islam with other practices and imposing illegal taxes.

## Usman dan Fodio

Usman dan Fodio, born in 1754 in Gobir, was a Fulani Muslim scholar, teacher and preacher of the Qadiriyya Sufi order. From the 1770s he travelled and preached, attracting many followers, Fulani and Hausa, with his call for reform, justice and strict observance of Islamic law, and criticised the Hausa rulers. The king of Gobir, Yunfa, a former student, turned against him. In 1804 Usman and his followers withdrew to Gudu, an act compared to the Prophet's Hijra, and he was declared Commander of the Faithful. He declared jihad.

## The jihad

Usman's followers, many of them Fulani herders, together with Hausa peasants, defeated the Hausa kings one by one: they won at Tabkin Kwatto in 1804, took Kano, Katsina and Zaria by 1808, and Gobir's capital, Alkalawa, in 1808, killing Yunfa. Usman issued flags to leaders who founded emirates in other areas, such as Adamawa, Bauchi, Gombe and Ilorin. The jihad also reached Bornu, which resisted under al-Kanemi.

## A federation of emirates

After the jihad, Usman retired to scholarship. His son Muhammad Bello became caliph at Sokoto in 1817, founded as the capital in 1809, and his brother Abdullahi governed the western emirates from Gwandu. The caliphate was a federation of about thirty emirates, each ruled by an emir who swore allegiance to the caliph, sent tribute and contributed to campaigns. Emirs, scholars and judges, alkalis, enforced Islamic law.

## Scholarship

Usman, Abdullahi and Bello wrote hundreds of works in Arabic, Fulfulde and Hausa on religion, law, politics, history and medicine. Usman's daughter Nana Asmau, a poet and teacher, wrote in Arabic, Fulfulde and Hausa and created the Yan Taru movement, which trained women teachers to educate rural women. Literacy and Islamic learning spread.

## Economy

The caliphate's population, perhaps ten million or more, was one of the largest in Africa. Kano, its commercial centre, produced dyed cloth, especially indigo, and leather goods exported across West Africa and the Sahara; the German explorer Heinrich Barth described its markets in the 1850s. The economy depended heavily on enslaved labour on plantations, and raids on non-Muslim peoples supplied slaves. By the late nineteenth century, the caliphate had one of the largest slave populations in the world.

## British conquest

In the 1890s the British Royal Niger Company, and from 1900 the British government, moved into northern Nigeria. Frederick Lugard led forces that took Kano and Sokoto in 1903. The caliph Attahiru I fled east with followers and was killed at Burmi. The British ruled through the emirs, a system called indirect rule, which preserved the caliphate's structure. The Sultan of Sokoto remains the spiritual leader of Nigeria's Muslims.
