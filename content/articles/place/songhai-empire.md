The Songhai Empire was the last and largest of the great Sahel empires of West Africa. In the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries it controlled the middle Niger river and the trade cities of Timbuktu, Djenné and its capital, Gao. A Songhai kingdom had existed around Gao for centuries, and was for a time subject to Mali, before it built an empire of its own.

## Gao before the empire

Gao, on the Niger river, was an important trading town by the ninth century, linked to North Africa by caravan routes. Its rulers converted to Islam around 1000, and royal tombstones from the twelfth century, some made of Spanish marble, have been found nearby. In the late thirteenth century Gao became part of the Mali Empire, but the Songhai regained independence under the Sunni or Sonni dynasty in the fourteenth century.

## Sonni Ali

Sonni Ali, who ruled from about 1464 to 1492, was a brilliant general who used a river fleet on the Niger and a cavalry army to build an empire. He took Timbuktu from the Tuareg in 1468, and Djenné after a long siege. He defeated the Mossi and the Fulani. Muslim chroniclers of Timbuktu, whose scholars he persecuted, portrayed him as a cruel tyrant who mixed Islam with traditional religion; in Songhai oral tradition, he is remembered as a powerful magician-king.

## Askia Muhammad

After Sonni Ali's death in 1492, his general Muhammad Touré overthrew his son and founded the Askia dynasty. Askia Muhammad, a devout Muslim, made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1496–1497 with a large retinue and was named caliph of West Africa by the sharif of Mecca. He extended the empire from the Atlantic to the Hausa cities, appointed governors and ministers for the treasury, army, navy and agriculture, standardised weights and measures, and supported Islamic scholars. In his old age he was deposed by his sons in 1528.

## Society and economy

The Songhai Empire's wealth came from the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, slaves, cloth and books, and from agriculture along the Niger, where enslaved and dependent people farmed rice for the state. Timbuktu had a population of tens of thousands, with scholars such as Ahmad Baba, who wrote over forty books. The Tarikh al-Sudan and Tarikh al-Fattash, chronicles written in Timbuktu in the seventeenth century, are the main sources for Songhai history.

## The Moroccan invasion

The sultan of Morocco, Ahmad al-Mansur, wanted control of the gold trade and the salt mines of Taghaza, which Songhai claimed. In 1590 he sent an army of about 4,000 men, many of them Spanish converts and mercenaries armed with muskets, under the command of Judar Pasha, across the Sahara. At Tondibi in 1591, they defeated the much larger army of Askia Ishaq II, whose soldiers had spears and bows; a stampede of cattle driven at the Moroccans failed.

## Aftermath

The Moroccans took Gao and Timbuktu, deporting scholars, including Ahmad Baba, to Marrakesh. But they could not control the vast empire, and Songhai resistance continued in Dendi, in the south-east. The Moroccan soldiers in Timbuktu became a local ruling class, the Arma, and the region broke up into smaller states. Trade shifted increasingly to the Atlantic coast.

It is largest on the 1530 map at about 720,000 square kilometres, almost 80 percent of it in today's Mali, with the rest in Niger, Burkina Faso and Mauritania.
