The Inca Empire was the largest state in the Americas before the arrival of Europeans. Its people called it Tawantinsuyu, "the four parts together", after the four quarters into which it was divided. From its capital at Cusco, high in the Andes of today's Peru, it ruled perhaps ten million people along some 4,000 kilometres of mountains and coast.

## Origins

Inca legends say that the founder, Manco Capac, and his sister-wife emerged from Lake Titicaca or from a cave at Pacaritambo and founded Cusco. Archaeologists date the beginnings of the Inca kingdom around Cusco to the thirteenth century, after the decline of the Wari and Tiwanaku states. For two centuries, the Incas were one of many small groups competing in the southern highlands.

## Rulers

Pachacuti, who took power around 1438, transformed the kingdom into an empire. His son Topa Inca Yupanqui, who ruled from 1471 to 1493, expanded it north to Quito and south into Chile, and conquered the Chimú kingdom on the coast. Huayna Capac (1493–1527) extended the empire north into Colombia and spent much of his reign in Quito. The ruler, the Sapa Inca, "unique Inca", was considered the son of the sun, and married his sister, the coya.

## Tawantinsuyu

The empire was divided into four quarters, suyus, meeting at Cusco: Chinchaysuyu to the north-west, Antisuyu to the north-east, Collasuyu to the south-east and Cuntisuyu to the south-west. Provinces were governed by Inca officials and local lords, kurakas. Households were counted in a decimal system for labour service. Quechua was spread as the administrative language. Conquered groups were sometimes moved, as mitmaqkuna, to secure provinces and spread Inca customs.

## Labour and storage

Instead of taxes in money or goods, the Inca state required labour. Households worked on state and temple lands, built roads and terraces, served in the army, and produced cloth, especially the fine cumbi textiles valued more than gold. The state stored food, cloth and weapons in thousands of storehouses, qollqa, along the roads. Chosen women, aqllakuna, lived in special houses and wove fine cloth and brewed chicha beer for state festivals.

## The road network

The Qhapaq Ñan, the Inca road system, ran about 40,000 kilometres, with two main routes along the coast and through the highlands, connected by cross roads. Rest houses, tambos, were placed along the routes. Relay runners, chasquis, carried messages and khipus quickly, covering about 240 kilometres a day. Suspension bridges made of woven grass crossed deep gorges; one, at Q'eswachaka, is still rebuilt every year by local communities. The road network is a World Heritage Site.

## Religion

The Inca state religion centred on the sun god, Inti, worshipped at the Coricancha in Cusco, whose walls were covered in gold. Other gods included the creator Viracocha, the moon goddess Mama Quilla and the earth mother Pachamama. Mummies of dead rulers were kept and consulted, and huacas, sacred places and objects, were honoured across the empire. In the capacocha ceremony, children were sacrificed on high peaks; mummies found on Llullaillaco in Argentina in 1999 are among the best preserved in the world.

## The conquest

Huayna Capac died in the 1520s, probably of smallpox, which had spread ahead of the Spanish. His sons Huáscar and Atahualpa fought a civil war. Atahualpa had just won when Francisco Pizarro's small Spanish force captured him at Cajamarca in 1532, took a room full of gold and silver as ransom, and executed him in 1533. Inca resistance continued from Vilcabamba until 1572, when the last Inca ruler, Túpac Amaru, was captured and executed.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Inca Empire on the 1492, 1500 and 1530 maps. It is largest on the 1500 map, at about 1.55 million square kilometres: Peru has about 40 percent, Chile a quarter, and Bolivia, Argentina and Ecuador the rest. Cusco, Lima's site and La Paz's are Inca on our maps, with Quito's added by 1530. Before it, the coast belongs to the Chimú; on the 1600 map the region is the Viceroyalty of Peru.
