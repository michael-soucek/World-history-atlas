The Habsburg Monarchy was not a single country but a collection of crowns and lands ruled by the House of Habsburg from Vienna. At various times it included Austria, Bohemia, Hungary, Croatia, parts of Italy, the southern Netherlands and Galicia, each with its own laws and institutions, held together by the dynasty.

## The rise of the Habsburgs

The Habsburgs took their name from Habsburg Castle in today's Switzerland. In 1273 Rudolf of Habsburg was elected king of Germany, and in 1278 he defeated King Ottokar II of Bohemia and took Austria. The family's motto was said to be "Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry". Maximilian I married Mary of Burgundy, gaining the Netherlands; his son Philip married Joanna of Castile, heiress of Spain; and his grandson Ferdinand married Anna of Hungary and Bohemia.

## The Austrian and Spanish branches

When Charles V abdicated in 1556, the family split into a Spanish branch, ruling Spain, its empire, the Netherlands and Italian lands, and an Austrian branch, ruling Austria, Bohemia, Hungary and the imperial title. The two branches cooperated closely and intermarried, leading to genetic problems; the last Spanish Habsburg, Charles II, died childless in 1700.

## Turks and Protestants

For two centuries, the Austrian Habsburgs fought the Ottomans in Hungary. Vienna was besieged in 1529 and 1683; after the second siege was relieved, Habsburg armies reconquered Hungary, confirmed by the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699. The Habsburgs also led the Catholic Counter-Reformation in their lands, crushing the Bohemian Protestants at the White Mountain in 1620 and executing their leaders in Prague in 1621.

## Maria Theresa

When Charles VI died in 1740 without a son, his daughter Maria Theresa inherited his lands under the Pragmatic Sanction, but Prussia, Bavaria and France attacked. She lost Silesia but kept the rest. She reformed the army and administration, introduced compulsory primary education in 1774 and limited the labour obligations of peasants. She had sixteen children, including Joseph II and Marie Antoinette, queen of France.

## Joseph II

Joseph II, sole ruler from 1780 to 1790, introduced radical reforms: he abolished serfdom, granted religious toleration to Protestants, Orthodox Christians and Jews, closed many monasteries, made German the language of administration and reorganised the legal system. Many reforms provoked resistance, especially in Hungary and the Austrian Netherlands, and some were reversed after his death.

## The Austrian Empire

During the Napoleonic Wars, Francis II proclaimed the Austrian Empire in 1804 and dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. After Napoleon's defeat, Vienna hosted the Congress of 1814–1815, and the Austrian chancellor Klemens von Metternich dominated European politics, suppressing liberal and nationalist movements, until the revolutions of 1848 forced him out. Franz Joseph, who came to the throne in 1848 and reigned until 1916, crushed the Hungarian revolution with Russian help.

## Vienna

Under the Habsburgs, Vienna became one of Europe's great capitals, with the Hofburg and Schönbrunn palaces, and a centre of music, home to Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert and later Brahms and the Strauss family. The Habsburg lands were home to many peoples and languages, a diversity that became the monarchy's central problem in the age of nationalism.

## The end

Defeat in the First World War broke the monarchy apart in 1918 into Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and other states. Emperor Charles I withdrew from government in November 1918 and went into exile.

## On our maps

Our maps use the name from 1650 to 1815. Its largest outline, about 675,000 square kilometres on the 1715 map, is spread across a dozen modern countries, with the largest shares in Italy, Hungary, Romania, the Czech Republic and Austria. Vienna, Prague, Budapest, Bratislava, Zagreb and Ljubljana are Habsburg on our maps throughout this period, Milan for most of it and Brussels until 1800. On the 1878 map the realm appears as Austria-Hungary.
