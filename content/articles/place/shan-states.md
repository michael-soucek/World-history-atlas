The Shan States were a group of principalities in the hills of what is now eastern Myanmar, ruled by the Shan, a Tai-speaking people related to the Thai and Lao. Each state, called a möng, was governed by a hereditary prince with the title saopha, "lord of the sky", often written sawbwa in Burmese. Some were tiny; the largest controlled sizeable valleys and towns.

## The Shan people

The Shan, who call themselves Tai, are related to the Thai of Thailand, the Lao and the Dai of Yunnan in China. Their language belongs to the Tai-Kadai family. Shan communities live mainly in the valleys of the Shan plateau, an upland region of eastern Myanmar crossed by the Salween river, growing wet rice in irrigated fields, while other peoples, such as the Palaung, Lahu, Wa and Akha, live in the surrounding hills.

## The saophas

Each Shan state was ruled by a saopha, a hereditary prince, who lived in a haw, a palace, and governed with officials and village headmen. Saophas collected taxes, judged disputes and led troops. They often married into each other's families and into the Burmese and Thai royal families. The largest states, such as Hsenwi, Hsipaw, Kengtung, Yawnghwe and Mong Nai, controlled important trade routes between China and the lowlands.

## Shan power in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries

After the fall of Pagan in the late thirteenth century, Shan lords became powerful in upper Burma. Three Shan brothers, who had helped resist the Mongols, controlled central Burma in the early fourteenth century, and the Ava kingdom, founded in 1364, was ruled by Shan-descended kings for long periods. The Shan state of Mong Mao, in today's Yunnan, was powerful in the fourteenth century until the Ming defeated it. In 1527 the Confederation of Shan States, led by Mong Yang, took Ava and ruled it until 1555.

## Under the Burmese kings

The Toungoo king Bayinnaung conquered the Shan states in the 1550s and made them tributaries. Burmese kings required saophas to send sons to the royal court as pages, where they learned Burmese ways, and daughters as royal wives. The Shan states supplied troops and paid tribute, while keeping internal autonomy. Theravada Buddhism, shared with the Burmese, was central to Shan culture, and Shan monasteries preserved manuscripts in the Shan script.

## British rule

After the British annexed upper Burma in 1885, they brought the Shan states under indirect rule, recognising the saophas, who signed agreements with the British. In 1922 the British created the Federated Shan States, with a council of chiefs. The Shan states were governed separately from Burma proper.

## After independence

At the Panglong Conference in 1947, Shan leaders agreed to join an independent Burma, with the promise of autonomy and a possible right to leave after ten years. Sao Shwe Thaik, the saopha of Yawnghwe, became the first president of Burma in 1948. After the military coup of 1962, the saophas were arrested; Sao Shwe Thaik died in prison. Shan nationalist armies have fought the central government since the late 1950s, and the region, part of the Golden Triangle, became notorious for opium production. Conflicts continue today.
