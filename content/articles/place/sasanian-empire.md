The Sasanian Empire was the last great Iranian empire before the coming of Islam. Its founder, Ardashir I, a local ruler from Persis in south-western Iran, defeated the last Parthian king in 224 CE. His dynasty, named after an ancestor called Sasan, ruled for over four hundred years and called its realm Eranshahr, the land of the Iranians.

## Ardashir and Shapur

Ardashir I presented himself as the restorer of Iranian greatness after the Parthians, claiming descent from the ancient kings and promoting the Zoroastrian religion. His son Shapur I (240–270) fought Rome with great success, defeating Gordian III, forcing Philip the Arab to pay a ransom, and capturing Valerian at Edessa in 260. His trilingual inscription at the Kaaba of Zoroaster, at Naqsh-e Rustam, lists his victories and the lands he ruled, calling him "king of kings of Iran and non-Iran". Roman prisoners were settled in Iran and helped build dams and bridges, such as the Band-e Kaisar at Shushtar.

## State and society

Sasanian society was divided into estates: priests, warriors, scribes and commoners. Great noble families, some of Parthian origin, held enormous power, and the king's authority depended on balancing them. The army's core was heavily armoured cavalry, the clibanarii, supported by elephants and infantry. Provinces were run by governors and royal officials, and the bureaucracy kept detailed records in Middle Persian.

## Zoroastrian church

Under the high priest Kartir, in the late third century, the Zoroastrian priesthood became powerful, building fire temples across the empire and at times persecuting Christians, Jews, Manichaeans and Buddhists. Mani, the founder of Manichaeism, was imprisoned and died around 274 or 277. Christians in Persia, especially after Rome adopted Christianity, were sometimes suspected of loyalty to the enemy and faced persecution, though the Church of the East also flourished under Sasanian rule.

## Mazdak and Khosrow I

In the late fifth century, a religious reformer named Mazdak preached a movement of social equality and shared property, which King Kavad I at first supported. His son Khosrow I Anushirvan, "of the immortal soul", crushed the Mazdakites, reformed land taxes on a fixed basis, created a standing army paid by the state and divided the empire into four military regions. Khosrow welcomed Greek philosophers expelled from Athens, had Indian works such as the Panchatantra translated, and built the great palace at Ctesiphon whose vaulted arch still stands.

## Culture

Sasanian culture was rich and influential. Silver plates showing royal hunting scenes, textiles with roundels of animals, and rock reliefs of kings receiving power from the gods were admired across Eurasia. The game of chess arrived from India in this period, and polo was a royal sport. Middle Persian literature, including epics of ancient kings, later fed into Ferdowsi's Shahnameh. Sasanian administration, court ceremony and art deeply influenced the Abbasid Caliphate.

## The last war

Khosrow II, who came to power with Byzantine help in 590, turned against Byzantium after the emperor Maurice was murdered in 602. His armies conquered Syria, took Jerusalem in 614 and carried off the True Cross, took Egypt in 619 and reached the Bosporus. The emperor Heraclius counterattacked through the Caucasus, defeated a Persian army near Nineveh in 627 and sacked Khosrow's palace. Khosrow was deposed and killed in 628. In the following four years, about ten rulers came and went, including two of Khosrow's daughters, Boran and Azarmidokht.

## Fall

Exhausted by war, plague and civil strife, the empire faced the Arab armies from 633. After al-Qadisiyyah, the Arabs took Ctesiphon in 637, and after Nahavand in 642 the empire collapsed. Yazdegerd III fled east and was killed near Merv in 651. His son Peroz sought help from Tang China. Many Zoroastrians eventually migrated to India, where they became the Parsis.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on the 300, 500 and 600 maps, largest on the 500 map at about 3.5 million square kilometres. Nearly half of that is in today's Iran, with large shares in Afghanistan, Iraq and Pakistan. Baghdad's site, Tehran, Isfahan, Tbilisi and Yerevan are Sasanian on our maps. Before it, the region is Parthian; on the 700 map it belongs to the Umayyad Caliphate.
