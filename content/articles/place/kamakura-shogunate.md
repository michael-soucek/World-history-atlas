The Kamakura shogunate was the first military government of Japan. From 1185 to 1333, warrior rulers based in the eastern town of Kamakura held real power, while the emperor and his court in Kyoto kept their prestige but little authority. It began nearly seven centuries during which Japan was largely governed by shoguns.

## The rise of the warriors

In the Heian period, the imperial court in Kyoto was dominated by the Fujiwara family, while in the provinces, warrior families, the samurai, grew powerful, managing estates and fighting. Two great clans, the Taira and the Minamoto, fought for influence at court in the twelfth century. Taira no Kiyomori dominated the court from the 1160s, and married his daughter to the emperor.

## The Genpei War

In 1180 the Minamoto rose against the Taira in the Genpei War. Minamoto no Yoritomo, who had been exiled as a boy, raised an army in the east, and his half-brother Yoshitsune, a brilliant commander, won a series of victories. In 1185, at the sea battle of Dan-no-ura, the Taira were destroyed; the child emperor Antoku drowned with his grandmother. The war inspired the Tale of the Heike, a great epic of Japanese literature, which begins with the sound of temple bells telling of the impermanence of all things. Yoritomo, jealous of Yoshitsune, hunted him down, and Yoshitsune died in 1189.

## Yoritomo's government

Yoritomo set up his government, the bakufu, "tent government", at Kamakura, his base in the east, far from the court. In 1185 he gained the right to appoint military governors, shugo, in each province, and stewards, jitō, on estates, giving him control of the country's military and land. In 1192 the emperor appointed him shogun. His vassals, gokenin, owed him loyalty and military service in return for protection of their lands.

## The Hōjō regents

After Yoritomo's death in 1199, his widow Hōjō Masako, the "nun shogun", and her family took control. The Hōjō ruled as regents, shikken, for puppet shoguns, often children from the Fujiwara or imperial families. In 1221 the retired emperor Go-Toba tried to overthrow the shogunate in the Jōkyū War, but was defeated and exiled, strengthening Kamakura's control. In 1232 Hōjō Yasutoki issued the Goseibai Shikimoku, a law code for the warrior class, based on custom and fairness, which influenced later laws.

## The Mongol invasions

In 1274 and 1281, Kublai Khan sent fleets of Mongol, Chinese and Korean troops to invade Kyushu. The regent Hōjō Tokimune refused Kublai's demands for submission. Samurai fought fiercely on the beaches, and stone walls were built along Hakata Bay after the first invasion. Both times, storms destroyed much of the invading fleets. The Japanese called these winds kamikaze, "divine winds". The invasions were also depicted in the Mongol Invasion Scrolls, commissioned by a samurai to record his deeds.

## New Buddhism

The Kamakura period saw new forms of Buddhism that reached ordinary people. Hōnen and Shinran taught Pure Land Buddhism, saying that repeating the name of Amida Buddha brought rebirth in the Pure Land. Nichiren taught devotion to the Lotus Sutra. Zen, brought from China by Eisai and Dōgen, appealed to warriors. The great bronze Buddha of Kamakura was cast in 1252. Realistic sculptures by artists such as Unkei and Kaikei, such as the guardian figures at Tōdai-ji, are masterpieces.

## The fall

The cost of defending against the Mongols, without new lands to reward warriors, strained the loyalty of the gokenin. In 1331 Emperor Go-Daigo plotted to restore imperial rule. In 1333 the general Ashikaga Takauji turned against the shogunate and took Kyoto, while Nitta Yoshisada attacked Kamakura. The regent Hōjō Takatoki and hundreds of his followers took their own lives. Go-Daigo's brief Kenmu Restoration ended when Takauji founded the Ashikaga shogunate in 1336.
