The Kingdom of Benin was one of the oldest and most developed states of the West African forest, centred on Benin City in what is now southern Nigeria. It has no connection with the modern Republic of Benin, which took its name from the nearby Bight of Benin in 1975. The kingdom is famous today for the Benin Bronzes, and its monarchy survives.

## The Ogisos

The Edo people's oral traditions tell of a first dynasty of kings, the Ogisos, "rulers of the sky", who ruled from around the tenth century. When the last Ogiso was banished, the Edo, according to tradition, asked Oduduwa, king of the Yoruba city of Ife, for a ruler. Oduduwa sent his son Oranmiyan, who married a local woman and left, and their son Eweka I became the first oba, around the twelfth or thirteenth century.

## Ewuare the Great

Oba Ewuare, who ruled in the mid-fifteenth century, transformed Benin into an empire. He rebuilt Benin City after a civil war, laid out broad streets and divided it into a royal palace area and town quarters for guilds of craftsmen. He strengthened the city's walls and moats. Benin City and the surrounding region were enclosed by a vast network of earthworks, the Benin moats, estimated at thousands of kilometres in total, built over centuries. Ewuare expanded the kingdom through conquests and created councils of chiefs.

## The Portuguese

Portuguese sailors reached Benin in the 1480s. Oba Esigie, who ruled in the early sixteenth century, sent an ambassador to Lisbon, allowed missionaries and traded with the Portuguese, who helped him in a war against the Igala. Benin traded pepper, ivory, cloth, beads and, for a time, enslaved captives, in exchange for brass manillas, coral beads, cloth and firearms. Benin restricted the sale of male slaves for long periods, and the slave trade played a smaller role than in neighbouring states. The Dutch, English and French also traded at Benin's port of Ughoton.

## The oba and the court

The oba was a sacred king, believed to be descended from the gods, with power over life and death. He lived in a large palace with courtyards, guarded by guilds of officials and craftsmen. Royal ceremonies, festivals and sacrifices were elaborate. A Dutch visitor, Olfert Dapper, described Benin City in 1668 as large and orderly, with broad straight streets and houses kept clean.

## The Benin Bronzes

Royal guilds of brass casters, using the lost-wax method, made thousands of plaques, heads and figures, along with ivory carvers who made masks and tusks. The plaques, which once decorated the pillars of the palace, show the oba, chiefs, warriors, ceremonies and Portuguese soldiers and traders. Commemorative heads of past obas were placed on altars. The ivory mask of Queen Mother Idia, mother of Oba Esigie, became a symbol of African art; a version was used as the emblem of FESTAC, the 1977 festival of African culture.

## The British expedition

In the nineteenth century, Benin resisted British influence and the palm oil trade. In January 1897, a British party led by Acting Consul James Phillips travelled to Benin City during an important religious festival, against the oba's wishes, and most of its members were killed. In February, a British punitive expedition of about 1,200 men captured and burned Benin City. Oba Ovonramwen was deposed and exiled to Calabar, where he died in 1914. The British took thousands of bronzes, ivories and other objects, which were sold to museums and collectors across Europe and America, including the British Museum.

## Restoration and return

In 1914 the British restored the monarchy, with Ovonramwen's son as Oba Eweka II. Benin became part of colonial Nigeria. Since the 2020s, museums and governments, in Germany, the United States and elsewhere, have begun returning Benin Bronzes to Nigeria. The current oba, Ewuare II, crowned in 2016, is an important traditional ruler.
