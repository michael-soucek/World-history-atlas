Great Zimbabwe is the largest ancient stone structure in Africa south of the Sahara. Built by the ancestors of the Shona people between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries, it was the capital of a kingdom that grew rich on cattle and on the gold trade with the Indian Ocean. The modern country of Zimbabwe takes its name from it; dzimba dza mabwe means "houses of stone" in Shona.

## Before Great Zimbabwe

Bantu-speaking farmers settled the Zimbabwe plateau in the first millennium CE, growing sorghum and millet, herding cattle and working iron. In the tenth to thirteenth centuries, the kingdom of Mapungubwe, on the Limpopo river, at the border of today's South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe, became the first major state in the region, trading gold and ivory to the coast. Its royal hill burials contained a famous gold rhinoceros. After Mapungubwe declined, around 1300, Great Zimbabwe became the leading centre.

## The site

Great Zimbabwe covers about 7 square kilometres in the south-east of the Zimbabwe plateau. It has three main areas. The Hill Complex, on a granite hill, has enclosures built among the boulders and was probably the oldest royal and ritual centre. The Great Enclosure, in the valley below, has an outer wall about 250 metres long, up to 11 metres high and 5 metres thick, built of granite blocks without mortar, decorated with a chevron pattern, and inside, a solid conical tower about 10 metres high, perhaps symbolising a grain bin or royal power. The Valley Ruins contain many smaller enclosures and houses of daga, earth and gravel. At its height in the fourteenth century, perhaps 10,000 to 18,000 people lived there.

## The Zimbabwe birds

Eight carved soapstone birds, about 40 centimetres tall, mounted on pillars, were found at the site, probably representing royal ancestors or a spiritual messenger. Several were taken to South Africa in the colonial period, and most have been returned. The Zimbabwe bird appears on the national flag and coat of arms.

## Gold and trade

The kingdom's wealth came from cattle and from controlling the trade in gold, mined on the plateau, ivory and copper, through the Swahili coastal city of Sofala, in today's Mozambique, to Kilwa and across the Indian Ocean. Excavations have found glass beads from India and the Middle East, Chinese celadon and porcelain, Persian pottery and a coin from Kilwa. Gold was not used locally as money but as a trade good and for ornaments.

## Society

The ruling elite lived in the stone enclosures, while commoners lived in surrounding huts. The kingdom probably ruled a network of smaller zimbabwes, stone-walled sites across the region, of which over a hundred are known. Religion probably centred on ancestral spirits, mhondoro, and a high god, Mwari, still honoured by the Shona today.

## Decline

Great Zimbabwe was largely abandoned by the mid-fifteenth century. Overpopulation, exhaustion of soils, firewood and pastures, and shifts in the gold trade northwards are possible reasons. Its successors were the Mutapa state, in the north, which traded with the Portuguese, and the Torwa or Butua kingdom, centred on Khami, in the south-west, followed by the Rozvi.

## Colonial myths

When Europeans reached the ruins in the late nineteenth century, many refused to believe Africans had built them. The German geologist Karl Mauch, in 1871, attributed them to the Queen of Sheba, and Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company promoted theories of Phoenician or Arab builders. Amateur diggers destroyed much evidence. Archaeologists David Randall-MacIver in 1905 and Gertrude Caton-Thompson in 1929 showed the site was African, but Rhodesia's white minority government continued to censor this conclusion until the 1970s. Great Zimbabwe became a symbol of African nationalism and is a World Heritage Site.

## On our maps

Our maps show the kingdom from 1000 to 1400, with an outline of up to about 210,000 square kilometres, three-quarters of it in today's Zimbabwe and the rest in Mozambique, South Africa and Botswana. The early outlines stand for the communities that preceded the stone city. On the 1492 map the region belongs to Mutapa.
