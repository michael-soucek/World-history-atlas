The Ghana Empire was the first great state of the western Sahel, the band of grassland south of the Sahara. It was centred in what is now south-eastern Mauritania and western Mali, a long way north of the modern country of Ghana, which took its name when it became independent in 1957. Its own people, the Soninke, called it Wagadu; "ghana" seems to have been a royal title that Arab writers used for the whole kingdom.

## The Soninke and Wagadu

The people of Ghana were the Soninke, speakers of a Mande language who farmed millet and sorghum in the Sahel and herded cattle. In Soninke tradition, the kingdom of Wagadu was protected by a great snake, Bida, which demanded the yearly sacrifice of a young woman in return for rain and gold. When a young man killed the snake to save his beloved, the rains stopped, the gold disappeared and the Soninke scattered. The legend may preserve memories of drought and decline.

## The trade in gold

West Africa's gold came from the fields of Bambuk and Bure, near the upper Senegal and Niger rivers, south of Ghana. Ghana's power came from controlling the routes between these goldfields and the Saharan caravan towns, such as Awdaghust, where North African and Berber merchants traded salt, copper, cloth, horses and luxury goods. Salt from Saharan mines such as Taghaza was in great demand in the Sahel and forest regions. Arab geographers described a "silent trade" in which gold and salt were exchanged without the traders meeting, though this may be a legend.

## Al-Bakri's description

The Andalusian geographer al-Bakri, writing in 1068 from earlier reports, gives the fullest description of Ghana. He wrote that the king, Tunka Manin, held court surrounded by pages holding gold-decorated shields and swords, with dogs wearing gold collars. Petitioners knelt and threw dust on their heads. The king's town had a palace and domed buildings, and sacred groves where priests guarded the royal tombs and idols. Muslim merchants lived in a separate town with twelve mosques, and Muslims served the king as interpreters, treasurers and ministers, though the king himself followed traditional religion.

## Power and army

According to al-Bakri, the king could raise an army of 200,000 men, 40,000 of them archers, a number that is probably exaggerated but shows the state's reputation. Ghana ruled many tributary peoples, and its kings inherited power through the matrilineal line, passing to the son of the king's sister.

## Koumbi Saleh

Excavations at Koumbi Saleh, in southern Mauritania, since the 1910s have uncovered a large town of stone houses, a mosque and cemeteries, occupied from around the ninth to the fourteenth centuries. Many archaeologists identify it with the Muslim town of Ghana's capital, though the royal town has not been securely located.

## Decline and legacy

Ghana's decline in the eleventh and twelfth centuries is debated. Arab sources say the Almoravids, Berber reformers from the Sahara, took Ghana in 1076, but many historians now see this as an exaggeration and stress the gradual shift of trade routes eastward and the drying of the Sahel. The Sosso kingdom then dominated the region until Sundiata Keita founded Mali. The Soninke spread across West Africa as traders. When the Gold Coast became independent in 1957, its leaders chose the name Ghana to honour the old empire.

## On our maps

Our maps show Ghana on every map from 300 to 1100, at between roughly 200,000 and 335,000 square kilometres, split between today's Mauritania and Mali. Its largest outline is on the 300 map. Because the empire's early history is so poorly documented, its shape on any map is an estimate.
