The Huns appeared on the steppes north of the Black Sea around 370 CE, coming from somewhere further east. Their origins are still debated; some historians link them to the Xiongnu who had fought Han China centuries earlier, but the evidence is thin. What is certain is the effect of their arrival: they defeated the Alans and the Gothic kingdoms of the region and drove many Goths to seek refuge inside the Roman Empire.

## Who were the Huns?

The origins of the Huns are uncertain. Ancient writers described them as a new and terrifying people from beyond the Sea of Azov. Since the eighteenth century, some scholars have linked them to the Xiongnu, the nomadic empire that fought Han China, based partly on the similarity of the names and on similar bronze cauldrons found across the steppe. Others argue that the Huns were a new confederation formed on the steppe, possibly including some descendants of the Xiongnu among many other peoples. Their language is unknown, though some names suggest Turkic or other Central Asian connections.

## The Roman view

The Roman historian Ammianus Marcellinus, writing in the 390s, described the Huns as savage horsemen who ate raw meat, lived on horseback and practised skull deformation, binding the heads of infants to lengthen their skulls, a practice confirmed by archaeology. Such descriptions reflected Roman fear and prejudice, but the Huns' military strength was real: they were superb mounted archers, using powerful composite bows, and moved quickly across great distances.

## The domino effect

Around 370 the Huns defeated the Alans on the Volga and then the Gothic kingdom of Ermanaric in today's Ukraine. In 376 tens of thousands of Goths fled to the Danube and asked to enter the Roman Empire, setting off events that led to the Roman defeat at Adrianople in 378. Historians debate how far the Huns triggered the wider migrations of peoples that followed, but their arrival clearly disrupted the balance on Rome's frontiers.

## An empire of many peoples

By the 420s, the Huns had moved their centre to the Hungarian plain. Their empire was a loose network of peoples, including Goths, Gepids, Rugians, Sciri and Alans, ruled by Hunnic kings and their nobles. Subject peoples provided warriors, and the Huns collected tribute and plunder from the Roman Empire. Romans also hired Hunnic troops: the western general Aetius relied on them for years.

## Attila's court

The Roman diplomat Priscus visited Attila's court in 449 and left the most detailed account of it. He described a wooden palace and compound, banquets with ritual toasts, and a Greek merchant who had been captured, freed and chose to live among the Huns, praising their way of life over Roman taxes and corruption. Attila was served on wooden plates, while his guests had silver. Priscus also described Attila's queen, Kreka, and his sons.

## Attila

Attila ruled alone after killing his brother around 445. He ravaged the Balkans, then turned west. In 451 he invaded Gaul and was checked at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains by the Roman general Aetius and his Visigothic allies. The next year he invaded Italy and sacked several cities, but disease and supply problems forced him back; tradition credits Pope Leo I with persuading him to leave. Attila died in 453, on the night of his wedding.

## After Attila

After Attila's death in 453, his sons divided his empire and quarrelled. The subject peoples, led by the Gepid king Ardaric, rose in revolt and defeated the Huns at the River Nedao in 454, killing Attila's eldest son, Ellac. Another son, Dengizich, was killed fighting the Romans in 469, and his head was displayed in Constantinople. The remaining Huns merged with other steppe peoples, such as the Bulgars.

## Memory

The Huns became a symbol of barbarian destruction in European memory. In German legend, Attila appears as Etzel in the Nibelungenlied, and in Norse legend as Atli. Hungarians later came to regard Attila as a founding figure, though the Magyars arrived in the region four centuries after the Huns.
