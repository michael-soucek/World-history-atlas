Canada became a self-governing country in 1867, when three British colonies in North America united in the Dominion of Canada. It grew to stretch from the Atlantic to the Pacific and north to the Arctic, the second-largest country in the world, and gained full legal independence from Britain in stages over the following century.

## First Nations, Inuit and Métis

Indigenous peoples have lived in what is now Canada for at least 12,000 years, in many nations with diverse ways of life: the Haudenosaunee farmers of the Great Lakes, the Cree and Anishinaabe of the forests, the Blackfoot and others of the plains, the fishing and woodworking peoples of the Pacific coast, famous for their totem poles, and the Inuit of the Arctic. Norse explorers briefly settled at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, around 1000.

## New France

John Cabot reached Newfoundland for England in 1497. Jacques Cartier explored the St Lawrence for France in 1534–1542, and Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec City in 1608. New France grew slowly, based on the fur trade with Indigenous allies, especially the Huron-Wendat and Algonquin, and on farming along the St Lawrence. Catholic missionaries worked among Indigenous peoples. Montreal was founded in 1642. The Hudson's Bay Company, founded by England in 1670, traded in the north.

## British conquest

In the Seven Years' War, the British took Quebec after the Battle of the Plains of Abraham in 1759, where both commanders, James Wolfe and the Marquis de Montcalm, were killed. The Treaty of Paris in 1763 gave New France to Britain. The Quebec Act of 1774 allowed French Canadians to keep their Catholic religion and civil law. After the American Revolution, about 40,000 Loyalists moved north, leading to the creation of Upper Canada, now Ontario, and Lower Canada, now Quebec, in 1791. In the War of 1812, British, Canadian and Indigenous forces, including the Shawnee leader Tecumseh, repelled American invasions.

## Toward self-government

Rebellions in 1837–1838 in both Canadas demanded responsible government. Lord Durham's report recommended uniting the Canadas, done in 1841, and responsible government was granted by 1848. In 1867 the British North America Act created the Dominion of Canada, uniting Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick, with John A. Macdonald as the first prime minister.

## From sea to sea

Canada bought Rupert's Land from the Hudson's Bay Company in 1870. The Métis of Red River, led by Louis Riel, resisted, leading to the creation of Manitoba. British Columbia joined in 1871, on the promise of a railway, and Prince Edward Island in 1873. The Canadian Pacific Railway was completed in 1885. The same year, Riel led the North-West Rebellion and was hanged. The numbered treaties with First Nations opened the west to settlement, and the Indian Act of 1876 controlled Indigenous life. Residential schools, run by churches for the government until the 1990s, separated children from their families, and many children died; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission reported in 2015.

## Independence and the twentieth century

Over 60,000 Canadians died in the First World War, and the victory at Vimy Ridge in 1917 became a national symbol. The Statute of Westminster of 1931 confirmed Canada's legislative independence. Canada fought in the Second World War, including on D-Day at Juno Beach. Newfoundland joined in 1949. The Quiet Revolution in Quebec in the 1960s led to a sovereignty movement; referendums in 1980 and 1995 rejected independence, the second by about 50.6 to 49.4 percent. In 1982 the constitution was patriated, with a Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Canada adopted official bilingualism in 1969 and multiculturalism in 1971, and created Nunavut, an Inuit-majority territory, in 1999.
