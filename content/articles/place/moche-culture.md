The Moche culture flourished on the desert coast of northern Peru from about 100 to 800 CE. Living in river valleys that cut through one of the driest places on earth, the Moche built irrigation canals, huge mud-brick pyramids and some of the most remarkable pottery and metalwork of the ancient Americas.

## The desert coast

The north coast of Peru is one of the driest places on earth, but rivers flowing from the Andes create fertile valleys every 20 to 60 kilometres. The Moche lived in valleys such as Moche, Chicama, Jequetepeque, Lambayeque and Piura. The cold Humboldt current makes the sea one of the richest fishing grounds in the world, and the Moche fished from reed boats, still used today, called caballitos de totora.

## Irrigation and farming

The Moche built large irrigation canals, some running between valleys, to farm maize, beans, squash, peanuts, chillies, cotton and other crops. Fertiliser from guano, seabird droppings, collected on offshore islands, may have been used. Llamas carried goods from the highlands, and guinea pigs and ducks were raised for food.

## The Huacas del Sol y de la Luna

At the site of Moche, near Trujillo, the Huaca del Sol, built of an estimated 140 million adobe bricks, was one of the largest structures in the Americas. Many bricks bear marks, possibly identifying the communities that made them as tribute. The Huaca de la Luna, a temple, was rebuilt several times, each layer covering the last, preserving brightly painted reliefs of a fanged deity, called the Decapitator, holding a knife and a severed head. Between the two huacas was an urban centre with houses, workshops and streets.

## Pottery

Moche potters were among the finest in the ancient Americas. They made stirrup-spout vessels in the form of people, animals, plants, buildings and gods, and portrait vessels showing individuals at different ages, perhaps rulers. Fineline paintings on pottery show complex scenes: warriors fighting, captives being stripped and led away, ceremonies, myths, hunting, fishing and daily life. Some vessels depict sexual acts and illness. Moulds allowed workshops to produce many copies.

## Metalwork

Moche metalworkers used gold, silver and copper, and techniques such as gilding, soldering and alloying, to make ornaments, ear spools, nose ornaments, headdresses and masks. Electrochemical replacement gilding coated copper objects with thin layers of gold.

## Sipán and the Sacrifice Ceremony

The tomb of the Lord of Sipán, found in 1987 by Walter Alva, contained a man buried with gold and silver peanut-shaped beads, ear spools, a gold backflap and sceptre, accompanied by sacrificed attendants, a dog and llamas. Paintings on pottery show the "Sacrifice Ceremony", in which captives' blood was offered to a figure called the Warrior Priest. The finds at Sipán and the tomb of a priestess at San José de Moro, found in 1991, showed that these scenes were real rituals and that the figures were real people. At El Brujo, the tattooed mummy of a powerful woman, the Señora de Cao, was found in 2005.

## Warfare

Moche art shows warriors in pairs fighting with clubs and shields, defeating and capturing opponents. Many scholars think these were ritual combats to obtain victims for sacrifice. Skeletons of sacrificed men, with wounds, have been found at the Huaca de la Luna.

## Decline

Around 600 CE, the Moche faced severe El Niño floods and droughts, and sand dunes buried fields. Moche society reorganised, and the centre moved north to sites such as Pampa Grande. By around 800 CE, Moche culture had faded, and the Wari influence spread. Moche traditions influenced later cultures, such as the Lambayeque, or Sicán, and the Chimú.
