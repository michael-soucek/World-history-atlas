The Kingdom of Scotland was traditionally founded in 843, when Kenneth MacAlpin became king of both the Gaelic Scots and the Picts. It began as a kingdom north of the Firth of Forth, known as Alba, and grew over the following centuries to cover most of what is now Scotland, before uniting with England in 1707.

## Picts, Scots and Britons

Before the kingdom of Scotland, northern Britain was home to several peoples: the Picts in the north and east, known for their carved symbol stones; the Gaelic-speaking Scots of Dál Riata in the west, who came from Ireland; the Britons of Strathclyde; and the Angles of Northumbria in the south-east. Saint Columba founded the monastery of Iona in 563, spreading Christianity. Viking raids from the late eighth century weakened the Picts and helped the Scots under Kenneth MacAlpin unite the two peoples.

## Medieval kings

Macbeth, who ruled from 1040 to 1057, was a real king, very different from Shakespeare's tragic villain; he made a pilgrimage to Rome. Malcolm III, who defeated Macbeth, married the English princess Margaret, later Saint Margaret, who promoted church reform. David I, in the twelfth century, introduced feudalism, founded royal burghs, abbeys such as Melrose and Holyrood, and issued Scotland's first coins. Alexander III's death in 1286, when his horse fell from a cliff, led to a succession crisis.

## The Wars of Independence

Edward I of England chose John Balliol as king, but treated him as a vassal. When Balliol rebelled, Edward invaded in 1296, deposed him and took the Stone of Destiny, on which Scottish kings were crowned, to Westminster. William Wallace and Andrew Moray defeated the English at Stirling Bridge in 1297, but Wallace was defeated at Falkirk in 1298 and executed in London in 1305. Robert the Bruce, crowned in 1306, won at Bannockburn in 1314, and the Treaty of Edinburgh-Northampton in 1328 recognised Scotland's independence.

## The Stewarts

The Stewart dynasty began in 1371 with Robert II, grandson of Robert the Bruce. Many Stewart kings came to the throne as children, leading to periods of conflict among the nobility. James I was a prisoner in England for eighteen years. James IV, a Renaissance king, founded a navy and a university at Aberdeen, but was killed with much of the nobility at Flodden in 1513. Scotland's universities, St Andrews, Glasgow and Aberdeen, all date from the fifteenth century.

## Reformation and Mary

John Knox, influenced by John Calvin, led the Reformation in Scotland, which became officially Protestant in 1560, with a Presbyterian church, the Kirk. Mary, Queen of Scots, a Catholic, returned from France in 1561, but her marriages, the murder of her husband Lord Darnley and her marriage to the Earl of Bothwell led to her forced abdication in 1567. She fled to England, where Elizabeth I imprisoned her and, in 1587, had her executed.

## The union

In 1603 Mary's son James VI became James I of England. In the seventeenth century, Scottish Covenanters resisted royal religious policy, and Scotland was involved in the civil wars. The failure of the Darien scheme, a Scottish colony in Panama in the 1690s, ruined many investors and helped persuade the Scottish Parliament to accept union with England in 1707, though many Scots opposed it. Scotland kept its own legal system, church and education, and the Scottish Parliament was restored in 1999.
