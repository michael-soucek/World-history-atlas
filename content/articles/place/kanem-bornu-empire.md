Kanem-Bornu was one of the longest-lived states in African history. Centred around Lake Chad, it lasted in one form or another for about a thousand years, from roughly the ninth century to 1900, ruled for most of that time by a single dynasty, the Sayfawa, and then by the family of al-Kanemi.

## Kanem

The first state, Kanem, arose north-east of Lake Chad, in what is now the country of Chad, among the Kanembu and related peoples, perhaps including Saharan nomads called the Zaghawa. Arab geographers mention it from the ninth century. Its rulers, called mai, grew rich by controlling trade routes across the Sahara to the Fezzan and Tripoli, exchanging enslaved people, ivory and other goods for horses, cloth, salt and metal goods. The mai was treated as sacred and rarely seen in public.

## The Sayfawa and Islam

According to tradition, the Sayfawa dynasty traced its descent to Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, a pre-Islamic Yemeni hero. Around 1085, Mai Hummay, or Umme Jilmi, converted to Islam, and his successors made the pilgrimage to Mecca and invited Muslim scholars. In the thirteenth century, Mai Dunama Dabbalemi built a large cavalry army, extended Kanem north to the Fezzan, in today's Libya, and founded a hostel in Cairo for Kanemi students and pilgrims. According to chroniclers, he also opened a sacred object, the mune, which was believed to cause disaster.

## The move to Bornu

In the late fourteenth century, under pressure from the Bulala people and dynastic conflict, the Sayfawa abandoned Kanem and moved west of Lake Chad to Bornu, in today's north-eastern Nigeria and Niger. After decades of instability, Mai Ali Gaji, who ruled from about 1465 to 1497, restored order and founded a walled capital, Ngazargamu, on the Komadugu Yobe river, which remained the capital for over three centuries.

## Idris Alooma

Bornu reached its height under Mai Idris Alooma, who ruled from about 1564 to 1596. He imported firearms and hired Turkish musketeers from the Ottoman Empire, and used camels for transport. He defeated the Bulala and retook Kanem, and campaigned against the Hausa cities, the Tuareg and others. He promoted Islamic law, built brick mosques and established courts. His imam, Ahmad ibn Furtu, wrote a chronicle of his campaigns, one of the earliest detailed histories written in sub-Saharan Africa. Bornu exchanged embassies with the Ottomans and Morocco.

## Society and trade

Bornu was a major centre of Islamic learning, and its scholars were respected across West Africa. The economy depended on agriculture, cattle, the trans-Saharan trade, and slave raiding to the south. Bornu exported enslaved people to North Africa through the Fezzan, and imported horses, weapons, cloth and books. The Kanuri language became dominant.

## Al-Kanemi

In the early nineteenth century, the Fulani jihad, led by Usman dan Fodio, spread to Bornu, accusing its rulers of mixing Islam with paganism. In 1808 the Fulani sacked Ngazargamu. Muhammad al-Amin al-Kanemi, a scholar from Kanem, led the resistance, defeating the Fulani and writing letters to Sultan Muhammad Bello of Sokoto arguing that Bornu was a Muslim state and could not be attacked. Al-Kanemi became the real ruler, taking the title Shehu, and founded a new capital at Kukawa in 1814. His son Umar ended the Sayfawa dynasty in 1846.

## The end

In 1893 Rabih az-Zubayr, a warlord from Sudan, conquered Bornu and destroyed Kukawa. The French defeated and killed Rabih at Kousséri in 1900, and Bornu was divided between British Nigeria, German Cameroon and French Chad and Niger. The British restored the Shehu, whose descendants still hold the title of Shehu of Borno, with their seat in Maiduguri. The region has suffered greatly from the Boko Haram insurgency since 2009.
