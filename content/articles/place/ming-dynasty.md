The Ming dynasty ruled China for 276 years, from 1368 to 1644, between two foreign-led dynasties: the Mongol Yuan before it and the Manchu Qing after. Its founder, Zhu Yuanzhang, was born into a poor farming family, worked as a monk and a beggar, and rose to lead one of the rebel armies that drove the Mongols out. He took the reign name Hongwu and made Nanjing his capital.

## Hongwu's China

Zhu Yuanzhang, the Hongwu emperor, set out to rebuild a country devastated by famine, plague and war. He ordered land surveys and population registers, the Yellow Registers and the Fish-Scale Maps, resettled people on abandoned land and organised villages into groups responsible for taxes and order. He set up a hereditary military class with its own farmland, intended to feed the army without burdening the treasury. His rule was also extremely harsh: in purges against the chancellor Hu Weiyong and the general Lan Yu, tens of thousands of people were executed.

## Yongle and the move to Beijing

When Hongwu died in 1398, his grandson the Jianwen emperor tried to reduce the power of his uncles. One of them, Zhu Di, the Prince of Yan, rebelled and in 1402 took Nanjing, where the palace burned and Jianwen disappeared. As the Yongle emperor, Zhu Di moved the capital to his old base in the north, Beijing, which was rebuilt around the Forbidden City between 1406 and 1420. The Grand Canal was restored to supply the capital with grain from the south. Yongle also sponsored the Yongle Encyclopedia, a vast compilation of about 11,000 volumes, and led five campaigns into Mongolia, dying on the last of them in 1424.

## The voyages of Zheng He

Between 1405 and 1433 the eunuch admiral Zheng He commanded seven expeditions of the treasure fleet, with hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of men, to Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, Arabia and East Africa. The voyages brought envoys to the Ming court and displayed Chinese power, but they were costly, and after Yongle's death officials argued that they brought little benefit. The voyages stopped, and the court restricted private overseas trade for much of the following century.

## The northern frontier

The Mongols remained the Ming's main enemy. In 1449 the young Zhengtong emperor led an army against the Oirat leader Esen and was captured at Tumu, a humiliating disaster. Afterwards the Ming relied increasingly on fortifications. Much of the Great Wall that tourists visit today, faced with brick and stone, with watchtowers along mountain ridges near Beijing, was built in the sixteenth century. A peace with the Mongol ruler Altan Khan in 1571, which opened border markets, finally eased the pressure.

## Government and the examinations

The Ming state was run by scholar-officials chosen through the civil-service examinations, which tested candidates on the Confucian classics using the commentaries of Zhu Xi. The examinations became more formalised, with the rigid "eight-legged essay". Because Hongwu had abolished the chancellorship, emperors depended on Grand Secretaries and on palace eunuchs, who at times held enormous power. The eunuch Wei Zhongxian dominated the court in the 1620s and persecuted officials of the Donglin faction.

## Economy and society

The Ming population grew from perhaps 65–90 million at the start to perhaps 150 million or more by 1600. Commercial cities flourished, especially in the lower Yangtze, producing silk and cotton cloth. The kilns of Jingdezhen made the blue-and-white porcelain that was exported across the world. Large amounts of silver arrived from Japan and from Spanish America via Manila, and in the 1570s the official Zhang Juzheng's "Single Whip" reforms converted many taxes into payments in silver. Novels such as Journey to the West, Water Margin and The Plum in the Golden Vase were written for an urban readership.

## Europeans arrive

The Portuguese reached China in 1513 and in 1557 were allowed to settle at Macau. Jesuit missionaries followed, most famously Matteo Ricci, who arrived in Beijing in 1601, learned Chinese, dressed as a Confucian scholar and introduced European mathematics, astronomy and maps. New crops from the Americas, such as maize, sweet potatoes and peanuts, spread through southern China.

## Crisis

The last decades of the dynasty brought a combination of disasters. The Wanli emperor, who reigned from 1572 to 1620, refused for years to meet his ministers or fill vacant posts. The Ming spent heavily to help Korea against Japanese invasions in the 1590s. A cooling climate, often linked to the Little Ice Age, brought droughts and floods, and fluctuations in silver imports disrupted the economy. Unpaid soldiers and starving peasants joined rebel armies, while the Manchus grew stronger in the north-east.

## Collapse

The last Ming decades brought drought, famine, unpaid armies and a court paralysed by factions. In 1644 the rebel Li Zicheng took Beijing and the last emperor hanged himself on a hill behind the palace. A Ming general then let the Manchus through the Great Wall to fight the rebels, and the Manchus stayed to found the Qing. Ming princes held out in the south as the Southern Ming until 1662.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Ming from 1400 to 1600, and it is largest on the 1530 map, at about 6 million square kilometres. The basemap's Ming reaches far into Manchuria, so around 14 percent of that area lies in today's Russia; the core of the state was China proper, and its control of the north-east was much looser than the map suggests. Beijing, Nanjing, Xi'an, Guangzhou and Shanghai are all Ming cities on our maps. On the 1300 map the same lands belong to the Mongol Yuan; by 1650 they are Qing.
