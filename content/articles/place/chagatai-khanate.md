The Chagatai Khanate was the Mongol state in Central Asia, named after Genghis Khan's second son, Chagatai, who received the lands between the Altai mountains and the Amu Darya when his father divided his empire. It lasted, in various forms, from the thirteenth century to the seventeenth, and its name survives in Chagatai, the Turkic literary language of Central Asia.

## Chagatai's inheritance

Chagatai, who died in 1242, was known as a strict guardian of Genghis Khan's laws, the Yassa. His ulus included the steppes of today's Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, the Ili valley, and the oasis cities of Transoxiana, such as Samarkand and Bukhara, though the cities were at first governed by officials answering to the Great Khan, such as Mahmud Yalavach and his son Masud Beg. The khans themselves lived as nomads, moving between pastures, with their main camp near Almaliq in the Ili valley.

## Kaidu and the wars with Kublai

The khanate was weak and often dominated by its neighbours. From the 1260s, Kaidu, a grandson of Ögedei, built a power base in Central Asia and controlled the Chagatai khans for decades, fighting Kublai Khan and the Ilkhanate. After Kaidu's death in 1301, the Chagatai khan Duwa made peace with the Yuan dynasty in 1304, and the khanate became more independent.

## Turkification and Islam

The Mongol ruling class gradually adopted the Turkic language of the majority. Khan Kebek, who ruled from 1318 to 1326, built a palace near Nasaf, issued silver coins, kebeki, and favoured the settled regions of Transoxiana. His brother Tarmashirin, who ruled from about 1331, converted to Islam and was visited by Ibn Battuta, but was overthrown around 1334 by nomadic chiefs in the east who saw him as abandoning Mongol traditions.

## East and west

After the 1340s, the khanate split. In the west, in Transoxiana, Turkic emirs held real power. Emir Qazaghan ruled through puppet khans from 1346 to 1358. In the 1360s, Timur, of the Barlas tribe, rose and, by 1370, ruled Transoxiana, still through puppet khans of Chagatai's line. In the east, the khanate continued as Moghulistan, "land of the Mongols", ruled by Tughlugh Timur, who converted to Islam in the 1350s, and his descendants, nomads of the steppes of today's Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan and the Tarim basin. The Dughlat emirs held great power there.

## The Moghul khans

The Moghul khans fought Timur and his successors and the Oirats and Kazakhs. Mirza Muhammad Haidar Dughlat, a cousin of Babur, wrote the Tarikh-i Rashidi in the sixteenth century, a history of the Moghuls. In the sixteenth century, the Yarkent Khanate, with its capital at Yarkand in the Tarim basin, continued Chagatai rule until the Dzungars conquered it in the 1680s.

## The Chagatai language

The Turkic literary language of Central Asia, written in the Arabic script, became known as Chagatai. Ali-Shir Navai, in the fifteenth century, wrote poetry and prose in Chagatai, arguing that Turkic was as fit for literature as Persian. Babur's memoirs, the Baburnama, were written in Chagatai. It is the ancestor of modern Uzbek and Uyghur.

## Legacy

The Chagatai Khanate transmitted Genghisid legitimacy across Central Asia. Timur married into the family, and the Mughal emperors of India descended from Chagatai through Babur's mother.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Chagatai Khanate from 1279 to 1500. Its largest outline, about 3.3 million square kilometres on the 1279 map, lies across today's China and Kazakhstan, with the rest in Uzbekistan and its neighbours. Samarkand, Tashkent, Almaty and Kashgar lie inside it for much of the period. In the later maps the name covers the eastern khanate of Moghulistan. On the 1530 map, the region is held by the Bukhara Khanate and others.
