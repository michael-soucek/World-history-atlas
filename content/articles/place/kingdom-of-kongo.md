The Kingdom of Kongo was one of the largest states of west-central Africa, ruling the lands south of the lower Congo river, in today's northern Angola and the western Democratic Republic of the Congo. It was founded in the late fourteenth century and, from the 1480s, developed close ties with Portugal, including the adoption of Christianity by its kings.

## The kingdom

Kongo's ruler, the manikongo, governed from his capital at Mbanza Kongo, appointing governors to the provinces and collecting tribute in cloth, livestock and nzimbu shells, which served as currency. Tradition holds that the kingdom was founded by Lukeni lua Nimi, who crossed the Congo river from the north and conquered the region.

## The kingdom's structure

The manikongo ruled from Mbanza Kongo, on a hill in northern Angola, and governed through provinces such as Soyo, Mbamba and Nsundi, ruled by governors he appointed, often relatives. The king was elected by a council of nobles from among descendants of the royal line, which often led to succession disputes. Kongo's economy was based on farming, crafts such as weaving raffia cloth and iron working, and trade. Nzimbu shells from the island of Luanda served as currency, and the king controlled their supply.

## Afonso I

Afonso I, who ruled from about 1509 to 1542, was a devout Christian who promoted the church, built churches and schools, and sent young nobles, including his son Henrique, to study in Portugal. Henrique was consecrated bishop in 1518. Afonso wrote letters to the Portuguese kings, some of which survive, in which he asked for priests, teachers and doctors, and complained that Portuguese traders were kidnapping free people, including nobles, and selling them as slaves. He tried to regulate the slave trade, but it continued to grow.

## Christianity in Kongo

Kongolese Christianity blended Catholic beliefs with local traditions. Kongo sent ambassadors to the pope; one, Antonio Manuel ne Vunda, died in Rome in 1608 and is commemorated by a bust in the church of Santa Maria Maggiore. Capuchin missionaries worked in Kongo in the seventeenth century, and their accounts describe the kingdom.

## The Jaga and the Portuguese

In 1568 Jaga warriors invaded and sacked Mbanza Kongo, and the king appealed to Portugal, which sent troops. The Portuguese founded Luanda in 1576 and built the colony of Angola to the south, which became a major source of enslaved people. Conflict between Kongo and Angola grew. Kongo allied with the Dutch in the 1640s against Portugal.

## Mbwila and civil war

At the Battle of Mbwila on 29 October 1665, Portuguese and allied forces defeated Kongo's army and killed King António I, whose head was taken to Luanda. Rival claimants, based in different provinces, fought for the throne, and Mbanza Kongo was abandoned in 1678. The wars produced many captives, who were sold into the slave trade; many enslaved Africans taken to the Americas came from Kongo.

## Kimpa Vita

Around 1704, a young noblewoman, Kimpa Vita, claimed to be possessed by Saint Anthony and began the Antonian movement, preaching that Jesus and the saints were Kongolese and calling for the restoration of the kingdom at Mbanza Kongo. Thousands followed her. She was captured and burned as a heretic in 1706 on the orders of King Pedro IV, who reunited the kingdom by 1709.

## Later history

Kongo survived as a smaller, decentralised kingdom. In the late nineteenth century, it became part of Portuguese Angola, with parts in the Belgian Congo and French Congo after the Berlin Conference. After a revolt in 1913–1914, the Portuguese abolished the kingdom's independence.
