The Safavids were a family of Sufi religious leaders who became the kings of Iran. They ruled from 1501 until 1736 and turned Iran into a Shia Muslim country, the most lasting change of their era.

## The Sufi order

Sheikh Safi al-Din of Ardabil, who died in 1334, was a respected Sufi master whose order attracted followers from across Iran, Anatolia and the Caucasus. His descendants led the order and, over time, it became more militant and adopted Shia beliefs. In the fifteenth century, Sheikh Junayd and his son Haydar led followers in raids against Christian Georgia and Circassia. Haydar gave his followers a distinctive red hat with twelve folds, honouring the twelve imams, which gave them the name Qizilbash. Both Junayd and Haydar were killed in battle.

## Ismail, the boy king

Haydar's son Ismail was a child when his father died, and spent years in hiding in Gilan, protected by followers. In 1499, at about twelve, he emerged with a small band of Qizilbash and in 1501 took Tabriz and declared himself shah. His followers regarded him as a semi-divine figure, and he wrote poems in Turkish, under the pen name Khatai, in which he described himself in exalted religious terms. He conquered most of Iran within a decade.

## Imposing Shia Islam

Ismail declared Twelver Shiism the official religion and required public cursing of the first three caliphs, revered by Sunnis. Because Iran had few Shia scholars, the Safavids invited them from Jabal Amil, in today's Lebanon, Bahrain and Iraq. Over the following two centuries, Iran became predominantly Shia, a defining feature of its identity to this day. Sunni scholars and Sufi orders that resisted were suppressed.

## Tahmasp

Ismail's son Tahmasp I reigned for 52 years, from 1524 to 1576, the longest of any Safavid. He fought the Ottomans and the Uzbeks and moved the capital to Qazvin. In the Peace of Amasya of 1555, he and Suleiman the Magnificent agreed borders. Tahmasp sheltered the Mughal emperor Humayun, who had lost his throne. A patron of painting, he commissioned the magnificent illustrated Shahnameh of Shah Tahmasp, but later grew strict and religious.

## Abbas the Great

After a period of civil war among the Qizilbash, Abbas I took the throne in 1588. He created a new army of ghulams, Georgian, Armenian and Circassian slave soldiers, and musketeers and artillery, reducing the power of the Qizilbash. He recovered Tabriz and Baghdad from the Ottomans and Herat from the Uzbeks. He moved the capital to Isfahan in 1598 and rebuilt it, with the vast Naqsh-e Jahan square, the Shah Mosque, the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, the Ali Qapu palace and the Chaharbagh avenue. He also blinded or killed several of his own sons, fearing plots.

## The later shahs

Abbas's successors were raised in the harem rather than trained in government. The Safavid state remained prosperous under Abbas II, but under Sultan Husayn (1694–1722) the government grew weak and religiously intolerant. In 1722 Afghan forces from Kandahar took Isfahan after a long siege, and Husayn surrendered. His son Tahmasp II continued as a nominal shah under the general Nader, who deposed the last Safavid, Abbas III, and crowned himself in 1736.
