According to Roman tradition, the people of Rome expelled their last king in 509 BCE and resolved never to have another. For nearly five centuries afterwards the city was governed by elected magistrates, above all two consuls chosen every year, advised by a senate of former officials. Under that system a city-state on the Tiber came to rule the Mediterranean.

## How the Republic worked

Power was spread out on purpose. Consuls held office for a year and checked each other. The senate had no formal power to make law but enormous influence over policy, money and war. Assemblies of citizens elected magistrates and passed laws, and the tribunes of the plebs could veto acts against ordinary citizens. In practice a small group of wealthy families held most of the senior offices: it was an oligarchy with elections rather than a democracy.

## Patricians and plebeians

Early Roman society was divided between patricians, a small group of old aristocratic families, and plebeians, everyone else. In the Conflict of the Orders, which lasted about two centuries, the plebeians used the threat of mass withdrawal from the city to win rights: their own officials, the tribunes; a written law code, the Twelve Tables, around 450 BCE; access to the consulship from 367 BCE; and, in 287 BCE, laws passed by their own assembly binding on all Romans. By then a new nobility of rich patrician and plebeian families had formed.

## Citizens and soldiers

Roman citizens had the right to vote, to appeal against magistrates and to a fair trial. In return, men with property served in the legions. The Roman army was organised in legions of heavy infantry, arranged in flexible lines of maniples, which proved more adaptable than the Macedonian phalanx. Rome's great strength was manpower: it granted citizenship or alliance to conquered Italian communities, which then supplied troops, allowing it to survive defeats that would have destroyed other states.

## Family and religion

The Roman family was headed by the paterfamilias, who in law had great power over his children, even adult sons. Romans honoured their ancestors, keeping wax masks of distinguished forebears in their homes. Religion was a public duty: priests and augurs, drawn from the same elite, performed sacrifices and read the will of the gods in the flight of birds and the entrails of animals before any major decision. Rome adopted gods from Etruscans and Greeks, identifying Jupiter with Zeus and Minerva with Athena.

## Rome and Italy

After the expulsion of the kings, Rome fought its Latin neighbours, the Etruscan city of Veii, which it destroyed in 396 BCE, and the Gauls, who sacked Rome around 390 BCE. In three Samnite Wars, from 343 to 290 BCE, Rome conquered central Italy, and it defeated King Pyrrhus of Epirus, who won costly "Pyrrhic" victories, in 275 BCE. Roman roads, beginning with the Via Appia in 312 BCE, and colonies of settlers tied Italy together.

## Empire overseas

The Punic Wars brought Rome its first provinces: Sicily, Sardinia and Spain. In the second century BCE Rome defeated Macedon and the Seleucids, and in 146 BCE destroyed both Carthage and Corinth. Provinces were governed by former magistrates, who often enriched themselves, and tax collection was farmed out to companies of publicani. Huge numbers of enslaved captives were brought to Italy to work on large estates, latifundia, displacing small farmers. Slave revolts followed, the largest led by Spartacus in 73–71 BCE.

## The Gracchi and the generals

In 133 BCE the tribune Tiberius Gracchus proposed redistributing public land to poor citizens and was killed by senators. His brother Gaius met a similar fate in 121 BCE. The general Gaius Marius, consul seven times, recruited landless volunteers, who looked to their generals for land and pay. The Social War of 91–87 BCE forced Rome to grant citizenship to its Italian allies. Sulla marched on Rome with his army in 88 BCE and later made himself dictator, publishing lists of enemies to be killed, the proscriptions.

## The last generation

In the following decades, Pompey, Crassus and Caesar dominated politics. Cicero, consul in 63 BCE, foiled a conspiracy led by Catiline and became the most famous orator of the age; his speeches and letters are a rich source for the period. Caesar's civil war against Pompey and the senate, his dictatorship and assassination, and the wars between Antony and Octavian brought the Republic to an end.

## Legacy

The Roman Republic, with its senate, elected magistrates and checks on power, inspired later republics. The founders of the United States studied it closely, and the American Senate and the word "republic" itself recall it.

## On our maps

Our maps follow the Republic from 500 to 100 BCE. At first it is little more than Rome; by the 100 BCE map it covers about 1.2 million square kilometres, with nearly a third of that in Spain and much of the rest in Italy, western Turkey, Greece and Tunisia. That date is just before the great expansion of the first century BCE: Caesar's conquest of Gaul, for instance, came between 58 and 50 BCE.
