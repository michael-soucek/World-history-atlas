The Wari state was one of the first empires of the Andes. From its capital, near today's Ayacucho in the Peruvian highlands, it spread its control and influence across much of Peru between about 600 and 1000 CE, centuries before the Incas, who built on some of its achievements.

## Origins

The Ayacucho valley had been home to farming communities for centuries, including the Huarpa culture. Around 600 CE, Wari grew rapidly into a large city, influenced by contacts with the coast, especially the Nasca culture, famous for its lines in the desert, and with Tiwanaku, near Lake Titicaca, from which Wari adopted religious imagery.

## The capital

The city of Wari covered about 15 square kilometres, with a core of about 2 square kilometres, and may have had tens of thousands of inhabitants. It was built of large, walled rectangular compounds of rough stone, some several storeys high, with courtyards and rooms, as well as D-shaped temples, used for rituals and offerings. Underground galleries held elite burials, though many have been looted.

## An imperial network

Wari built administrative centres across the Peruvian highlands, such as Pikillacta, near Cusco, a vast planned complex of identical rooms and courtyards arranged on a grid, with over 700 buildings, perhaps used for storage, administration and housing workers; Viracochapampa, in the north; and Cerro Baúl, in the south, near Tiwanaku's colonies. These centres, linked by roads that the Incas later used, suggest an empire that controlled labour and production. Wari promoted terraced farming and irrigation in highland valleys, increasing maize production.

## Records and feasts

Wari may have used khipus, knotted cords used by the Incas to record numbers and perhaps other information; early khipus have been found at Wari sites. Large feasts with chicha, maize beer, brewed in large vessels, were important for building loyalty and alliances; at Cerro Baúl, archaeologists found a brewery and evidence of a final feast before the site was ritually burned around 1000 CE.

## Art

Wari art featured the "Staff God", a figure holding two staffs, also seen at Tiwanaku, as well as winged attendants and animals. Wari weavers made exceptionally fine tapestry tunics, with complex geometric and abstract designs, among the finest textiles of the ancient Americas, worn by the elite. Large ceramic vessels, painted with figures, were deliberately smashed as offerings.

## El Castillo de Huarmey

In 2013, archaeologists led by Miłosz Giersz found an unlooted royal tomb at El Castillo de Huarmey, on the north coast, containing the remains of 58 noblewomen, some probably queens, buried with gold and silver jewellery, earrings, weaving tools and fine textiles, along with sacrificed attendants. The find showed Wari's reach to the coast and the important role of elite women.

## Decline

Wari declined around 1000 CE. A long drought, internal conflict and the breakdown of the system of feasting and alliances may have played a part. Its administrative centres were abandoned, some deliberately burned. The highlands broke up into smaller states and chiefdoms until the rise of the Incas in the fifteenth century. Wari's roads, administrative centres, storage systems, terracing and possibly khipus prefigured the Inca Empire's methods of rule.
