The Portuguese Empire was the first of the European overseas empires and the last to end. It began with the capture of Ceuta in North Africa in 1415 and ended with the handover of Macau to China in 1999, nearly six centuries later.

## Ships and navigation

Portugal's success rested on maritime technology and knowledge. The caravel, a light ship with lateen sails, could sail closer to the wind than earlier ships, and later the larger carrack carried cargo on long voyages. Portuguese navigators learned to use the astrolabe and quadrant to measure latitude, and developed the volta do mar, sailing far out into the Atlantic to catch favourable winds. Mapmakers kept records of the coasts discovered, and the crown kept them secret.

## Africa

The Portuguese built forts and trading posts along the African coast, such as Elmina in today's Ghana, founded in 1482, which traded for gold. They established relations with the Kingdom of Kongo, whose king converted to Christianity. From the fifteenth century, Portugal was also deeply involved in the slave trade, first to Europe and the Atlantic islands, and then to Brazil. Angola, centred on Luanda from 1576, became a major source of enslaved people.

## The Estado da Índia

After Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, Portugal tried to control the spice trade by force. Afonso de Albuquerque, governor from 1509, captured Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511 and Hormuz in 1515, creating a network of fortified ports called the Estado da Índia, governed from Goa. The Portuguese used their naval power to demand that Asian ships buy licences, cartazes. They reached China, settling in Macau in 1557, and Japan in 1543, where Jesuits converted many and Portuguese merchants carried silk between China and Japan.

## Brazil

Brazil became Portugal's most valuable colony. Sugar plantations in the north-east, worked by enslaved Africans, made it the world's largest sugar producer in the seventeenth century. The discovery of gold and diamonds in Minas Gerais in the 1690s brought a gold rush and great wealth to Portugal, funding buildings such as the Mafra palace. Brazil's capital moved to Rio de Janeiro in 1763.

## Union with Spain and decline in Asia

From 1580 to 1640, Portugal was ruled by the Spanish kings. During this time, the Dutch and English attacked Portuguese possessions in Asia, Africa and Brazil. The Dutch took Malacca in 1641 and Ceylon in the 1650s, and occupied north-eastern Brazil until 1654. After independence was restored in 1640, Portugal kept Brazil, Angola, Mozambique, Goa, Macau and Timor.

## The nineteenth and twentieth centuries

After Brazil's independence in 1822, Portugal focused on Africa. In the Scramble for Africa, its claim to the territory between Angola and Mozambique was rejected by Britain in the 1890 ultimatum. Under Salazar's Estado Novo, Portugal insisted that its colonies were "overseas provinces". India took Goa by force in 1961. Wars of independence in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea-Bissau from 1961 ended with the Carnation Revolution of 1974. East Timor was invaded by Indonesia in 1975, and Macau returned to China in 1999.

## Legacy

Portuguese is spoken by over 250 million people today, in Portugal, Brazil, Angola, Mozambique and other countries. The empire's legacy includes mixed cultures, architecture and the deep scars of slavery and colonial wars.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on most maps from 1492 to 1994. Its largest outline, on the 1945 map, is about 2.1 million square kilometres, almost all of it Angola and Mozambique. Brazil is often drawn under its own label on our maps, so these figures leave it out at many dates. Rio de Janeiro and Salvador are Portuguese on our maps from 1650 to the 1870s, and Luanda in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
