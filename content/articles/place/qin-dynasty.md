The Qin dynasty lasted only fifteen years, from 221 to 206 BCE, but it created China as a unified empire. Its founder, who called himself the First Emperor, conquered the rival Warring States and imposed a single system of government on them, and the Western name "China" probably comes from Qin.

## The rise of Qin

Qin began as a small state on the western frontier of the Zhou world, in the Wei river valley, raising horses and fighting nomadic peoples. Other Zhou states considered it semi-barbarian. Its position, protected by mountains and passes, made it hard to attack. In the fourth century BCE, Duke Xiao of Qin appointed Shang Yang, who carried out radical reforms: abolishing hereditary aristocratic privileges, rewarding soldiers by the number of enemy heads they cut off, organising households into groups that were collectively punished for crimes, encouraging farming and dividing the state into counties. The Zhengguo canal, completed around 246 BCE, irrigated large areas and increased harvests.

## Li Si and Legalism

Qin's rulers followed Legalism, a philosophy that held that people are motivated by rewards and punishments, and that the state should use strict laws to maintain order and strength. Li Si, a student of the philosopher Xunzi, became chancellor and designed many of the First Emperor's policies. Han Feizi, the leading Legalist thinker, was imprisoned and poisoned in Qin in 233 BCE, reportedly through Li Si's jealousy.

## Unification

Between 230 and 221 BCE, Qin conquered Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan and Qi. The First Emperor divided the empire into 36 commanderies, later more, each run by a civil governor, a military commander and an inspector, all appointed by the centre. Former aristocratic families were moved to the capital, Xianyang. Weapons were collected and melted down to make twelve giant bronze statues.

## Standardisation

The First Emperor standardised the Chinese script, adopting the small seal script and abolishing regional variations, which helped unify Chinese culture. Coins, weights and measures, and the width of cart axles were standardised. An empire-wide network of roads, the Straight Road and imperial highways, and canals, such as the Lingqu canal linking the Yangtze and Pearl river systems, was built.

## Expansion and walls

General Meng Tian drove the Xiongnu from the Ordos region and linked earlier walls into a long northern frontier wall. Qin armies conquered the south, reaching Guangdong and northern Vietnam. These projects required hundreds of thousands of labourers, and many died.

## Burning of books

In 213 BCE, on Li Si's advice, the emperor ordered the burning of historical records of other states and works of philosophy and poetry, except those in the imperial library and works on medicine, divination and farming. According to the historian Sima Qian, in 212 BCE, 460 scholars were buried alive. Some modern historians doubt or reinterpret parts of this account, which was written under the Han, who had reasons to portray the Qin badly.

## The terracotta army

The First Emperor was obsessed with immortality. His vast tomb near Xi'an is guarded by an army of over 7,000 life-size terracotta soldiers, with horses and chariots, each figure with individual features. Farmers digging a well discovered them in 1974.

## Collapse

After the First Emperor died in 210 BCE, the eunuch Zhao Gao and Li Si forged his will, forcing the heir, Fusu, to commit suicide and putting the younger son Huhai on the throne as the Second Emperor. Zhao Gao later had Li Si executed. In 209 BCE, Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, conscripts who faced execution for arriving late because of rain, led the first revolt. Rebellions spread, and in 206 BCE the last Qin ruler surrendered to Liu Bang. Xiang Yu burned Xianyang.

## Legacy

Despite its brief rule, the Qin created the model of a unified, centralised Chinese state that lasted until 1912.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Qin on the 323 and 300 BCE maps, before unification, as the kingdom of Qin in the west at about 590,000 square kilometres, with Xi'an's site inside it. The unified empire of 221–206 BCE falls between our 300 and 200 BCE maps; by 200 BCE the region belongs to the [Han dynasty](/place/han-dynasty).
