Switzerland grew out of an alliance of mountain communities in the Alps. According to tradition, in 1291 the valleys of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden swore a pact of mutual defence; the date is now Swiss National Day, 1 August. Over the following centuries other towns and regions joined, forming a confederation of largely self-governing cantons.

## The early confederacy

In the thirteenth century, the Habsburgs tried to strengthen their control over the Alpine valleys around Lake Lucerne. The communities of Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden, which controlled routes to the Gotthard Pass, allied for mutual defence; the oldest surviving charter dates from 1291. The legend of William Tell, forced by a Habsburg bailiff to shoot an apple off his son's head, became a symbol of Swiss freedom. Lucerne, Zurich, Glarus, Zug and Bern joined in the fourteenth century.

## Victories and expansion

The Swiss infantry, with pikes and halberds, defeated Habsburg knights at Morgarten in 1315 and Sempach in 1386, where, according to legend, Arnold von Winkelried sacrificed himself to break the enemy line. In the Burgundian Wars, the Swiss defeated Charles the Bold at Grandson, Murten and Nancy in 1476–1477. In the Swabian War of 1499, they won virtual independence from the Holy Roman Empire. After defeat at Marignano in 1515, the Confederacy adopted a policy of avoiding wars of expansion, though Swiss mercenaries continued to serve foreign rulers.

## Reformation and division

Huldrych Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich from 1519, and John Calvin made Geneva a Protestant centre from 1541, attracting refugees from France, Italy and England. Catholic and Protestant cantons fought civil wars, the Kappel Wars, in which Zwingli was killed in 1531. Religious division shaped Swiss politics for centuries, but the Confederacy survived.

## The Helvetic Republic

In 1798 the French invaded and set up the Helvetic Republic, a centralised state, which abolished the subject territories ruled by the cantons. After much unrest, Napoleon's Act of Mediation of 1803 restored a federal system. The Congress of Vienna in 1815 recognised Switzerland's permanent neutrality and added Geneva, Valais and Neuchâtel.

## The federal state

In the Sonderbund War of 1847, a short civil war, liberal Protestant cantons defeated a league of seven Catholic cantons. The constitution of 1848 created a federal state with a parliament, a seven-member executive, the Federal Council, and Bern as its seat. A revised constitution in 1874 introduced the referendum, and the popular initiative followed in 1891, making Switzerland a leading example of direct democracy.

## Industry and neutrality

Switzerland industrialised in textiles, watchmaking, chemicals and machinery. Railways, tunnels such as the Gotthard tunnel, opened in 1882, and tourism developed. The Red Cross was founded in Geneva by Henri Dunant in 1863. During the Second World War, Switzerland remained neutral, mobilised its army and took in some refugees but turned others away; its banks' dealings with Nazi Germany were later criticised. Women gained the federal vote only in 1971, and the last canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, was forced to grant them the cantonal vote in 1990.

## Modern Switzerland

Switzerland has four national languages, German, French, Italian and Romansh, and 26 cantons. It is one of the world's richest countries, a centre of banking, pharmaceuticals and international organisations, such as the UN's European headquarters, the World Health Organization and CERN. It joined the United Nations in 2002 but remains outside the European Union.
