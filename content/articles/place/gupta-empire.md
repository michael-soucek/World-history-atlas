The Gupta Empire ruled much of northern India from the early fourth century CE to the mid-sixth. It is often called a golden age of Indian civilisation, a label some historians now question, but the period's achievements in mathematics, literature and art are not in doubt.

## Kingship and religion

The Gupta kings presented themselves as great Hindu monarchs. Samudragupta performed the ancient horse sacrifice, ashvamedha, to proclaim his supremacy, and issued gold coins showing the sacrificial horse. The Guptas favoured the worship of Vishnu, whose emblem, the bird Garuda, appeared on their seals. But they also supported Buddhist and Jain institutions, and the great Buddhist university of Nalanda was founded under Kumaragupta I in the fifth century.

## Samudragupta and Chandragupta II

The Allahabad pillar inscription, written by the court poet Harisena, describes Samudragupta's campaigns: kings of the north he "uprooted", kings of the south he defeated and then restored as vassals, and frontier states that paid him tribute. Samudragupta is also shown on his coins playing a lute. Chandragupta II, known as Vikramaditya, defeated the Shaka rulers of western India around 400, gaining the ports of Gujarat. He also made a marriage alliance with the Vakatakas of the Deccan. Later legend made Vikramaditya an ideal king, surrounded by nine great scholars, the "nine gems", including Kalidasa.

## Faxian's account

The Chinese Buddhist monk Faxian travelled in India from about 399 to 412 to collect scriptures. He described the Gupta lands as peaceful and prosperous, with light punishments, free hospitals in Pataliputra and generous support for monasteries, and noted that respectable people did not eat meat or drink alcohol. His account is one of the main foreign sources on the period.

## Mathematics and astronomy

Aryabhata, born in 476, wrote the Aryabhatiya, which gave a value of pi of about 3.1416, worked on trigonometry, and explained eclipses by the shadows of the earth and moon rather than by demons. Varahamihira, in the sixth century, wrote a great compendium of astronomy and astrology. The decimal place-value notation, with a symbol for zero, was developing in this period; the earliest certain use of zero as a number appears in the work of Brahmagupta in 628, shortly after the Gupta era.

## Literature and art

Kalidasa, the greatest classical Sanskrit poet and playwright, wrote the drama Shakuntala and the poem The Cloud Messenger. The Puranas, collections of myths and genealogies, and the law codes of Narada and others took shape. The Kama Sutra is usually dated to this era. Gupta sculpture, such as the seated Buddha from Sarnath, and the early cave temples at Udayagiri set enduring standards. The paintings at Ajanta, though made under the Vakatakas, belong to the same artistic world.

## Economy and society

The Guptas issued fine gold coins, a sign of a wealthy economy, and India traded with the Roman and later Byzantine world, Southeast Asia and China. Land grants to Brahmins and temples became more common, which some historians see as a step towards a more decentralised, feudal order. The caste system became more rigid, and the position of women appears to have declined.

## The end of the Guptas

The Alchon Huns, from Central Asia, attacked the north-west from the 460s. Skandagupta defeated them, but later rulers lost Punjab and central India to the Hun kings Toramana and Mihirakula in the early sixth century. Local dynasties such as the Maukharis and the later Guptas of Magadha took over, and in the seventh century Harsha briefly reunited much of the north.
