Chimor, the kingdom of the Chimú people, was the largest state on the coast of Peru before the Incas. From its capital, Chan Chan, it controlled about 1,000 kilometres of desert coastline in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, until the Incas conquered it around 1470.

## Heirs of the Moche

The Chimú lived in the Moche valley, the same region as the earlier Moche culture, whose traditions of irrigation, metalwork and pottery they inherited. Between the Moche and the Chimú, the Lambayeque or Sicán culture flourished to the north, famous for its gold masks and its founder legend of Naymlap, who arrived by sea. Chimú culture took shape around 900 CE.

## Taycanamo

According to an oral tradition recorded by the Spanish, the dynasty's founder, Taycanamo, arrived in the Moche valley on a raft of balsa logs, saying he had been sent by a great lord to rule. He and his descendants built the kingdom. Spanish sources list several rulers, but the details are uncertain.

## Chan Chan

Chan Chan, near today's Trujillo, was the largest adobe city in the Americas and one of the largest pre-industrial cities anywhere, covering about twenty square kilometres. At its heart were about ten huge rectangular compounds, the ciudadelas, surrounded by walls up to nine metres high. Each contained courtyards, storerooms, audience rooms called audiencias, wells and a burial platform. Many archaeologists believe each was the palace of a king, built during his reign, and became his tomb and the centre of his cult after his death, with his successor building a new one. The walls were decorated with reliefs of fish, seabirds, waves, nets and geometric patterns. Around the compounds lived nobles, and in simpler quarters artisans, perhaps tens of thousands of them, worked metals, textiles and wood.

## Water and farming

The Chimú built an extensive irrigation system, with canals linking valleys, such as the La Cumbre canal, about 80 kilometres long, designed to bring water from the Chicama valley to the Moche valley. They also dug sunken gardens, wachaques, reaching down to the water table. They farmed maize, beans, squash, cotton and other crops, and fished the rich waters of the Pacific.

## Crafts

Chimú craftsmen were famous for metalwork in gold, silver and copper, such as ceremonial knives, tumi, beakers and ear ornaments, and for fine textiles, including feathered cloth. Their pottery was mostly black, made in moulds. After the conquest, the Incas brought many Chimú metalworkers to Cusco.

## Expansion

From the fourteenth century, under kings such as Ñancen-pinco and later Minchançaman, Chimor expanded north to Tumbes, near today's border with Ecuador, and south towards the Lima region, conquering the Lambayeque valley around 1370. It ruled through governors and local lords.

## Sacrifice

At Huanchaquito-Las Llamas, near Chan Chan, archaeologists have found the remains of over 140 children and over 200 young llamas sacrificed around 1450, one of the largest known mass child sacrifices in the Americas, possibly in response to flooding caused by an El Niño event.

## Inca conquest

Around 1470, the Inca prince Topa Inca Yupanqui conquered Chimor. According to tradition, the Incas cut off the water supply to Chan Chan. The king Minchançaman was taken to Cusco, and his son was made a vassal ruler. The Incas took Chan Chan's treasures and craftsmen. Chan Chan is now a World Heritage Site, threatened by erosion and rain.
