The Zhou was the longest-lasting dynasty in Chinese history, ruling, at least in name, for about eight centuries, from around 1046 to 256 BCE. In that time China's classical culture took shape: Confucius, Laozi and Sunzi all lived under the Zhou, and its ideas of kingship shaped Chinese politics for three thousand years.

## The conquest of the Shang

The Shang dynasty ruled the Yellow River valley from around 1600 to 1046 BCE, and is known from oracle bones, inscribed with the earliest Chinese writing. The Zhou, from the Wei river valley, rose under King Wen and his son King Wu, who defeated the last Shang king, said to be cruel and decadent, at the Battle of Muye around 1046 BCE. The Duke of Zhou, Wu's brother, acted as regent for the young King Cheng, and was later revered by Confucius as a model of virtue.

## The feudal system

The early Zhou kings granted lands to relatives and allies, who ruled them as lords, owed the king loyalty and military service, and performed rituals in his honour. Bronze vessels, used in ancestral rituals, carried inscriptions recording royal gifts and appointments, and are a main source for Zhou history. The Mandate of Heaven justified Zhou rule and later became a principle for every Chinese dynasty.

## The Spring and Autumn period

After 771 BCE, when the Western Zhou capital fell to the Quanrong nomads and rebel lords, the Eastern Zhou kings ruled from Luoyang with little power. The period from 770 to 476 BCE, named after the Spring and Autumn Annals, a chronicle of the state of Lu, saw over a hundred states compete. Powerful states, such as Qi, Jin, Chu, Wu and Yue, took turns as hegemon, ba, leading alliances in the king's name.

## The Warring States

From 475 to 221 BCE, seven large states fought for supremacy: Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Han, Zhao and Wei. Warfare changed: massed infantry armies of conscripted peasants, iron weapons, crossbows and cavalry replaced aristocratic chariot warfare, and battles could involve hundreds of thousands. States built walls along their borders. Reforms strengthened states: Shang Yang's in Qin made it the most powerful. Iron tools and irrigation increased farming, and coins, in the shape of knives and spades, were used.

## The Hundred Schools

The period produced China's great philosophical traditions. Confucius taught ethics and good government; his follower Mencius argued that human nature is good. Laozi, traditionally the author of the Daodejing, and Zhuangzi taught Daoism, harmony with the natural way. Mozi taught universal love and opposed offensive war. The Legalists, such as Han Feizi, argued for strict laws and strong rulers. Sunzi's Art of War dealt with strategy.

## Literature and culture

The Book of Songs, a collection of poems from the Western Zhou and Spring and Autumn periods, the Book of Documents and the Book of Changes, the Yijing, became Confucian classics. The poet Qu Yuan of Chu, who drowned himself around 278 BCE, is remembered in the Dragon Boat Festival. The tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng, from around 433 BCE, contained a set of 65 bronze bells.

## The end

In 256 BCE Qin deposed the last Zhou king, King Nan, and in 221 BCE Qin unified China.

## On our maps

Our maps use the name from 500 to 300 BCE for China's Warring States as a whole, with the largest outline, about 1.9 million square kilometres, on the 323 BCE map. Beijing's, Shanghai's and Nanjing's sites lie inside it. The basemap's outline stands for the world of Zhou states rather than a single kingdom under the king's control.
