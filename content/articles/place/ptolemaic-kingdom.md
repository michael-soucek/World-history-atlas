The Ptolemaic Kingdom was the last dynasty to rule ancient Egypt as an independent kingdom, though its kings were Macedonian Greeks. Ptolemy, one of Alexander the Great's closest companions, took Egypt after Alexander died in 323 BCE and declared himself king in 305 BCE. His descendants ruled for almost three hundred years, and the last of them was Cleopatra VII.

## Ptolemy I

Ptolemy, son of Lagus, was a Macedonian noble and boyhood friend of Alexander. After Alexander's death he took Egypt as satrap and secured his position by hijacking Alexander's body as it was being taken to Macedon, burying it first at Memphis and later in Alexandria, where the tomb became a place of pilgrimage. Ptolemy fought off invasions by rival generals, took the title of king in 305 BCE and wrote a history of Alexander's campaigns, later used by the historian Arrian. He was given the name Soter, "saviour", by the people of Rhodes, whom he helped against a siege.

## Ruling Egypt

The Ptolemies ran Egypt as an efficient, centralised state geared to producing revenue. Royal officials registered land, livestock and people, controlled the production of oil, beer and papyrus, and collected taxes, often through tax farmers. Thousands of Greek papyri, preserved in the dry sands of the Fayum, show these details: the archive of Zenon, estate manager for the finance minister Apollonius in the third century BCE, is one of the richest records of everyday life in antiquity. Greek and Macedonian soldiers were given plots of land in return for military service.

## Two peoples

Greeks and Egyptians largely lived under different laws and courts, and Greeks dominated the top of society. But the two cultures mixed. Many Egyptians learned Greek, some Greeks adopted Egyptian religion and names, and there were mixed marriages. Ptolemy I promoted the god Serapis, combining the Egyptian Osiris-Apis with Greek features, whose cult spread across the Mediterranean. The priest Manetho wrote a history of Egypt in Greek, dividing it into the thirty dynasties still used today.

## Ptolemy II and the golden age

Ptolemy II Philadelphus, who ruled from 283 to 246 BCE, married his full sister Arsinoe II, beginning the dynasty's custom of brother-sister marriage, and the two were worshipped as gods. Under him, Alexandria flourished: the Pharos lighthouse, one of the Seven Wonders of the World, was completed, and the Library grew. Ptolemy II held a famous procession with elephants, golden statues and exotic animals that displayed the dynasty's wealth. Ptolemaic power extended to Cyprus, the Levant, Cyrenaica and parts of the Aegean.

## The Museum and Library

The Museum, a research institution dedicated to the Muses, and the Library attracted scholars from the whole Greek world. The kings collected books aggressively, reportedly copying every book found on ships in the harbour. Scholars there included the mathematician Euclid, the astronomer Aristarchus, the geographer Eratosthenes, who estimated the earth's circumference, the doctors Herophilus and Erasistratus, who studied human anatomy, and poets such as Callimachus and Theocritus. The library's eventual destruction, over several centuries, has become a symbol of lost knowledge.

## Decline

After the reign of Ptolemy III, the kingdom weakened. At the Battle of Raphia in 217 BCE, Ptolemy IV defeated the Seleucids with the help of Egyptian troops trained as a phalanx, and Egyptian revolts followed in the south, where rebel pharaohs ruled Thebes for about twenty years. The Rosetta Stone, a decree of 196 BCE written in hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek for the boy-king Ptolemy V, comes from this period; its discovery in 1799 made it possible to decipher hieroglyphs. Later Ptolemies fought each other in bitter family wars, and Rome increasingly decided who ruled.

## Cleopatra's reign

Cleopatra VII came to the throne in 51 BCE with her brother Ptolemy XIII. She was an able ruler who managed famine and floods, and her alliance with Caesar and then Antony aimed to restore Ptolemaic power in the eastern Mediterranean; Antony granted her territories in the Levant and Cyprus. Her defeat at Actium ended this ambition.

## Under Rome's shadow

From the second century BCE the kingdom survived increasingly on Roman goodwill. Cleopatra VII tried to turn that dependence into partnership, first with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony. After their defeat at Actium in 31 BCE, Octavian took Alexandria, and Antony and Cleopatra died by suicide in 30 BCE. Egypt became a Roman province, governed directly on behalf of the emperor. On our 1 BCE map it is Roman.

## Legacy

Alexandria remained one of the great cities of the Roman and Byzantine world and a centre of learning, of Jewish and later Christian thought. Many Ptolemaic temples, such as those at Edfu, Dendera, Kom Ombo and Philae, are among the best-preserved in Egypt.

## On our maps

Our maps show the kingdom from 300 to 100 BCE. Even at its largest, on the 100 BCE map, it covers about 600,000 square kilometres, and around 80 percent of that is in today's Egypt; the rest is Cyrenaica in Libya and the southern Levant. Jerusalem, Damascus and Beirut are Ptolemaic on our maps in this period, reflecting a century of wars with the Seleucids for control of Syria and Palestine.
