The Minoans built Europe's first civilisation with palaces, writing and long-distance trade, on the island of Crete between about 3000 and 1450 BCE. We do not know what they called themselves: the name "Minoan" was coined by the archaeologist Arthur Evans after Minos, the legendary king of Crete in Greek myth.

## Knossos and Arthur Evans

The palace of Knossos, near Heraklion, was excavated from 1900 by the British archaeologist Arthur Evans, who named the civilisation after the legendary King Minos. Evans also reconstructed parts of the palace in concrete, with painted columns, a practice now criticised. Knossos covered about 20,000 square metres, with hundreds of rooms around a central courtyard, several storeys high, with grand staircases, light wells, storerooms and a sophisticated drainage system. The complexity of the plan may lie behind the Greek myth of the Labyrinth and the Minotaur.

## The palaces

The first palaces were built around 1900 BCE and destroyed around 1700 BCE, possibly by earthquakes, then rebuilt on a grander scale. Besides Knossos, there were palaces at Phaistos, Malia, Zakros and Galatas. Phaistos is famous for the Phaistos Disc, a clay disc stamped with signs in a spiral, which has never been deciphered and may even be a forgery. The palaces controlled agricultural production, storing grain, olive oil and wine in huge jars, pithoi, and were centres of crafts.

## Frescoes and art

Minoan frescoes show a vivid, colourful world: the "bull-leaping" fresco from Knossos, showing young men and women vaulting over a bull; the "Prince of the Lilies"; dolphins in the queen's megaron; and women in elaborate dresses, nicknamed "La Parisienne" by excavators. Many were heavily restored. Minoan potters made fine Kamares ware and marine-style pottery decorated with octopuses. Craftsmen made gold jewellery, such as the Malia pendant of two bees, carved stone vessels and seals.

## Religion

Minoan religion seems to have focused on female deities, perhaps a mother goddess. Faience figurines from Knossos, the "snake goddesses", show women holding snakes. Worship took place at peak sanctuaries, in sacred caves and at palace shrines. Symbols included the double axe, labrys, and the "horns of consecration". Some evidence, such as at the site of Anemospilia, has been interpreted as human sacrifice, though this is debated.

## Thera

The eruption of the volcano on the island of Thera, Santorini, was one of the largest in human history. It buried the Minoan-style town of Akrotiri, whose preserved houses and frescoes, such as the "Spring Fresco" with swallows, are remarkable; no bodies were found, suggesting the inhabitants escaped. The eruption is now dated by radiocarbon to around 1600 BCE, earlier than once thought, and the Minoan palaces on Crete continued for over a century afterwards. Some have linked the eruption to the legend of Atlantis.

## Trade and influence

The Minoans traded with Egypt, where Minoan-style frescoes were found at Avaris, the Levant, Cyprus and the Aegean islands, exporting oil, wine, textiles and crafts and importing metals. Thucydides later wrote of a "thalassocracy", a sea empire, of Minos, though the extent of Minoan political control is uncertain.

## The end

Around 1450 BCE most palaces were destroyed by fire. Knossos survived, but under Mycenaean control, as shown by tablets in Linear B, an early form of Greek. Knossos was destroyed around 1375 BCE. Minoan traditions survived in some mountain communities, the Eteocretans, into the first millennium BCE.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Minoan civilisation on the 4000, 3000 and 2000 BCE maps, as an outline of Crete of about 8,000 square kilometres. The earliest map uses the name for the island's Neolithic farming communities, before the palace civilisation developed. On the 1500 BCE map Crete belongs to the Greek world.
