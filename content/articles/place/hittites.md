The Hittites built one of the great empires of the Bronze Age in central Anatolia, in what is now Turkey. From their capital at Hattusa, they ruled much of Anatolia and northern Syria in the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE, rivalling Egypt for power in the Near East.

## Language and writing

The Hittites called their language Nesite, after the city of Nesa, or Kanesh. It is the oldest attested Indo-European language, related to Latin, Greek and Sanskrit, and its decipherment by Bedřich Hrozný in 1915 transformed linguistics. The Hittites wrote in cuneiform, borrowed from Mesopotamia, and also used a hieroglyphic script for the related Luwian language. The archives of Hattusa, with about 30,000 tablets, contain treaties, laws, rituals, prayers, letters and myths, in Hittite, Akkadian, Hurrian, Luwian and other languages.

## Hattusa

The Hittite capital, Hattusa, near the modern village of Boğazkale in central Turkey, was a vast fortified city, with walls stretching about six kilometres, gates decorated with lions and sphinxes, temples and a royal citadel. The nearby rock sanctuary of Yazılıkaya has carved processions of gods and goddesses. Hattusa is a World Heritage Site.

## The Old Kingdom

The Hittite kingdom was founded in the seventeenth century BCE by Hattusili I, who made Hattusa his capital. His grandson Mursili I raided Aleppo and, around 1595 BCE, Babylon, ending the dynasty of Hammurabi. After Mursili's assassination, a period of internal conflict followed. King Telipinu, around 1500 BCE, issued an edict setting rules for royal succession to end the bloodshed.

## The empire

Under Suppiluliuma I, in the fourteenth century BCE, the Hittites became an empire. He destroyed the kingdom of Mitanni and took control of northern Syria, setting up his sons as viceroys at Carchemish and Aleppo. An Egyptian queen, probably Tutankhamun's widow, wrote asking him to send a son to marry her and become pharaoh; the prince was killed on the way. Mursili II and Muwatalli II fought Egypt and the peoples of western Anatolia, and Muwatalli moved the capital temporarily to Tarhuntassa.

## Kadesh and peace

At Kadesh, around 1274 BCE, Muwatalli II's army fought Ramesses II. Both claimed victory, but the Hittites kept control of Syria. Hattusili III, who usurped the throne from his nephew, made a peace treaty with Ramesses around 1259 BCE, and later sent his daughter to marry the pharaoh. His wife, Queen Puduhepa, was a powerful figure who corresponded with Ramesses and had her own seal.

## Law and religion

Hittite laws were less harsh than those of Mesopotamia, often requiring compensation rather than physical punishment. The Hittites called themselves the land of the "thousand gods", adopting gods from conquered peoples, with the storm god Teshub and the sun goddess of Arinna at the head. Their myths include the Kumarbi cycle, which resembles Greek myths about the succession of the gods.

## Collapse and the Neo-Hittites

Around 1180 BCE, the empire collapsed amid drought, famine, attacks by neighbouring peoples, such as the Kaska, and internal conflict. Hattusa was abandoned and burned. In south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria, Neo-Hittite states, such as Carchemish, Malatya and Tabal, continued Hittite traditions, using Luwian hieroglyphs, until the Assyrians conquered them. The "Hittites" of the Bible probably refers to these later peoples.
