The Ilkhanate was the Mongol state in Iran and the Middle East, founded by Genghis Khan's grandson Hülegü in the 1250s. For about eighty years, Mongol rulers governed Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus and much of Anatolia, and in that time they turned from conquerors who sacked Baghdad into Muslim patrons of Persian culture.

## Hülegü's campaign

In 1253 the Great Khan Möngke sent his brother Hülegü west with a large army, including Chinese siege engineers, to complete the conquest of the Islamic lands. In 1256 Hülegü destroyed the mountain fortresses of the Nizari Ismailis, the so-called Assassins, including Alamut. In 1258 he besieged Baghdad, which fell after two weeks; the city was sacked, and the last Abbasid caliph, al-Mustasim, was executed. In 1260 the Mongols took Aleppo and Damascus. When Möngke died in 1259, Hülegü stayed in the conquered lands and founded his own dynasty. The title Ilkhan probably meant "subordinate khan", acknowledging the Great Khan Kublai in China.

## Enemies on every side

Hülegü's advance was stopped in 1260 at Ain Jalut, in Palestine, where the Mamluks of Egypt defeated the army he had left behind. The Ilkhans fought the Mamluks for control of Syria for sixty years, losing again at Homs in 1281 and Marj al-Saffar in 1303, though Ghazan briefly took Damascus in 1299–1300. They also fought their cousins, the Golden Horde, for Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, and the Chagatai Khanate in the east. The Golden Horde allied with the Mamluks against them.

## Seeking European allies

Looking for allies against the Mamluks, the Ilkhans exchanged embassies with the popes and the kings of France and England. Hülegü's wife, Doquz Khatun, was a Nestorian Christian, and Christians hoped for Mongol support. In 1287 Rabban Bar Sauma, a Nestorian monk born near Beijing, travelled to Rome, Paris and Bordeaux on behalf of Ilkhan Arghun, meeting Philip IV of France and Edward I of England, though no alliance resulted.

## Ghazan's conversion and reforms

The early Ilkhans favoured Buddhism and tolerated all religions. In 1295 Ghazan converted to Islam with many of his Mongol followers, and Islam became the state religion. Advised by his vizier Rashid al-Din, a physician of Jewish origin who had converted to Islam, Ghazan reformed taxation to limit abuses, restored agriculture and irrigation, standardised coins, weights and measures, and protected trade. His brother Öljeitü, who succeeded him in 1304, converted to Shia Islam and built a new capital at Soltaniyeh, with a great domed mausoleum, one of the largest brick domes in the world.

## Culture

The Ilkhanate linked Iran with China, and Chinese influences, such as dragons, phoenixes, lotus flowers and landscape styles, entered Persian painting, ceramics and textiles. At Tabriz, Rashid al-Din founded a scholarly quarter, the Rab'-e Rashidi, with a hospital, library and workshops, and wrote the Compendium of Chronicles, a history of the Mongols and of the peoples of the world, from China to Europe. At Maragheh, Hülegü built an observatory for the astronomer Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, whose models of planetary motion anticipated features of Copernicus's work. The Great Mongol Shahnameh, an illustrated copy of Ferdowsi's epic from the 1330s, is a masterpiece of Persian painting.

## The end

Abu Said, the last Ilkhan of Hülegü's line, made peace with the Mamluks in 1323. In the 1330s, the Black Death struck the region. When Abu Said died in 1335 without an heir, perhaps poisoned, rival commanders set up puppet khans, and the realm broke apart among the Jalayirids in Iraq and Azerbaijan, the Chobanids, the Injuids, the Muzaffarids in Fars and the Sarbadars in Khurasan. Timur conquered most of these lands at the end of the fourteenth century.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Ilkhanate on the 1279 and 1300 maps, largest on the second at about 4.1 million square kilometres. Nearly 40 percent of that is in today's Iran, with large shares in Turkey, Afghanistan, Turkmenistan and Iraq. Baghdad, Tehran's site, Isfahan, Tbilisi, Yerevan and Baku are all Ilkhanid on our maps. Before it, much of the area belongs to the Khwarazmian Empire; on the 1400 map it is Timurid.
