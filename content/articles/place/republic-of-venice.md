The Republic of Venice lasted for over a thousand years, from its legendary first doge in 697 to its surrender to Napoleon in 1797. Built on islands in a lagoon at the head of the Adriatic, it became one of the great trading powers of the medieval Mediterranean, with an empire of ports and islands stretching to Crete and Cyprus.

## Origins in the lagoon

Venice was settled by people fleeing the mainland during the invasions that followed the fall of Rome, especially when the Lombards invaded Italy in 568. The islands of the lagoon offered safety. Venice was at first part of the Byzantine Empire, governed by a duke, the doge, elected by the people. According to tradition, the first doge was elected in 697. In 828 Venetian merchants stole the body of Saint Mark from Alexandria, and he became the city's patron saint; the winged lion became its symbol.

## Trade with the east

Venice's wealth came from trade between Europe and the eastern Mediterranean. A golden bull of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I in 1082 gave Venetian merchants freedom from taxes throughout the empire, in return for naval help against the Normans. Venetian convoys of state-owned galleys sailed regularly to Alexandria, Beirut, Constantinople and the Black Sea, bringing back spices, silk, cotton and other goods. Venetian ducats became one of the most trusted currencies in Europe.

## The Fourth Crusade

In 1202 the crusaders could not pay Venice for transport, and the elderly, blind doge Enrico Dandolo agreed to lead the fleet in exchange for their help in capturing the Christian city of Zara. The crusade was then diverted to Constantinople, which was sacked in 1204. Venice took three-eighths of the Byzantine Empire, including Crete, ports in Greece and islands in the Aegean, and the four bronze horses that now stand in St Mark's Basilica.

## Rival of Genoa

Venice fought a series of wars with its rival, Genoa, for control of eastern trade. In the War of Chioggia from 1378 to 1381, the Genoese occupied Chioggia in the lagoon, but the Venetians blockaded and defeated them. Marco Polo was captured by the Genoese in an earlier war, in 1298.

## The government

Venice's government was famous for its stability. The doge was elected for life through a complex system of drawing lots and voting to prevent fraud and factions. Real power lay with councils: the Great Council of all adult male nobles, whose membership was closed in 1297 and recorded in the Golden Book; the Senate; and the Council of Ten, created in 1310, which handled state security and used secret informers. No doge could establish a dynasty, and one, Marino Faliero, was executed in 1355 for plotting to seize power.

## The Arsenal

The Venetian Arsenal, the state shipyard, employed thousands of workers and produced galleys on an assembly line. It could fit out a galley in a single day, as it did for the French king Henry III in 1574.

## Mainland expansion and decline

In the fifteenth century Venice conquered a mainland empire, the Stato da Terra, including Padua, Verona, Vicenza, Brescia and Bergamo. The League of Cambrai, an alliance of European powers, defeated Venice at Agnadello in 1509, but it recovered most of its lands. Venice contributed galleys to the victory over the Ottomans at Lepanto in 1571. But it lost Cyprus and Crete, and the opening of Atlantic trade routes reduced its importance.

## Decline

The Ottoman advance cost Venice its eastern possessions one by one: Cyprus in 1571, Crete in 1669 after a siege of over twenty years, and the Peloponnese in 1718. Atlantic routes reduced its share of trade. When Napoleon's army arrived in 1797 the Great Council voted itself out of existence, and Venice was handed to Austria later that year.

## Culture

Venice was a centre of printing, with the Aldine Press of Aldus Manutius, and of painting, with Bellini, Giorgione, Titian, Tintoretto and Veronese, and later Canaletto. Its music included Monteverdi and Vivaldi, and its carnival was famous throughout Europe.
