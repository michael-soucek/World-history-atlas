The Samanid Empire ruled north-eastern Iran and Central Asia from its capital at Bukhara in the ninth and tenth centuries. A Sunni Muslim dynasty of Persian origin, the Samanids presided over a revival of the Persian language and literature that shaped Persian culture for the next thousand years.

## The dehqans

The Samanids came from the dehqans, the Persian landowning gentry of Central Asia, who preserved Iranian traditions after the Arab conquest. Saman Khuda, the family founder, was from Balkh or nearby and converted to Islam, according to tradition, under the Umayyad governor Asad ibn Abdallah. His grandsons served the Abbasid caliph al-Mamun, who appointed them governors of Samarkand, Ferghana, Shash and Herat in 819.

## Ismail Samani

Ismail ibn Ahmad, who ruled from 892 to 907, united the family's territories and made Bukhara his capital. In 900 he defeated the Saffarid ruler Amr ibn al-Layth, taking Khurasan, and he campaigned against the Turks of the steppe, taking Talas in 893. He was famous for his justice; legend says he tore down Bukhara's walls, declaring that he would be its wall. His mausoleum in Bukhara, built of baked brick in intricate patterns, is one of the oldest and finest buildings of Central Asian Islamic architecture.

## Administration

The Samanids built an efficient administration modelled on the Abbasid caliphate, with departments, diwans, for finance, the army, correspondence and the postal service. Viziers such as al-Balami and al-Jayhani, a geographer, were scholars. The Samanids formally recognised the Abbasid caliph, sending gifts, while ruling independently. They supported Sunni Islam, but tolerated others; for a time, Nasr II favoured Ismaili missionaries, which led to a backlash.

## Bukhara and Samarkand

Bukhara became one of the great cultural centres of the Islamic world. The Samanid library was renowned, and the young Ibn Sina, who had cured the emir Nuh II, was allowed to use it, describing books he had never seen before. Samarkand produced paper and fine pottery with inscriptions in elegant Kufic script. The geographer al-Muqaddasi praised the Samanid lands.

## The Persian revival

The Samanids promoted New Persian as a literary language. The poet Rudaki, known as the father of Persian poetry, served at Nasr II's court. Translations of al-Tabari's history and commentary on the Quran into Persian were made under Mansur I. The poet Daqiqi began the Shahnameh, which Ferdowsi later completed. Persian became the language of culture across Iran and Central Asia, and later the Ottoman and Mughal courts.

## The silver trade

The Samanids minted large numbers of silver dirhams, made from silver mined in the Panjshir valley and elsewhere. Trade with the Volga Bulgars and the Rus' carried these coins north, and tens of thousands have been found in hoards in Russia, Scandinavia and the Baltic, especially on the Swedish island of Gotland. The Samanids also profited from the trade in Turkish slaves from the steppe.

## Fall

In the second half of the tenth century, Turkish military commanders, such as Alp Tigin, gained power, and rivalries weakened the state. The Ghaznavids took the south, and the Karakhanids took Bukhara in 999. The last Samanid, Ismail Muntasir, fought on until he was killed in 1005.
