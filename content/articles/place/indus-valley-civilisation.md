The Indus Valley Civilisation was the largest of the world's first urban civilisations. Between about 2600 and 1900 BCE, in what is now Pakistan and north-western India, its people built planned cities of baked brick with streets laid out on grids, covered drains and public wells. Yet we know it only from archaeology, because its writing has never been deciphered.

## Rediscovery

The civilisation was forgotten for nearly four thousand years. In the 1920s, excavations at Harappa in Punjab and Mohenjo-daro in Sindh revealed cities of a culture no one had suspected, and it is often called the Harappan civilisation after the first site. Since then more than a thousand settlements have been found, over an area larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia combined, from the Makran coast of Balochistan to the edges of Delhi and from Afghanistan to Gujarat.

## Early Harappan roots

The Indus civilisation grew out of farming communities in Balochistan and the Indus plains. Mehrgarh, at the foot of the Bolan Pass, was a farming village from around 7000 BCE, one of the earliest in South Asia, where people grew barley and wheat and herded cattle, sheep and goats. Early Harappan towns, such as Kot Diji and Kalibangan, appeared in the fourth and early third millennia BCE.

## The great cities

The mature Harappan civilisation, from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, had several large cities: Mohenjo-daro and Harappa in Pakistan, Ganweriwala in Cholistan, and Rakhigarhi and Dholavira in India. Cities were often divided into a raised citadel and a lower town, laid out on a grid of streets. Houses were built of standard-sized baked bricks, in a ratio of 1:2:4, and many had wells, bathing areas and toilets connected to covered drains. Dholavira, in Gujarat, had large reservoirs to store water in a dry region and a large inscription of ten signs, perhaps a signboard. Lothal, in Gujarat, had a large basin that some interpret as a dockyard.

## Crafts and trade

Indus craftsmen made beads of carnelian, agate and other stones, drilled with remarkable precision, shell bangles, pottery, copper and bronze tools, and gold jewellery. Standardised cubical weights, based on a binary and decimal system, were used across the region. Indus goods reached Mesopotamia, where texts refer to Meluhha, probably the Indus region, as a source of carnelian, ivory and timber. Indus seals have been found in Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf. Some trade went through the Gulf island of Dilmun, modern Bahrain.

## Seals and script

Thousands of small stone seals, carved with animals, such as a one-horned bull often called a unicorn, elephants, tigers and rhinoceroses, and short inscriptions, have been found. One seal shows a seated figure surrounded by animals, sometimes called a "proto-Shiva", though this interpretation is controversial. The Indus script has about 400 signs, and inscriptions average only five signs, making it very hard to decipher. Its language is unknown; Dravidian and other languages have been proposed.

## Society and religion

The absence of obvious palaces, royal tombs or temples has led some scholars to suggest that the Indus civilisation was governed by elites, merchants or priests rather than kings, or by a decentralised system. Figurines of women, sometimes interpreted as mother goddesses, and the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro suggest religious practices involving purity and water. The famous bronze figurine of a "dancing girl" and the stone bust of the "priest-king" from Mohenjo-daro are among its best-known works of art.

## Decline and legacy

From about 1900 BCE, the large cities were gradually abandoned, and smaller settlements spread to the east and south. Shifts in the courses of rivers, including the drying up of the Ghaggar-Hakra, a weakening of the monsoon, declining trade and other factors have been suggested. Genetic studies suggest that people of the Indus civilisation contributed to the ancestry of most South Asians today. Some elements, such as certain crafts and possibly religious practices, may have survived in later South Asian culture.

## On our maps

Our maps show the civilisation on the 4000, 3000 and 2000 BCE maps, with an outline of about 770,000 square kilometres, around 60 percent of it in today's Pakistan and the rest in India. The 4000 BCE outline stands for the earlier farming cultures from which the cities grew. Lahore's and Karachi's sites lie inside it.
