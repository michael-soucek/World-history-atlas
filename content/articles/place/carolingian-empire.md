On Christmas Day 800, in St Peter's Basilica in Rome, Pope Leo III crowned the Frankish king Charles, known to history as Charlemagne, as emperor of the Romans. It was the first time in over three hundred years that a ruler in western Europe had held the imperial title. The empire his family ruled lasted less than a century, but it shaped the map of Europe.

## Charlemagne's empire

Charlemagne inherited the Frankish kingdom in 768 and spent most of his reign at war. He conquered the Lombard kingdom of Italy, fought the pagan Saxons for three decades and forced their conversion, destroyed the Avar state in the Danube basin and created a march south of the Pyrenees around Barcelona. He governed through counts, checked by royal envoys, and held court above all at Aachen. His court scholars, led by Alcuin of York, revived Latin learning and copied ancient texts in a clear new handwriting, Carolingian minuscule, the ancestor of modern lower-case letters.

## Governing a vast realm

Charlemagne's empire had no fixed capital for most of his reign; the court travelled between royal estates and palaces, though in later years he settled at Aachen, where the hot springs pleased him. The realm was divided into around 300 counties, each run by a count who led local troops, held courts and collected revenues. Along the frontiers, marches were placed under margraves with wider military powers. Royal envoys, the missi dominici, travelled in pairs, a churchman and a layman, to check on the counts and announce the king's laws.

## Capitularies and the Church

Charlemagne issued laws in documents called capitularies, covering everything from the management of royal estates to church discipline. The Admonitio Generalis of 789 set out a programme of religious reform, requiring schools at monasteries and cathedrals, correct copies of the Bible and liturgy, and better education for priests. The king saw himself as responsible for the salvation of his people, and worked closely with bishops and abbots, who were also major landholders and royal servants.

## The Carolingian Renaissance

Scholars from across Europe gathered at Charlemagne's court: Alcuin from York, Paul the Deacon from Lombard Italy, Theodulf from Visigothic Spain and the Frank Einhard, who later wrote a biography of Charlemagne. They corrected texts, wrote grammars and poetry, and promoted the study of Latin. Monasteries such as Tours, Fulda, Corbie and St Gall copied manuscripts in the new Carolingian minuscule; much of the surviving classical Latin literature, including works of Cicero, Caesar and Tacitus, survives because Carolingian scribes copied it.

## Economy and coinage

The empire's economy was mostly agricultural, organised on great estates worked by peasants and serfs, as described in the Capitulare de villis and in estate surveys such as the polyptych of Saint-Germain-des-Prés. Charlemagne reformed the coinage around a silver penny, the denier, with 12 pennies to a shilling and 20 shillings to a pound. Trade continued through North Sea ports such as Dorestad and with the Islamic world.

## Relations with other powers

Charlemagne exchanged embassies with the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid, who sent him an elephant named Abul-Abbas, and negotiated with Byzantium, which eventually recognised his imperial title in 812. He fought the Saxons for over thirty years, a war marked by forced conversions and the massacre at Verden, and destroyed the Avar khaganate, seizing its treasure.

## Louis the Pious

Charlemagne crowned his son Louis as co-emperor in 813, and Louis succeeded him in 814. Louis promoted church reform and tried to keep the empire united, but his attempts to provide for a son by his second marriage, Charles the Bald, led his older sons to rebel against him twice. After his death in 840, his sons Lothar, Louis the German and Charles fought a civil war, including the bloody Battle of Fontenoy in 841.

## The Treaty of Verdun and after

The Treaty of Verdun in 843 divided the empire: Charles took the west, Louis the east, and Lothar, with the imperial title, a middle kingdom from Frisia to Italy, whose northern part, Lotharingia, gave its name to Lorraine. Viking raids, Magyar invasions and Arab attacks weakened all the kingdoms. After the deposition of Charles the Fat in 887, the Carolingian dynasty lost the east in 911 and the west in 987.

## Legacy

West Francia grew into France, East Francia into Germany. The imperial title passed to the East Frankish kings when Otto I was crowned in 962, the usual starting date of the Holy Roman Empire.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Carolingian Empire on the 800 and 900 maps. On the first it covers about 1.2 million square kilometres: France makes up 44 percent, Germany 27 and Italy 13, with smaller shares in Austria, Switzerland and the Low Countries. Paris, Cologne, Milan, Venice, Florence and Barcelona are inside it on our 800 map. The 900 map keeps the name for the divided Carolingian kingdoms.
