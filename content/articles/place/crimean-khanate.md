The Crimean Khanate was a state of the Crimean Tatars that ruled the Crimean peninsula and the steppes north of the Black Sea from 1441 to 1783. One of the successor states of the Golden Horde, it became a close ally and vassal of the Ottoman Empire and, for over three centuries, a powerful force in Eastern Europe.

## Founding

Crimea had been part of the Golden Horde since the thirteenth century, with Genoese trading colonies, such as Kaffa, on its southern coast. As the Horde broke apart, Hacı I Giray, a descendant of Genghis Khan through Jochi, established an independent khanate in Crimea around 1441, with support from the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. His dynasty, the Girays, ruled until 1783.

## Ottoman alliance

In 1475 an Ottoman fleet captured Kaffa and the other Genoese colonies, and the khan Mengli I Giray, who had been held captive by the Genoese, became an Ottoman vassal. The relationship was unusual: the Ottoman sultans confirmed the khans, and sometimes deposed them, but the khans conducted their own foreign policy, minted coins and had the first claim to the Ottoman throne if the Ottoman dynasty died out. Crimean cavalry fought in Ottoman campaigns in Hungary, Persia and elsewhere. In 1502 Mengli Giray destroyed the remnant of the Golden Horde, the Great Horde.

## Bakhchysarai

The khans built their capital at Bakhchysarai, in a valley in the mountains of southern Crimea, in the early sixteenth century. The Khan's Palace, rebuilt after a fire in 1736, includes mosques, a harem, a cemetery and the Fountain of Tears, which inspired Alexander Pushkin's poem The Fountain of Bakhchisaray. Crimea had many mosques, madrasas and Sufi lodges, and Crimean Tatar became a literary language.

## Raids and the slave trade

The khanate's economy depended heavily on raids into Poland-Lithuania, Muscovy and the Cossack lands of Ukraine, which captured large numbers of people to be sold in the slave markets of Kaffa and other ports, mostly to the Ottoman Empire. Historians estimate that over a million or more people were taken over three centuries. In 1571, Khan Devlet I Giray burned Moscow. Moscow and Poland-Lithuania paid the khans regular tribute, called gifts, to limit raids, and built defensive lines, such as Muscovy's Great Abatis Line.

## Cossacks and shifting alliances

The Cossacks of Ukraine, originally frontier communities partly formed in response to Tatar raids, fought the Tatars but sometimes allied with them. In 1648 Bohdan Khmelnytsky allied with Khan Islam III Giray against Poland, and Tatar cavalry helped win major victories, though the alliance was unstable.

## Russian pressure

From the late seventeenth century, Russia grew stronger. Russian armies attacked Crimea in 1687 and 1689 without success. In the Russo-Turkish War of 1735–1739, Russian troops sacked Bakhchysarai. In the war of 1768–1774, Russia occupied Crimea, and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca of 1774 made the khanate formally independent of the Ottomans, effectively under Russian influence. Russia installed the khan Şahin Giray, whose attempts at Western-style reforms provoked revolts.

## Annexation

In 1783 Catherine the Great annexed Crimea. Many Crimean Tatars emigrated to the Ottoman Empire, especially after the Crimean War of 1853–1856. Russian, Ukrainian and other settlers arrived, and Sevastopol became a naval base. In 1944 Stalin accused the Crimean Tatars of collaboration with Germany and deported the entire population, about 190,000 people, to Central Asia, where many died. They were allowed to return only in the late 1980s. After Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014, the Crimean Tatar assembly, the Mejlis, was banned, and many Tatars faced repression.
