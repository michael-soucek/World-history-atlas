The Parthian Empire ruled Iran and Mesopotamia for almost five centuries, from the mid-third century BCE to 224 CE. Its kings, the Arsacids, were Rome's great rivals in the east, and their mounted archers inflicted some of the worst defeats Roman armies ever suffered.

## The Parni

The Parni were a nomadic people of the Dahae confederation, from the steppes east of the Caspian Sea. Under Arsaces I, they took the province of Parthia, around 247 BCE, from its Seleucid governor, and adopted the name Parthians. The Arsacid dynasty, named after Arsaces, ruled for nearly five centuries. Their early capital was at Nisa, near modern Ashgabat in Turkmenistan, where archaeologists found ivory drinking horns, rhyta, with Greek designs.

## Mithridates I and II

Mithridates I, who ruled from about 171 to 132 BCE, conquered Media, Persis and Babylonia, making Parthia an empire. Mithridates II, from about 124 to 88 BCE, defended the eastern frontier against nomads, extended the empire to the Euphrates, took the title "king of kings" and, around 115 BCE, received a Han Chinese embassy. In 96 BCE he made the first contact with Rome, through the general Sulla, on the Euphrates.

## Government

The Parthian Empire was decentralised. The king ruled with the support of great noble families, such as the Suren, Karen and Mihran, who held vast lands and private armies, and of vassal kings in regions such as Armenia, Adiabene, Characene, Elymais and Persis. Greek cities, such as Seleucia, kept their own institutions. The royal council of nobles and magi advised the king and could choose among Arsacid princes for the throne.

## The army

The Parthian army was built around two types of cavalry: light horse archers, who could shoot while retreating, the "Parthian shot", and heavily armoured cataphracts, where both rider and horse were covered in armour, charging with long lances. At Carrhae in 53 BCE, the general Surena used this combination, with a camel train supplying fresh arrows, to destroy Crassus's army. About 20,000 Romans were killed and 10,000 captured.

## Wars with Rome

Rome and Parthia fought over Armenia and Mesopotamia for nearly three centuries. In 40 BCE, Parthian forces invaded Syria and Judaea, before being driven back. Mark Antony's invasion in 36 BCE was a disaster. Augustus negotiated the return of Crassus's lost standards in 20 BCE. Trajan captured Ctesiphon and reached the Persian Gulf in 116 CE, but Hadrian gave up the conquests. Lucius Verus and Septimius Severus also sacked Ctesiphon in the second century CE.

## Culture and religion

Parthian culture combined Iranian, Greek and Mesopotamian elements. Early kings called themselves "philhellene" on their coins, which had Greek inscriptions. Later, Parthian, an Iranian language, and Aramaic became more important. The Parthians followed Zoroastrianism and tolerated other religions, including Judaism, Buddhism and early Christianity. Parthian art, with its frontal figures, influenced the art of the Roman East, as at Dura-Europos and Hatra. The Parthian epic tradition preserved Iranian legends later recorded in the Shahnameh.

## Trade

Parthia controlled the overland trade routes between Rome and China, and profited from the trade in silk. Parthians reportedly prevented Han envoys from contacting Rome directly.

## The end

Civil wars among Arsacid princes and Roman invasions weakened the empire. Ardashir, a local ruler of Persis, rebelled, and in 224 CE defeated and killed Artabanus IV at Hormozgan, founding the Sasanian Empire.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Parthian Empire on the 1 BCE, 100 and 200 CE maps. Its largest outline, about 1.9 million square kilometres on the 1 BCE map, is three-quarters in today's Iran and most of the rest in Iraq. Baghdad's site, Tehran and Isfahan lie inside it.
