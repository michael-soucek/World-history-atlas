Russia is the largest country in the world, stretching across eleven time zones from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific. Its history as a state runs from the medieval principalities of the Rus' through the Grand Duchy of Moscow, the Tsardom of Russia, the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union to the Russian Federation of today.

## Land and peoples

Russia covers over 17 million square kilometres, about an eighth of the world's land, from the Baltic to the Pacific. Most of its people live in the European west, while Siberia, with its forests, tundra and permafrost, is sparsely populated. Russia is home to over 190 ethnic groups, including Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Chuvash and many peoples of Siberia and the Caucasus, and has many republics with their own languages. Orthodox Christianity is the main religion, with large Muslim, Buddhist and Jewish communities.

## From Moscow to empire

The East Slavic state of Kievan Rus' broke apart in the twelfth century and was conquered by the Mongols in the 1230s and 1240s. Moscow, a small principality, rose under Mongol overlordship and, under Ivan III, threw off the Golden Horde's authority in 1480. Ivan IV, "the Terrible", was crowned the first tsar in 1547 and conquered Kazan and Astrakhan, and Russian expansion across Siberia reached the Pacific in the 1640s. Peter the Great founded Saint Petersburg and proclaimed the Russian Empire in 1721.

## Culture

Russian literature, from Pushkin, Gogol, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky and Chekhov in the nineteenth century to Pasternak, Akhmatova, Bulgakov and Solzhenitsyn in the twentieth, is among the world's greatest. Russian composers such as Tchaikovsky, Mussorgsky, Rimsky-Korsakov, Rachmaninov, Stravinsky, Prokofiev and Shostakovich, and the ballet of the Bolshoi and Mariinsky theatres, are world-famous. Russian scientists include Dmitri Mendeleev, who created the periodic table, and Ivan Pavlov.

## The Soviet experience

For most of the twentieth century, Russia was the core of the Soviet Union. Russia led the Soviet Union's industrialisation, its victory in the Second World War, and its space programme, which launched Sputnik in 1957 and Yuri Gagarin, the first human in space, in 1961. It also suffered the Gulag, the famine of 1932–1933, purges and repression.

## The 1990s

After the Soviet collapse in 1991, President Boris Yeltsin introduced rapid market reforms, called shock therapy. Prices soared, savings were wiped out, and state industries were privatised, often to well-connected insiders who became wealthy oligarchs. In 1993 Yeltsin clashed with parliament and used tanks to shell the parliament building, then introduced a new constitution with a strong presidency. Life expectancy fell, and Russia defaulted on its debts in 1998. Chechnya, which declared independence, was devastated by war from 1994 to 1996 and again from 1999.

## Putin's Russia

Vladimir Putin, a former KGB officer, became prime minister in 1999 and president in 2000. High oil and gas prices brought economic growth in the 2000s. Putin centralised power, brought television under state control and curbed opposition; journalists and critics, such as Anna Politkovskaya and Boris Nemtsov, were murdered, and the opposition leader Alexei Navalny died in prison in 2024. After serving as prime minister from 2008 to 2012, Putin returned as president, and a constitutional change in 2020 allowed him to stay in power potentially until 2036.

## Wars with neighbours

Russia fought a brief war with Georgia in 2008. In 2014, after Ukraine's pro-Western revolution, Russia annexed Crimea and supported separatists in eastern Ukraine. On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the largest war in Europe since 1945, which has killed and wounded hundreds of thousands and led to sweeping Western sanctions against Russia.
