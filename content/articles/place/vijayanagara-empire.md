The Vijayanagara Empire dominated southern India for over two centuries, from its founding in 1336 to its defeat in 1565. Its capital, Vijayanagara, "city of victory", on the Tungabhadra river in today's Karnataka, was one of the largest cities in the world in the early sixteenth century. Its ruins, at Hampi, are now a World Heritage Site.

## The four dynasties

Vijayanagara was ruled in turn by four dynasties: the Sangama, who founded it; the Saluva, who took power in 1485; the Tuluva, the dynasty of Krishnadevaraya, from 1505; and the Aravidu, from 1570. Founding legends say that the brothers Harihara and Bukka, who had served the Kakatiya kingdom or the Hoysalas, were captured by the Delhi Sultanate and sent back south, where, guided by the sage Vidyaranya, they founded a Hindu kingdom. Historians debate the details.

## Rivalry with the Bahmanis

From its beginning, Vijayanagara fought the Bahmani Sultanate, founded in the Deccan in 1347, over the fertile Raichur Doab between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers. After the Bahmani state broke into five sultanates, Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmadnagar, Bidar and Berar, Vijayanagara played them against one another. Under the Aravidu minister Rama Raya, Vijayanagara's interventions in their affairs united the sultanates against it, leading to Talikota in 1565.

## Hampi

The capital, Vijayanagara, at modern Hampi, stood among granite boulder hills on the Tungabhadra river. It had several rings of fortifications, a royal centre with palaces, the Mahanavami platform, used for royal festivals, stepped tanks, aqueducts, an elephant stable and the Lotus Mahal. Great temples, such as the Virupaksha temple and the Vitthala temple with its stone chariot and musical pillars, attracted pilgrims. Visitors such as the Persian ambassador Abdur Razzaq in the 1440s and the Portuguese Domingo Paes and Fernão Nunes in the 1520s and 1530s compared it to Rome and described its markets selling diamonds, pearls and horses.

## Government and economy

The empire was divided into provinces governed by princes or nobles, called nayakas, who held land in return for providing troops. The nayaka system later allowed governors to become independent. The economy was based on agriculture, supported by irrigation tanks and channels, and on trade. Vijayanagara imported thousands of war horses each year from Arabia and Persia through the west-coast ports, many of them controlled by the Portuguese after 1510. Temples were major landowners and economic centres.

## Krishnadevaraya

Krishnadevaraya, who ruled from 1509 to 1529, is remembered as the greatest Vijayanagara king. He defeated the Bijapur Sultanate at Raichur in 1520 and the Gajapatis of Odisha. A great patron of Telugu literature, he wrote the poem Amuktamalyada, and his court was home to eight poets, the Ashtadiggajas. He maintained good relations with the Portuguese at Goa.

## Talikota and after

On 23 January 1565, at Talikota, the combined armies of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda and Bidar defeated Vijayanagara. Rama Raya was captured and beheaded, and his army fled. The victors sacked the capital for months, and it was never reoccupied. The Aravidu kings ruled from Penukonda and Chandragiri. In 1639 the Aravidu ruler's local governor granted the English East India Company the land on which it built Fort St George, the beginning of Madras, now Chennai.
