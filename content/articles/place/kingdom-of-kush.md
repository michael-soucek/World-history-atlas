Kush was the great kingdom of Nubia, the Nile valley south of Egypt in what is now Sudan. Over more than two thousand years, Kushite states traded with Egypt, were conquered by it, and in the eighth century BCE conquered Egypt in turn. Sudan has more pyramids than Egypt, most of them built by the kings of Kush.

## Kerma

The kingdom of Kerma, centred on the town of Kerma, near the third cataract, was one of the earliest states in sub-Saharan Africa. Its rulers built huge mud-brick temples, known today as deffufas, and were buried in great tumuli, with hundreds of sacrificed people and animals, especially cattle. Kerma controlled trade in gold, ivory, ebony and incense with Egypt. During the Second Intermediate Period, Kerma allied with the Hyksos against Thebes and raided Egypt, carrying off Egyptian statues.

## Egyptian rule

The New Kingdom pharaohs, starting with Ahmose and Thutmose I, conquered Kerma around 1500 BCE and ruled Nubia for about five centuries through a viceroy, the "King's Son of Kush". They built temples, such as Abu Simbel and Soleb, and towns, and Nubian gold made Egypt rich. Many Nubians adopted Egyptian religion, especially the worship of Amun, whose temple at Jebel Barkal became a sacred site.

## Napata

After Egyptian control ended around 1070 BCE, a new Kushite state arose at Napata, near Jebel Barkal. Its kings, buried at el-Kurru, adopted Egyptian religious and royal traditions. In the eighth century BCE, Kashta and then Piye extended their rule into Egypt, founding the Twenty-fifth Dynasty, which ruled Egypt until the Assyrians drove them out. Their rule over Egypt is recorded in temples and in the Bible.

## Meroë

From around the sixth century BCE, after an Egyptian army sacked Napata in 593 BCE, the royal centre gradually moved south to Meroë, in the fertile region between the Nile and the Atbara river, known as the "Island of Meroë". Meroë had abundant iron ore and wood for smelting, and large slag heaps show an iron industry. The kings and queens were buried in steep pyramids, over 200 of which survive at Meroë. Meroitic religion combined Egyptian gods with local gods, such as the lion-headed Apedemak.

## The kandakes

Kush was notable for its powerful queens, called kandake, the origin of the name Candace. In the late first century BCE, after the Romans took Egypt, Kushite forces attacked Roman Aswan and carried off a bronze head of Augustus, buried under a temple step at Meroë, where it was found in 1910. The Romans responded by invading Kush, but a treaty in 21–20 BCE was favourable to Kush. A kandake is mentioned in the Acts of the Apostles, whose treasurer was baptised by Philip.

## Writing

The Meroitic script, developed by the second century BCE, had both a hieroglyphic and a cursive form, derived from Egyptian. Its 23 signs were deciphered phonetically by the British scholar Francis Llewellyn Griffith in the early twentieth century, but the Meroitic language, possibly related to Nubian languages, is still only partly understood.

## Decline

From the third century CE, Kush weakened, as trade routes shifted to the Red Sea, controlled by Aksum, and nomadic peoples, the Noba and Blemmyes, pressed on its borders. Around 350 CE, the Aksumite king Ezana campaigned on the Nile. Meroë was abandoned, and the kingdoms of Nobadia, Makuria and Alodia emerged in Nubia.

## On our maps

Our maps show Kush on every map from 1500 BCE to 300 CE. Its largest outline, about 600,000 square kilometres on the 1 BCE map, lies almost entirely in today's Sudan. Khartoum's site lies inside it from 323 BCE. Before it, the region is drawn as Kerma; after 300, as Nubian kingdoms.
