Carthage began as a colony of Tyre, a Phoenician city on the coast of today's Lebanon. Tradition dates its founding to 814 BCE and credits it to Queen Elissa, better known by her Roman name, Dido. Built on a peninsula near modern Tunis, it grew into the capital of a trading empire that controlled much of the western Mediterranean for centuries.

## Phoenician roots

Carthage's founders came from Tyre, the great Phoenician trading city on the Levantine coast. The city's name, Qart-hadasht in Phoenician, means "new city". For centuries Carthage kept links with Tyre, sending offerings to its temple of Melqart. As the Assyrians and Babylonians pressed on the Phoenician homeland, Carthage became the leader of the western Phoenician settlements, which Romans called Punic.

## The city

Carthage stood on a peninsula on the Gulf of Tunis, protected by massive walls. Its famous harbours included a rectangular commercial port and a circular military harbour with an island in the middle, where the admiral's headquarters stood, surrounded by shipsheds for over 200 warships. The Byrsa hill was the citadel. At its height the city may have had several hundred thousand inhabitants. Around it, the fertile lands of North Africa grew grain, olives and grapes; the Carthaginian writer Mago wrote a treatise on agriculture so admired that the Roman senate had it translated into Latin.

## A sea empire

Carthage's power rested on its fleet and on trade. It controlled ports along the North African coast, in southern Spain, in western Sicily, in Sardinia and on the Balearic islands, and its merchants traded tin, silver, purple dye and grain. It was governed by elected magistrates called suffetes and a council of wealthy families. Unusually for a major power, Carthage relied heavily on paid mercenaries and allied troops rather than its own citizens.

## Government

Carthage was an oligarchic republic. Two elected chief magistrates, the suffetes, led the state, with a council of elders and a court of 104 judges that supervised generals. A popular assembly had some powers. Aristotle praised the Carthaginian constitution as one of the best of his time. Wealthy merchant and landowning families, such as the Magonids and the Barcids, dominated politics.

## Religion

The chief gods of Carthage were Baal Hammon and the goddess Tanit. A sacred precinct, known as the tophet, contained thousands of urns holding the cremated bones of infants and young animals, with stone markers dedicated to the gods. Greek and Roman writers accused the Carthaginians of child sacrifice. Most archaeologists now accept that some children were sacrificed, though the scale and meaning are debated.

## War with the Greeks

For over two centuries Carthage fought the Greek cities of Sicily, especially Syracuse, for control of the island. A Carthaginian army was defeated at Himera in 480 BCE, traditionally on the same day as the Greek victory at Salamis. Later Carthage held western Sicily, and in 310 BCE the Syracusan ruler Agathocles even invaded Africa.

## Explorers

Carthaginian sailors explored the Atlantic. According to a Greek text, the Periplus of Hanno, a Carthaginian admiral named Hanno sailed down the west coast of Africa in the fifth century BCE, perhaps as far as Sierra Leone or Cameroon, and encountered hairy people he called gorillas. Another, Himilco, explored the Atlantic coast of Europe.

## The wars with Rome

Carthage's interests collided first with the Greek cities of Sicily, then with Rome. The First Punic War (264–241 BCE) cost it Sicily. In the second, Hannibal led an army with war elephants from Spain over the Alps into Italy in 218 BCE and destroyed Roman armies at Lake Trasimene and Cannae, but could not take Rome. A Roman counter-invasion of Africa forced his return, and he was defeated at Zama in 202 BCE. The peace terms stripped Carthage of its fleet and empire.

## After the wars

After the Second Punic War Carthage paid off its indemnity quickly and prospered. In 149 BCE Rome used a dispute with the Numidian king Masinissa as a pretext for war. After a three-year siege, Scipio Aemilianus took the city street by street in 146 BCE. Survivors were sold into slavery and the city was razed; the story that the Romans sowed the ground with salt is a later invention. A century later, Caesar and Augustus refounded Carthage as a Roman colony, which became the capital of the province of Africa and an important Christian centre, home to Tertullian and Cyprian.

## On our maps

Our maps draw Carthage from 500 to 200 BCE, largest on the 323 BCE map at about 690,000 square kilometres. Only a sixth of that is in Tunisia; the rest is spread along the coasts of Algeria, Libya and Morocco, with nearly a fifth in Spain. Palermo, Tunis, Algiers and Tripoli are Carthaginian on our maps through the period, and Seville, Granada and Cordoba in its later decades.
