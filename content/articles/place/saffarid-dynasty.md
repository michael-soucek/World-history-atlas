The Saffarids were a Persianate dynasty from Sistan, the borderland of today's south-western Afghanistan and eastern Iran, that ruled from 861 until 1002. Founded by Ya'qub ibn al-Layth, a coppersmith turned warlord, they built in less than two decades a realm stretching from Kabul to the edge of Iraq. They were among the first local Iranian dynasties to break free of direct Abbasid control, and their court helped launch poetry in New Persian.

## The coppersmith of Sistan

Ya'qub was born in 840 in Karnin, a small town between Zaranj and Bost. His byname comes from his trade, for saffar means coppersmith. With his brothers he joined the ayyars, armed bands of young men who fought Kharijite rebels and opposed Abbasid-appointed governors in Sistan. In 861 he overthrew the ayyar leader Dirham ibn Nasr and took the title of emir, making Zaranj his capital.

## Conquest east and west

Ya'qub first turned east. In 864 he marched on Bost and on the lands of the Zunbils, whose ruler he killed, and he went on to take Kabul from the Hindu Shahis. In 865 he crushed the Kharijites of northern Sistan, and further campaigns reached Bamiyan, Balkh and Ghor. The silver mines of the Panjshir valley let him strike his own coins. Herat fell in 870, and in 873 he drove the Tahirids from their capital Nishapur, taking Khorasan. He then pushed west into Kerman, Fars and Khuzestan.

In 876 he advanced into Iraq towards Baghdad itself. The caliph's brother al-Muwaffaq met him at Dayr al-Aqul, about fifty miles from the capital, on 8 April 876. After a hard day's fighting the Saffarid army broke; the Abbasids had flooded the ground behind it, and many men drowned in the retreat. Ya'qub withdrew, still holding most of his gains, and died of colic at Gundeshapur on 5 June 879.

## Amr ibn al-Layth

His brother Amr succeeded him and for a time reached an understanding with the Abbasid caliph al-Mu'tadid, who recognised Saffarid rule over Khorasan, eastern Persia and Fars. In 898 the caliph named Amr governor of Transoxiana, the land of the rival Samanids, and encouraged him to attack them. The gamble failed: the Samanid ruler Ismail defeated and captured Amr near Balkh in 900, and sent him to Baghdad, where he was put to death in 902.

## A shrinking state

Amr's grandson Tahir kept Fars and Kerman for some years, but civil war broke out in 908, and in 912 the Samanids drove the family out of Sistan. Saffarid rule there was restored under Ahmad ibn Muhammad, who reigned from 922 to 963, but the dynasty was now a minor local power, usually paying homage to stronger neighbours. In 1002 Mahmud of Ghazni invaded Sistan and deposed the last ruler, Khalaf ibn Ahmad.

## Religion and culture

The founders' beliefs are disputed, partly because most surviving accounts were written by their enemies. Later writers called Ya'qub a Kharijite, an Ismaili or worse, while the historian C. E. Bosworth saw no strong religious commitment. More recent work by D. G. Tor argues that he was a Sunni volunteer fighter for the faith, supported by leading hadith scholars. The dynasty's cultural legacy is clearer. According to the chronicles, when poets praised one of Ya'qub's victories in Arabic, a language he did not understand, his secretary Muhammad ibn Vasif composed verses in Persian instead. Under Saffarid patronage, poets such as Muhammad ibn Vasif and Abu Salik al-Jirjani gave fresh impetus to literature in New Persian.
