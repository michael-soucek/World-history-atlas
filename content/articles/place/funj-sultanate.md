From its capital at Sennar on the Blue Nile, the Funj Sultanate ruled much of what is now central Sudan for over three hundred years, from 1504 until the Turco-Egyptian conquest of 1821. Also called the Sultanate of Sennar or the Blue Sultanate, it took the place of the old Christian Nubian kingdoms and presided over the spread of Islam and the Arabic language across the middle Nile. Its founder, Amara Dunqas, led the Funj, a people whose origins remain uncertain.

## Origins

Christian Nubia had been in decline for centuries. [Makuria](/place/makuria) had largely collapsed by the late 14th century, and in the 15th century an Arab tribal federation led by Abdallah Jammah is said to have destroyed what remained of [Alodia](/place/alodia). In the early 16th century the Funj, cattle-herding newcomers from the south, overcame this federation. Scholars still debate who the Funj were: Nubians, Shilluk, a social class rather than an ethnic group, or migrants from further afield. Traditions disagree on how the state began in 1504, but the Abdallab chiefs ended up as subordinate rulers under the Funj, who quickly adopted Islam. The traveller David Reubeni, visiting in 1523, described Amara Dunqas as a Muslim king forever touring his lands with mounted captains.

## Ottomans, Ajib and Ethiopia

The Ottomans, expanding along the Red Sea and up the Nile, saw the sultanate as an easy target, but in 1585 the Funj crushed an Ottoman force at Hannik, south of the Third Cataract, which then became the frontier. Internally, the Abdallab sheikh Ajib I grew strong enough to dominate the sultans and, from about 1604, to invade the Funj heartland. Sultan Adlan I defeated and killed him in 1611 or 1612, but under the peace that followed the Abdallab kept the lands north of the Nile confluence as vassal kings. A border war with Ethiopia in 1618 and 1619 devastated the eastern provinces.

## Peak

The 17th century was the sultanate's high point. Sennar became a fixed capital under Badi II, whose reign produced the oldest known administrative document, dated 1654. The Funj subdued the Kingdom of Taqali, conquered the gold-rich Fazughli in 1665 and at their widest stretched from the Third Cataract to the Ethiopian borderlands, and from the Red Sea to Kordofan. By the turn of the 18th century, under Badi III, Sennar was described as one of Africa's great trading cities, its wealth resting on royal control of caravans and gold.

## Kingship and faith

The sultan was a sacred ruler in the African manner: secluded in his palace, seen in public only veiled, and liable to be judged and executed if found wanting. Rank among nobles depended on marriage to royal women, and royal descent was traced through the female line until 1719. The early Funj were Muslims largely in name, but they invited Sufi holy men to settle, which over time deepened Islam and Arab identity among the population. The army relied on armoured cavalry and on slave soldiers captured in raids on the Nuba Mountains.

## Decline and conquest

Coinage and private commerce in the late 17th century broke the sultans' hold on trade and strengthened a merchant class and Islamic scholars critical of royal custom. In 1744 Badi IV repelled an invasion by the Ethiopian emperor Iyasu II on the Dinder river, but his harsh rule drove nobles to Muhammad Abu Likaylik, a Hamaj commander who took Sennar on 27 March 1762. From then on the Funj sultans were puppets of Hamaj regents. Kordofan fell to the [Sultanate of Darfur](/place/sultanate-of-darfur) in 1785 or 1786, outlying provinces broke away, and by 1800 the country was torn by civil war. When the army sent by [Muhammad Ali of Egypt](/person/muhammad-ali-of-egypt) arrived in 1821, the last sultan, Badi VII, formally submitted on 14 June.
