The Hawaiian Kingdom was an independent island nation in the Pacific from 1795 to 1893. Founded by Kamehameha I, who united the islands by conquest, it was recognised by the United States, Britain, France and other nations, before American businessmen overthrew its last queen.

## Polynesian Hawaii

Polynesian voyagers settled the Hawaiian islands around 1000 CE or earlier, sailing double-hulled canoes from the Marquesas or Society Islands. Hawaiian society was divided into chiefs, ali'i, priests, kahuna, commoners and outcasts. The kapu system of sacred rules governed daily life, including separating men and women at meals. Each island, and sometimes parts of islands, was ruled by its own high chief, who often fought each other.

## Cook and Kamehameha

The British explorer James Cook reached the islands in 1778, naming them the Sandwich Islands. On his return in 1779, he was killed at Kealakekua Bay in a conflict over a stolen boat. Kamehameha, a chief from the island of Hawaii, used European weapons and advisers, such as John Young and Isaac Davis, to conquer the other islands. In 1795 he won the Battle of Nu'uanu on O'ahu, where many defenders were driven over the cliffs. In 1810 King Kaumuali'i of Kaua'i accepted his rule, uniting the islands. Kamehameha's law, the Law of the Splintered Paddle, protected the weak.

## Change

After Kamehameha's death in 1819, his widow Ka'ahumanu and his son Liholiho, Kamehameha II, broke the kapu by eating together in public, abolishing the old religion. In 1820 American Protestant missionaries arrived, creating a written form of Hawaiian and opening schools; by the 1850s, literacy among Hawaiians was very high. Whaling ships made Honolulu and Lahaina busy ports, and sandalwood was exported to China.

## A constitutional monarchy

Kamehameha III, who reigned from 1825 to 1854, issued a constitution in 1840, creating a legislature. In 1843 Britain and France recognised Hawaii's independence, after a British officer briefly seized the islands and Britain restored them; the date, 31 July, was celebrated as Restoration Day. In the Great Māhele of 1848, land was divided among the king, chiefs and the government, and later commoners and foreigners could own land. Within decades, most land had passed to foreigners, mainly Americans.

## Sugar and immigration

Sugar plantations, owned largely by American families, expanded, and a reciprocity treaty of 1875 allowed Hawaiian sugar into the United States duty-free, in return for American access to Pearl Harbor. Plantation owners brought workers from China, Japan, Portugal, the Philippines and elsewhere. Introduced diseases, such as smallpox and measles, caused the Native Hawaiian population to fall from perhaps 300,000 or more in 1778 to about 40,000 by 1890. Leprosy patients were exiled to Kalaupapa on Moloka'i, where Father Damien cared for them.

## Kalākaua and the Bayonet Constitution

King David Kalākaua, who reigned from 1874 to 1891, was the first monarch to travel around the world, revived hula and Hawaiian traditions and built 'Iolani Palace. In 1887 a group of mostly American businessmen, the Hawaiian League, with an armed militia, forced him to sign the Bayonet Constitution, which limited royal power and gave voting rights to wealthy foreigners while excluding most Asians and many Native Hawaiians.

## The overthrow

Kalākaua's sister, Queen Lili'uokalani, who succeeded him in 1891, tried to issue a new constitution restoring royal power. On 17 January 1893, the Committee of Safety, led by American businessmen, with the support of the US minister John Stevens and US Marines, overthrew her. She yielded to avoid bloodshed, hoping the United States would restore her. President Cleveland condemned the overthrow, but the provisional government refused to restore her and declared the Republic of Hawaii in 1894. After a failed royalist uprising in 1895, the queen was imprisoned in the palace. The United States annexed Hawaii in 1898. In 1993 Congress formally apologised. Lili'uokalani wrote the song "Aloha 'Oe".

## On our maps

Our maps show the Hawaiian Kingdom on the 1815, 1878, 1880 and 1900 maps, with an outline of about 14,000 square kilometres covering the islands. Honolulu lies inside it. On the 1900 map the basemap still uses the kingdom's name, although the islands had been annexed by then. By 1914 they are part of the United States.
