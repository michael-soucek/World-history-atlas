Between the Nile and the Red Sea stretches the Eastern Desert, and for thousands of years it has been the home of the Beja, a Cushitic-speaking people of herders whose lands today run from south-eastern Egypt through eastern Sudan into north-western Eritrea. Estimates of their number range from about 1.9 million to 3 million. Their ancestors have been linked to peoples recorded in the region since ancient Egyptian times, and their language is the only surviving member of the northern branch of Cushitic.

## Ancient roots

Ancient sources knew the desert peoples east of the Nile by several names: the Medjay in Egyptian texts, and later the Blemmyes in Greek and Roman ones. Archaeologists link the Medjay to the "pan-grave" culture, with sites at Khor Arba'at and Erkowit in the modern Beja heartland, and describe most of its people as desert nomads keeping donkeys, sheep and goats. Most scholars accept that the [Blemmyes](/place/blemmyes), who raided Roman Egypt and by the late fourth century held a kingdom in Lower Nubia, were ancestors of the Beja. Some linguists read names on third-century potsherds from the Eastern Desert as an early form of the Beja language.

## Aksum, Nubia and the medieval kingdoms

In the third century the [Kingdom of Aksum](/place/kingdom-of-aksum) conquered Beja territory and made it a vassal; Aksumite inscriptions in Ge'ez call the people Bəga. The ninth-century historian al-Ya'qubi listed several Beja kingdoms between Aswan and Massawa, while the tenth-century geographer al-Maqdisi described the Beja of his day as Christians, showing that the region's religious picture was mixed.

## Arabisation and Islam

Arab tribes migrating into the Eastern Desert gradually changed Beja society. Intermarriage brought Arabic speech, clothing and patterns of kinship to some groups, and by the fifteenth century the Beja had become Muslim. Yet the Arab newcomers did not settle the desert in large numbers, and much of Beja life kept its own character. By the eighteenth century the Hadendoa had become the dominant group across much of eastern Sudan.

## The Mahdist War and after

In the [Mahdist War](/event/mahdist-war) of the 1880s and 1890s Beja groups were divided: the Hadendoa backed the Mahdi, the Bisharin and Amarar supported the British, and some Beni Amer fought alongside the Ethiopian commander Ras Alula. Rudyard Kipling's "Fuzzy-Wuzzy" referred to the Hadendoa who fought the British. In [Sudan](/country/sudan) the Beja Congress, founded in 1952 to seek regional autonomy, joined the armed opposition in the 1990s, signed a peace agreement with the government in 2006 and withdrew from it in 2011, citing slow implementation and alleged election fraud.

## Clans and languages

The Beja are organised in clan groups, among them the Bishareen, Hadendoa, Amarar, Ababda, Beni Amer, Hallenga and Habab. Language varies between them. Most speak Beja, which its speakers call Bidhaawyeet, and Ethnologue counted about 2.76 million speakers across Sudan, Eritrea and Egypt in 2022–23. The Beni Amer largely speak Tigre, a Semitic language, while the Ababda and Hallenga mostly speak Arabic. Researchers note that Beja keeps high prestige: poetry and claims to land carry weight only when expressed in it. Most Beja live in Sudan's Red Sea, River Nile, Al Qadarif and Kassala states, around Port Sudan, with smaller communities in Eritrea's Gash-Barka, Anseba and Northern Red Sea regions and in south-eastern Egypt.
