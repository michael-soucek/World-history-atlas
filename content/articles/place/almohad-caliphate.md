The Almohads were a Berber religious movement that founded an empire in North Africa and Spain in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. At its height it united the Maghreb from Morocco to Tunisia with al-Andalus, the only time all of North Africa west of Egypt was ruled by a Berber dynasty.

## Ibn Tumart

The movement was founded by Muhammad ibn Tumart, a religious scholar of the Masmuda Berbers of the High Atlas, born around 1080. He studied in the east, possibly in Baghdad, and returned to Morocco around 1120, preaching a radical doctrine of the absolute unity of God, tawhid, and criticising the Almoravids for their legal literalism, which he said led them to imagine God in human form, and for the freedom of their women, who went unveiled while their men veiled their faces. His followers were called al-Muwahhidun, "those who affirm the unity of God", which the Spanish turned into Almohads. In 1121 he declared himself the Mahdi, the rightly guided one, and established a base at Tinmel in the mountains.

## Abd al-Mumin

After Ibn Tumart's death around 1130, his disciple Abd al-Mumin, a Berber of the Zanata, not the Masmuda, took the leadership and the title of caliph, a claim no North African ruler had made before. After years of war, he took Marrakesh from the Almoravids in 1147 and massacred many of its inhabitants. He conquered the central Maghreb and, in 1159–1160, Tunisia and Tripolitania, driving out the Normans of Sicily who held coastal towns. He also extended Almohad rule into al-Andalus and created a hereditary dynasty, the Mu'minids.

## Government and religion

The Almohads ruled through a hierarchy of Ibn Tumart's original companions, councils of tribal leaders and a class of trained officials, the talaba. Almohad doctrine was enforced strictly: the Maliki law books were burned under Abu Yusuf Yaqub, and Jews and Christians were ordered to convert or leave. Many fled; the family of the philosopher Maimonides left Córdoba around 1148 and eventually settled in Egypt. Some Jews converted outwardly.

## Philosophy and science

At the same time, the Almohad court supported philosophy. The caliph Abu Yaqub Yusuf invited the philosopher and physician Ibn Tufayl, author of the philosophical novel Hayy ibn Yaqzan, and through him Ibn Rushd, known in Europe as Averroes, who wrote commentaries on Aristotle that later shaped scholarship in Christian Europe. Ibn Rushd also served as judge in Seville and Córdoba and as court physician.

## Architecture

The Almohads built monumental mosques with austere, elegant decoration. The Kutubiyya mosque in Marrakesh, with its minaret about 77 metres high, the unfinished Hassan Tower in Rabat, and the Great Mosque of Seville, whose minaret survives as the Giralda bell tower of Seville Cathedral, are among their works. The three towers became models for later minarets in North Africa.

## Alarcos and Las Navas de Tolosa

The caliph Abu Yusuf Yaqub al-Mansur defeated Alfonso VIII of Castile at Alarcos in 1195. But in 1212, at Las Navas de Tolosa, a coalition of Castile, Aragon and Navarre, preached as a crusade, crushed the army of the caliph Muhammad al-Nasir. Over the following decades the Christian kingdoms took Córdoba in 1236, Valencia in 1238 and Seville in 1248.

## Collapse

In North Africa, governors and tribes broke away: the Hafsids in Tunisia declared independence in 1229, the Zayyanids took Tlemcen in 1236, and the Marinids, a Zanata Berber dynasty, expanded across Morocco. The caliph al-Mamun even renounced Ibn Tumart's doctrine in 1229. The Marinids took Marrakesh in 1269, ending the dynasty.
