The Mali Empire dominated West Africa for about two centuries, from the 1230s to the early 1400s, and at its height was one of the largest and richest states in the world. It grew from a small Mandinka kingdom on the upper Niger, and its language and institutions shaped the region long after its decline.

## Mansas and griots

Mali's rulers held the title mansa, "king" or "emperor". Their history is preserved partly in Arabic writings and partly in the oral traditions of the griots, the jeli, hereditary historians, musicians and advisers who served royal families. The Epic of Sundiata, still performed with the kora harp and the balafon, is the most famous example. Mali's heartland, the Manden, lay on the upper Niger near today's border of Mali and Guinea; its capital, often called Niani, has not been located with certainty.

## Sundiata

The empire's founder, Sundiata Keita, is the hero of an epic still recited by griots, the hereditary storytellers and historians of the Mande world. In the story, he overcomes a childhood disability, goes into exile and returns to defeat the sorcerer-king Sumanguru of the Sosso around 1235. Historians treat the details with caution, but Sundiata's victory and the founding of Mali under his rule are generally accepted.

## Government

Mali was a federation of provinces and tributary kingdoms. The mansa ruled directly over the core, through governors in other regions, and received tribute from vassal kings. According to tradition, Sundiata's assembly at Kurukan Fuga organised the clans and assigned roles to groups such as griots, blacksmiths and leatherworkers. Islam was the religion of the court and the merchants, while many ordinary people kept their traditional beliefs, and the mansas also took part in traditional rituals.

## Gold, salt and copper

Mali controlled the goldfields of Bambuk and Bure and later the gold of the Akan forest, and the trading cities of Timbuktu, Djenné and Gao. Caravans brought salt from Taghaza, copper and horses from the north. The fourteenth-century writer al-Umari, who gathered information in Cairo, described Mali as one of the four great kingdoms of the world.

## Mansa Musa

Mali's wealth came from gold mined in the south and from the trans-Saharan trade. Its most famous ruler, Mansa Musa, made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 with a vast caravan and gave away so much gold in Cairo that, according to Arab writers, its value there fell for years. He brought back scholars and an architect, and under his rule Timbuktu became a centre of Islamic learning; the Djinguereber mosque there dates from his reign. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta visited Mali in 1352–1353 and described its justice and safety, as well as customs that surprised him.

## Ibn Battuta's visit

The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta visited Mali in 1352–1353, during the reign of Mansa Sulayman. He praised the safety of travel, writing that travellers had no fear of robbers, the people's strict observance of prayer and their memorisation of the Quran, but criticised the gifts he received as too small and was shocked that women went unveiled and that subjects threw dust on their heads before the king. His account is one of the main eyewitness descriptions of the empire.

## Learning

Timbuktu and Djenné became centres of Islamic learning, with scholars, libraries and the manuscript trade. Timbuktu's Sankore mosque became a centre of study. Malian scholars travelled to Fez and Cairo. The Djenné mosque, rebuilt in its current mud-brick form in 1907, stands on the site of a mosque first built in the thirteenth century.

## Ships to the Atlantic

According to al-Umari, Mansa Musa told the governor of Cairo that his predecessor had sent two hundred ships to explore the Atlantic Ocean and then led a second expedition of two thousand ships himself, never to return. Some writers have speculated that Malians reached the Americas, but there is no archaeological evidence for this.

## Decline

After Musa and Sulayman, succession disputes weakened the empire. The Mossi kingdoms raided from the south, the Tuareg took Timbuktu in 1433, and the Songhai under Sonni Ali took over the Niger bend. The Portuguese, arriving on the coast in the fifteenth century, traded directly with Mali's western provinces. Mali shrank to its Mandinka heartland, and in 1599 a Malian attempt to retake Djenné failed.

## On our maps

Our maps draw Mali from 1279 to 1500. It is largest on the 1279 map, at about 1.75 million square kilometres, with 45 percent of that in today's Mali and the rest in Mauritania, Burkina Faso, Senegal, Niger and Guinea. Timbuktu is inside it on our maps until 1492, and the site of Dakar until 1530. Like other medieval Sahel states, its borders are approximate: Mali ruled through tributary chiefs as much as through direct control.
