The Toltecs were the dominant people of central Mexico between roughly 950 and 1150 CE, with their capital at Tula, in the modern state of Hidalgo, north of the Valley of Mexico. They are hard to separate from legend, because the Aztecs, who came later, saw the Toltecs as their cultural ancestors and the model of all civilisation, and told many stories about them.

## After Teotihuacan

After Teotihuacan declined in the sixth and seventh centuries, central Mexico broke into several regional centres, such as Xochicalco and Cholula, in a period archaeologists call the Epiclassic. Migrants from the north, Nahua-speaking peoples, moved into the region. According to later traditions, the Toltecs were led by a chief named Mixcoatl and settled at Tula, called Tollan, "place of reeds".

## Tula

Tula grew from about 900 CE into a city covering about 16 square kilometres, with perhaps 30,000 to 60,000 inhabitants. Its ceremonial centre, Tula Grande, had a large plaza with temple pyramids, ballcourts and colonnaded halls. On top of Pyramid B stand four colossal stone columns, about 4.6 metres tall, carved as warriors, the Atlantes, wearing butterfly-shaped breastplates and feathered headdresses, holding spear-throwers, atlatls. They once supported the roof of a temple. A wall, the coatepantli, "serpent wall", shows serpents devouring skeletons. Tula also had a tzompantli, a rack for displaying skulls, and chacmools, reclining stone figures holding a dish on their stomachs, probably for offerings.

## Warriors and trade

Toltec art is full of warriors, eagles and jaguars eating human hearts, and images of the feathered serpent, Quetzalcoatl. Tula controlled trade in obsidian from the Pachuca mines and traded with regions as far as Central America and the south-western United States, from which turquoise came.

## Quetzalcoatl

Aztec stories tell of a Toltec ruler named Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, a wise priest-king devoted to the feathered serpent god, who opposed human sacrifice. His rival, the god Tezcatlipoca, "smoking mirror", tricked him into getting drunk and breaking his vows, and he left Tula in shame, travelling east to the sea, where in some versions he burned himself and became the morning star, and in others sailed away on a raft of serpents, promising to return. Historians cannot easily separate history from myth in these accounts, which may reflect real political conflicts at Tula.

## Chichén Itzá

Striking similarities between Tula and the Maya city of Chichén Itzá in Yucatán, including warrior columns, feathered serpent columns, chacmools, skull racks and colonnaded halls, show strong contacts in the tenth and eleventh centuries. Maya traditions tell of a Kukulcán, "feathered serpent", who came from the west. Scholars debate whether Toltecs conquered Chichén Itzá, whether Chichén influenced Tula, or whether both shared a wider culture.

## Decline

Around 1150 Tula was sacked and burned, and its population declined. Possible causes include drought, internal conflict and invasions by other northern peoples. Many Toltecs may have moved to other parts of Mesoamerica.

## Legacy

For the Aztecs, the Toltecs represented civilisation, art and knowledge; the word toltecatl came to mean a skilled artist or craftsman. Aztec rulers claimed descent from Toltec royalty to legitimise their power, and the Aztecs removed Toltec sculptures from Tula to Tenochtitlan.
