The Frankish Kingdom was the most successful of the states that replaced Roman rule in western Europe. The Franks were a group of Germanic peoples living along the lower Rhine, some of whom served Rome as soldiers. In the late fifth century one of their kings, Clovis I, united them and took most of Roman Gaul. The country of France takes its name from them.

## The Franks before Clovis

The Franks first appear in Roman sources in the third century as a group of Germanic peoples raiding across the lower Rhine. Some settled within the empire as allies and soldiers, and Frankish officers rose to high rank in the Roman army. Clovis's father, Childeric I, ruled from Tournai, in today's Belgium; his grave, found in 1653, contained gold jewellery decorated with bees and a ring inscribed with his name.

## Clovis

Clovis became king around 481. He defeated Syagrius, the last Roman commander in northern Gaul, at Soissons in 486, the Alamanni around 496 and the Visigoths at Vouillé in 507, killing their king Alaric II. He then eliminated rival Frankish kings, many of them relatives. His conversion to Catholic Christianity, under the influence of his wife Clotilde and the bishop Remigius of Reims, won him the support of the Church and the Gallo-Roman population. He issued the Salic Law, a code of Frankish customary law, and made Paris his capital, where he died in 511.

## Divided kingdoms

Following Frankish custom, Clovis's kingdom was divided among his four sons, and divisions continued for generations, creating sub-kingdoms: Austrasia in the east, Neustria in the west, and Burgundy, conquered in the 530s. Rivalries between the Merovingian kings were often bloody, as in the feud between the queens Brunhilda and Fredegund in the late sixth century, described by the historian Gregory of Tours in his History of the Franks. Dagobert I, who ruled a united kingdom from 629 to 639, was the last strong Merovingian king.

## Society and religion

Frankish Gaul combined Roman and Germanic traditions. Gallo-Roman bishops, such as Gregory of Tours, were powerful local leaders. Monasteries spread, founded by Irish missionaries such as Columbanus, who established Luxeuil around 590. Latin evolved into early forms of French in the west, while Frankish dialects were spoken in the east. Merovingian kings wore their hair long as a sign of royalty.

## The mayors of the palace

The mayor of the palace began as the head of the royal household and became the real ruler. Pippin of Herstal, mayor of Austrasia, defeated Neustria in 687 and governed the whole kingdom. His son Charles Martel reunited the realm after a civil war, defeated an Umayyad force near Tours in 732 and extended Frankish power into Frisia, Bavaria and Provence. The Merovingian kings, called "do-nothing kings" by later writers, were left with only ceremonial duties.

## The new dynasty

In 751 Charles Martel's son Pippin the Short, with the approval of Pope Zacharias, deposed the last Merovingian, Childeric III, who was sent to a monastery, and was anointed king. In return, Pippin defended the pope against the Lombards in Italy and gave him the lands that became the Papal States. Pippin's son Charlemagne inherited the kingdom in 768.

## Legacy

Under Pippin's son Charlemagne the kingdom grew into an empire. When that empire was divided in 843, the western part, West Francia, became the kingdom of France, and the eastern part the core of the later German kingdom.
