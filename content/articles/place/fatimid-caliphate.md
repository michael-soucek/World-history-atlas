The Fatimid Caliphate was a Shia Muslim empire that ruled North Africa, Egypt and, for a time, Syria and the holy cities of Arabia, from 909 to 1171. Its rulers claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad's daughter Fatima, after whom the dynasty is named, and set themselves up as rival caliphs to the Sunni Abbasids of Baghdad. They founded Cairo.

## The Ismaili movement

The Ismailis were a branch of Shia Islam who believed that the line of imams continued through Ismail, son of the sixth imam, Jafar al-Sadiq. In the ninth century, Ismaili missionaries, dais, spread their teachings secretly across the Islamic world, from Yemen to North Africa and Iran. They predicted the coming of the Mahdi, a messianic leader. The dai Abu Abdallah al-Shii won the support of the Kutama Berbers in today's Algeria and defeated the Aghlabid rulers of Ifriqiya in 909.

## The Mahdi

Abdullah al-Mahdi, the Ismaili imam, came from Syria in disguise, was captured, rescued and proclaimed caliph in 909. He built a new capital, Mahdia, on the Tunisian coast. His successors faced revolts, including a massive Kharijite Berber uprising led by Abu Yazid in the 940s, which nearly destroyed the dynasty. The Fatimids also expanded into Sicily.

## The conquest of Egypt

Under the caliph al-Muizz, the general Jawhar al-Siqilli, a former slave of European origin, conquered Egypt in 969 with little resistance. He founded the new city of al-Qahira, Cairo, north of the old capital Fustat, with palaces for the caliph, and the mosque of al-Azhar, completed in 972. Al-Muizz moved to Cairo in 973.

## Government

The Fatimid caliph was also the Ismaili imam, considered infallible by his followers. The government was run by a vizier and a large bureaucracy, many of whose officials were Christians and Jews. The army included Berbers, Turks, Sudanese and Armenians. The majority of Egyptians remained Sunni Muslims or Coptic Christians, and the Fatimids generally did not force conversion. The Cairo Geniza, a store of documents from a synagogue in Fustat, reveals the lives of Jewish merchants trading across the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean in the Fatimid period.

## Prosperity and learning

Fatimid Egypt prospered from trade between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, through the Red Sea, and controlled Mecca and Medina. The Fatimids built mosques, such as al-Hakim and al-Aqmar, palaces and city gates, such as Bab al-Futuh and Bab Zuwayla. The caliph al-Hakim founded the Dar al-Ilm, "House of Knowledge", in 1005. The scientist Ibn al-Haytham worked in Cairo under al-Hakim. Fatimid crafts, rock crystal, lustreware and textiles, were famous.

## Al-Hakim

Al-Hakim, caliph from 996 to 1021, was unpredictable. He issued strange decrees, banning certain foods and the game of chess, ordering dogs to be killed, and restricting women's movements. He persecuted Christians and Jews, and in 1009 ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. He disappeared in 1021 during a night walk in the hills. A group of his followers who believed him divine founded the Druze faith.

## Decline

In the 1060s, famine and military conflict, the "great crisis", devastated Egypt. The Armenian general Badr al-Jamali restored order as vizier from 1074, and military viziers held real power afterwards. In 1094 a succession dispute split the Ismailis into Nizaris, who founded the Assassins in Iran and Syria, and Mustalis. The Crusaders took Jerusalem in 1099. Saladin ended the Fatimid caliphate in 1171.
