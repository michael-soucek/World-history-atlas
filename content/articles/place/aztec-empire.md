The Aztec Empire was an alliance of three city-states in the Valley of Mexico: Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan. Formed in 1428, it dominated central Mexico for nearly a century, until the Spanish and their indigenous allies destroyed it in 1521. The leading city, Tenochtitlan, was home to the Mexica people, whom we usually call the Aztecs.

## Tenochtitlan

According to their own history, the Mexica were wanderers from the north who settled on an island in Lake Texcoco around 1325, where their god had told them they would see an eagle perched on a cactus, an image now on the Mexican flag. They built Tenochtitlan into a city of perhaps 200,000 people, linked to the shore by causeways, with canals, floating garden plots called chinampas, markets and, at its centre, the twin temple of the Templo Mayor.

## The rise of the Mexica

The Mexica arrived in the Valley of Mexico in the thirteenth century as poor latecomers, serving as mercenaries for more powerful city-states such as Azcapotzalco. In 1428, under their ruler Itzcoatl, they allied with Texcoco and Tlacopan and defeated Azcapotzalco, forming the Triple Alliance. Itzcoatl ordered older historical books burned and a new history written that glorified the Mexica.

## Expansion

Moctezuma I (1440–1469) and later rulers, especially Ahuitzotl (1486–1502), expanded the empire through conquests across central and southern Mexico, reaching the Gulf Coast, the Pacific and as far as the borders of Guatemala. The empire included around 38 tributary provinces and millions of people speaking many languages. Some states, such as Tlaxcala, the Tarascan kingdom to the west and Metztitlan, resisted successfully. The Aztecs and Tlaxcala fought ritual "flower wars", partly to capture prisoners for sacrifice.

## Tenochtitlan's government

The ruler, the huey tlatoani, was chosen from the royal family by a council of nobles. He was assisted by the cihuacoatl, a powerful deputy. The Triple Alliance's three cities shared the tribute, but Tenochtitlan became dominant. Texcoco was famous for its poet-king Nezahualcoyotl, who built gardens, aqueducts and a dyke to protect Tenochtitlan from floods.

## Tribute

Conquered peoples paid tribute twice or four times a year. The Codex Mendoza, made for the Spanish after the conquest, lists the tribute of each province: cotton cloaks, warrior costumes, maize, beans, cacao, feathers, jade, gold, rubber and much more. Tenochtitlan received enormous quantities, which supported its nobles, warriors and temples. Cacao beans and cotton cloaks served as currency.

## The Templo Mayor

The Templo Mayor, the great temple at the centre of Tenochtitlan, had twin shrines to Huitzilopochtli, god of war and the sun, and Tlaloc, god of rain. It was rebuilt seven times, each larger than the last. In 1978 electricity workers found a huge stone disc of the moon goddess Coyolxauhqui, leading to the excavation of the temple in the centre of Mexico City. Excavations have found thousands of offerings and racks of skulls, the tzompantli.

## Sacrifice

Aztec religion held that the sun and the cosmos depended on offerings of blood. Captives, as well as slaves and others, were sacrificed at temples, usually by having their hearts removed. Spanish accounts give huge numbers, which are likely exaggerated, but archaeological finds confirm large-scale sacrifice. The rededication of the Templo Mayor in 1487 involved many sacrifices.

## The conquest

Hernán Cortés landed on the Gulf coast in 1519 with a few hundred Spaniards. He won allies among the Totonacs and Tlaxcalans and entered Tenochtitlan, where he took the ruler Moctezuma II hostage. Moctezuma died in 1520 during an uprising, and the Spanish were driven out. They returned with tens of thousands of indigenous allies after smallpox had killed many in the city, including Moctezuma's successor Cuitláhuac. After a siege of about three months, Tenochtitlan fell in August 1521. Mexico City was built on its ruins.

## On our maps

Our maps show the empire only on the 1500 map, at about 345,000 square kilometres, all within today's Mexico, with Mexico City built on Tenochtitlan's site. On the 1492 map the same region is shown as Nahua city-states; on the 1530 map it is part of New Spain. Because the empire ruled through tribute rather than fixed borders, any outline of it is an approximation.
