For most of its history Macedon was a modest kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world, ruled from Aigai and later Pella and looked down on by Athenians as half-barbarian. In a single generation, under Philip II and his son Alexander, it became the most powerful state on earth.

## The kingdom of Macedon

Macedon occupied the plains and mountains around the Haliacmon and Axios rivers, in what is now northern Greece. Its kings, of the Argead dynasty, claimed descent from Heracles and the city of Argos, and were admitted to the Olympic Games as Greeks, although many southern Greeks regarded Macedonians as outsiders. The kingdom was rich in timber, horses and, after Philip's conquests, the gold and silver mines of Mount Pangaion. Unlike the Greek city-states, Macedon was a monarchy with a powerful landed aristocracy, whose sons served as the king's companions.

## Philip's reforms

Philip II had spent part of his youth as a hostage in Thebes, then the leading military power in Greece, and learned from its generals. As king he created a professional army: infantry armed with the long sarissa and organised in a deep phalanx, supported by Companion cavalry, light troops and siege engineers. He paid his soldiers well with the gold of Pangaion and recruited from conquered peoples. He extended the kingdom east into Thrace, founding Philippi, and took control of Thessaly. He married several times for political alliances, including to Olympias of Epirus, Alexander's mother.

## The League of Corinth

After the victory at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, in which the young Alexander led the cavalry charge, Philip organised the Greek states, except Sparta, into the League of Corinth, with himself as its leader. The league declared war on Persia, to avenge the Persian invasions of Greece. An advance force crossed into Asia in 336 BCE, but that summer Philip was stabbed to death by one of his bodyguards at the wedding of his daughter in Aigai. His tomb is probably among the royal tombs excavated at Vergina in 1977.

## Alexander

Philip was murdered in 336 BCE, and his twenty-year-old son Alexander took the army east. In eleven years he defeated the Persian Empire at the Granicus, Issus and Gaugamela, took Egypt, where he founded Alexandria, and marched through Iran and Central Asia to the Indus valley before his exhausted troops refused to go further. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE, aged 32, without an adult heir.

## Alexander's army and methods

Alexander inherited his father's army and generals. He combined the phalanx, which held the enemy in front, with decisive cavalry charges, often led by himself, against the weakest point in the enemy line. He was also a master of siege warfare, taking Tyre in 332 BCE by building a causeway to the island city. He integrated Persian nobles and troops into his army and administration, adopted elements of Persian court dress and ceremony, and in 324 BCE held a mass wedding at Susa where he and his officers married Persian noblewomen. Many Macedonians resented this, and he faced plots and a mutiny.

## Cities and culture

Alexander and his successors founded many new cities, often settled by veterans, from Alexandria in Egypt to Alexandria Eschate, "the furthest", in today's Tajikistan. Greek became a common language of administration and trade across a vast area, and Greek art, theatre and philosophy spread to Egypt, Mesopotamia, Iran and the borders of India. In Gandhara, Greek styles later influenced Buddhist sculpture.

## The wars of the successors

On Alexander's death, his generals first ruled in the name of his half-brother Philip III and his posthumous son, Alexander IV, both of whom were eventually murdered. Antigonus the One-Eyed tried to reunite the empire but was killed at Ipsus in 301 BCE by a coalition of Seleucus, Lysimachus and Cassander. Ptolemy held Egypt, Seleucus most of Asia, and after decades of struggle, Antigonus's grandson Antigonus Gonatas secured Macedon itself in 277 BCE. Celtic invaders raided Macedon and Greece in 279 BCE.

## Macedon and Rome

The Antigonid kings dominated Greece for a century. Philip V allied with Hannibal against Rome and was defeated at Cynoscephalae in 197 BCE. His son Perseus lost the Third Macedonian War at Pydna in 168 BCE, and Rome divided Macedon into four republics, then made it a province in 146 BCE.

## Legacy

The idea of Alexander as a world conqueror inspired rulers from Julius Caesar to Napoleon. In Persian tradition he appears both as a destroyer, who burned Persepolis, and, in later literature, as a legendary hero, Iskandar.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Macedonian Empire on a single map, 323 BCE, the year Alexander died. At about 5 million square kilometres it covers almost exactly the old Persian Empire: around 30 percent of it lies in today's Iran, with large parts in Pakistan, Afghanistan, Egypt and Turkey, and only a small share in Greece itself. On the 400 BCE map the same lands are Persian; on the 300 BCE map they are already split, most of the east going to the Seleucid Empire.
