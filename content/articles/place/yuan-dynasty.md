The Yuan dynasty was the Mongol dynasty that ruled China from 1271 to 1368. It was founded by Kublai Khan, Genghis Khan's grandson, and it was the first time that all of China was ruled by a foreign people. Its capital, Dadu, called Khanbaliq, "city of the khan", by the Mongols, was built on the site of modern Beijing.

## Kublai Khan

Kublai, a son of Tolui and Sorghaghtani Beki, governed northern China for his brother, the Great Khan Möngke, from the 1250s, surrounding himself with Chinese advisers. After Möngke's death in 1259, Kublai fought his brother Ariq Böke for the throne and won in 1264. In 1271, on the advice of his Chinese adviser Liu Bingzhong, he took a Chinese dynastic name, Da Yuan, "great origin", from the Book of Changes. He built Dadu, laid out on a Chinese plan with walls, palaces and lakes, and kept the summer capital of Shangdu, the Xanadu of Coleridge's poem.

## Conquest of the Song

The Mongols had conquered northern China from the Jin by 1234, but the Southern Song resisted. The siege of the twin cities of Xiangyang and Fancheng, on the Han river, lasted from 1268 to 1273, ended with the help of Persian engineers who built counterweight trebuchets. The Mongol general Bayan took the Song capital, Hangzhou, in 1276. In 1279, at the sea battle of Yamen, the last Song loyalists were defeated, and China was united under the Yuan.

## Ruling China

The Yuan divided the population into four groups: Mongols; semu, "people of various kinds", Central Asians, Persians, Uyghurs and others; Han, northern Chinese, Khitans and Jurchens; and nanren, southern Chinese, the former subjects of the Song. Mongols and semu held the highest posts, and many Chinese scholars, unemployed after the civil-service examinations were suspended until 1315, turned to the arts. Kublai used foreign administrators, such as the Muslim finance minister Ahmad Fanakati, who was assassinated in 1282. Paper money, the chao, was widely used. The Grand Canal was extended to Dadu, and sea transport of grain was developed.

## Foreign wars

Kublai's expansion abroad mostly failed. Two invasions of Japan, in 1274 and 1281, were destroyed in part by storms. Expeditions against Vietnam, Champa, Burma and Java in the 1280s and 1290s brought heavy losses, though some states accepted tribute relations. Korea's Goryeo kingdom became a Yuan vassal.

## A cosmopolitan age

The Yuan court welcomed foreigners. The Venetian Marco Polo, by his own account, served Kublai for seventeen years. Muslims, Christians, Buddhists and Daoists lived in China, and the Franciscan John of Montecorvino became archbishop of Khanbaliq in 1307. Tibetan Buddhism was favoured, and the lama Phagpa created a script for Mongolian and Chinese. Chinese drama, zaju, flourished, with playwrights such as Guan Hanqing, and painters such as Zhao Mengfu and Huang Gongwang created influential works. Blue-and-white porcelain developed in Jingdezhen in this period.

## Decline and fall

After Kublai's death in 1294, the dynasty was weakened by short reigns and succession struggles; there were ten emperors in forty years. In the 1340s and 1350s, floods of the Yellow River, famine, inflation of paper money, epidemics and heavy labour demands led to revolts, especially the Red Turban rebellion, linked to the White Lotus Buddhist movement. One rebel leader, Zhu Yuanzhang, defeated his rivals and founded the Ming dynasty in 1368. Ming armies took Dadu, and the last Yuan emperor, Toghon Temür, fled to Mongolia, where his descendants continued as the Northern Yuan.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Yuan on the 1279 and 1300 maps. Its largest outline, about 10.5 million square kilometres on the 1279 map, includes Mongolia, parts of Siberia and Korea as well as China, which makes up 60 percent of the area. Beijing, Xi'an, Shanghai, Nanjing and Guangzhou lie inside it, along with Ulaanbaatar's site and, as Goryeo was then a Yuan vassal, Seoul and Pyongyang.
