Italy as a single state is younger than the United States. For centuries after the fall of Rome, the peninsula was divided among kingdoms, city-states, the Papal States and foreign powers. It was unified in the nineteenth century, in a movement called the Risorgimento, "resurgence". This page covers modern Italy; for ancient Rome and the medieval states, see pages such as the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire), the [Republic of Venice](/place/republic-of-venice) and the [Papal States](/place/papal-states).

## Unification

After the Congress of Vienna in 1815, Italy was again divided: the kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont in the north-west, Austrian Lombardy and Venetia, the duchies of Parma, Modena and Tuscany, the Papal States and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies. Giuseppe Mazzini's Young Italy called for a united republic, and revolutions in 1848 failed. Count Camillo Cavour, prime minister of Piedmont, allied with France, and in 1859 they defeated Austria at Magenta and Solferino, winning Lombardy. In 1860 Giuseppe Garibaldi and about a thousand volunteers, the Redshirts, conquered Sicily and Naples. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed on 17 March 1861 under Victor Emmanuel II. Venice was added in 1866 and Rome in 1870, which became the capital.

## Liberal Italy

The new state faced deep problems: a poor, rural south, where banditry was suppressed by the army; high illiteracy; and hostility from the papacy. Between 1880 and 1915, about 13 million Italians emigrated, especially to the United States, Argentina and Brazil. Industry developed in the north, around Milan, Turin, with Fiat founded in 1899, and Genoa. Italy sought colonies, taking Eritrea and Somalia, but was defeated by Ethiopia at Adwa in 1896. It took Libya and the Dodecanese islands from the Ottomans in 1911–1912. Giovanni Giolitti, prime minister several times, introduced near-universal male suffrage in 1912.

## The First World War

Italy, initially neutral, joined the Allies in 1915 after being promised territory. It fought Austria-Hungary in the Alps and along the Isonzo river, suffering a catastrophic defeat at Caporetto in 1917, before victory at Vittorio Veneto in 1918. About 650,000 soldiers died. Italy gained South Tyrol, Trentino, Trieste and Istria, but nationalists called it a "mutilated victory" because it did not get Dalmatia and Fiume.

## Fascism

In the economic crisis and social unrest after the war, Benito Mussolini founded the Fascist movement in 1919. After the March on Rome in October 1922, King Victor Emmanuel III appointed him prime minister. Mussolini built a dictatorship, banning opposition parties and using violence; the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti was murdered in 1924. The Lateran Treaty of 1929 created Vatican City. Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935, using poison gas, intervened in the Spanish Civil War, allied with Nazi Germany, introduced antisemitic racial laws in 1938, and annexed Albania in 1939.

## The Second World War

Italy entered the war in June 1940 and suffered defeats in Greece and North Africa. After the Allies landed in Sicily in July 1943, the Fascist Grand Council deposed Mussolini, and Italy surrendered in September. Germany occupied the north and set up Mussolini as head of the Italian Social Republic. Italian partisans fought the Germans and Fascists, and German forces committed massacres of civilians. Mussolini was captured and shot by partisans in April 1945.

## The Republic

In a referendum on 2 June 1946, Italians voted to abolish the monarchy. The constitution of 1948 created a parliamentary republic. Italy was a founding member of NATO and of the European communities, and experienced an "economic miracle" in the 1950s and 1960s. The 1970s saw political terrorism, the "years of lead"; the Red Brigades kidnapped and killed former prime minister Aldo Moro in 1978. In the 1990s, corruption scandals, Tangentopoli, brought down the old parties. Italy's art, cuisine, fashion and design are famous worldwide.
