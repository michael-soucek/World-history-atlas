Long before the pharaohs, people lived along the Nile. Prehistoric Egypt covers the long stretch from the first human presence in the region, hundreds of thousands of years ago, to the unification of the country under a single king around 3100 BCE. The last part of this period, from roughly 6000 BCE, is often called the Predynastic period.

## Stone Age Egypt

People have lived in the Nile valley and the surrounding deserts for hundreds of thousands of years, and stone tools from the Lower Palaeolithic have been found along the river and in the oases. During wetter periods, the Sahara was a savannah with lakes, home to hunters who left rock art of giraffes, cattle and swimming figures, such as at the "Cave of Swimmers" in the Gilf Kebir. As the climate dried, people were drawn closer to the Nile.

## Early farmers

The earliest known farming villages in Egypt appear in the sixth and fifth millennia BCE, at sites such as Merimde Beni Salama in the western delta and in the Faiyum depression, where people grew emmer wheat and barley and kept sheep, goats and cattle, crops and animals domesticated in western Asia. In Upper Egypt, the Badarian culture, from around 4400 BCE, made fine thin pottery and buried their dead with goods, a sign of beliefs in an afterlife.

## Naqada I and II

The Naqada culture, named after a site north of Luxor excavated by Flinders Petrie in the 1890s, developed in three phases. In Naqada I, from about 4000 BCE, communities made black-topped red pottery and slate palettes for grinding cosmetics. In Naqada II, from about 3500 BCE, they made decorated pottery with images of boats, people and animals, used copper, and traded with western Asia; Mesopotamian influences, such as cylinder seals and motifs, appear. Towns grew, and elite tombs became larger. The painted tomb 100 at Hierakonpolis shows boats, hunters and a figure smiting enemies.

## Lower Egypt

In the delta, the Buto-Maadi culture developed differently, with different pottery and houses, and trade with the Levant. During Naqada II and III, the Upper Egyptian culture spread north, replacing the delta's traditions, perhaps through expansion, trade or conquest.

## Kingship emerges

In the Naqada III period, from around 3200 BCE, rulers called by modern scholars Dynasty 0 controlled large territories. Their names, written inside a serekh, a symbol of a palace facade topped by the falcon god Horus, appear on pottery. Tomb U-j at Abydos, from around 3200 BCE, belonged to a ruler possibly named Scorpion, and contained labels with some of the earliest Egyptian writing. Ceremonial maceheads and palettes, such as the Scorpion Macehead and the Narmer Palette, show kings performing rituals and defeating enemies. Hierakonpolis, the city of Horus, was an important centre.

## Unification

Egyptian tradition held that a king called Menes united Upper and Lower Egypt and founded Memphis. Modern scholars usually see unification as a gradual process over several generations, completed around 3100 BCE, with Narmer as a key figure. The symbols of kingship, the white crown of Upper Egypt and the red crown of Lower Egypt, the falcon Horus, and the image of the king smiting his enemies, were already established. They would remain central to Egyptian culture for the next three thousand years.
