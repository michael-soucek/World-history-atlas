Afghanistan lies at a crossroads between Central Asia, Iran and the Indian subcontinent. Over thousands of years it was part of many empires, from the Persians and Alexander the Great to the Kushans, Ghaznavids, Timurids and Mughals. As a state of its own, it dates from the eighteenth century.

## Crossroads of empires

The region's valleys and passes, such as the Khyber, have carried armies, traders and ideas for millennia. Bactria, in the north, was a rich province of the Achaemenid Empire and later a Greek kingdom founded by Alexander's successors; the ruins of the Greek city of Ai-Khanoum lie on the Oxus. Under the Kushans, Buddhism flourished, and the colossal Buddhas carved into the cliffs at Bamiyan in the sixth century stood until the Taliban destroyed them in 2001. Islam arrived with the Arab conquests and spread over the following centuries. Ghazni was the capital of Mahmud's empire, and Herat, under the Timurids, one of the great cultural centres of the Islamic world.

## The Hotaks and the Durrani Empire

In 1709 Mirwais Hotak, a Pashtun leader in Kandahar, rebelled against the Safavids, and his son Mahmud conquered Isfahan in 1722. Nader Shah crushed the Hotaks, but after his assassination in 1747, an assembly of Pashtun chiefs, a loya jirga, chose Ahmad Shah Durrani, a commander of Nader's army, as king. Ahmad Shah built an empire from Khorasan to Kashmir and Punjab, defeated the Marathas at Panipat in 1761 and is remembered as "Father of the Nation". His successors lost much of the empire to the Sikhs and to internal rivalries.

## The Great Game

In the nineteenth century Afghanistan lay between the expanding British Empire in India and the Russian Empire in Central Asia, a rivalry known as the Great Game. Britain invaded in 1839 to install a friendly ruler. In January 1842 the British army retreated from Kabul through the snow-bound passes and was almost entirely destroyed. In the Second Anglo-Afghan War of 1878–1880, Britain took control of Afghanistan's foreign affairs. The Durand Line, agreed in 1893, set the border with British India and divided the Pashtun lands, a border Afghanistan has never formally accepted. The emir Abdur Rahman, "the Iron Emir", centralised power brutally in the 1880s and 1890s.

## Independence and the monarchy

After the brief Third Anglo-Afghan War of 1919, Afghanistan regained full control of its foreign policy. King Amanullah introduced a constitution, girls' schools and Western dress, but conservative revolts forced him out in 1929. Under King Zahir Shah, from 1933 to 1973, Afghanistan stayed neutral in the Second World War and received aid from both the United States and the Soviet Union. A new constitution in 1964 introduced elections. In 1973 his cousin Mohammad Daoud Khan overthrew him and declared a republic.

## Communist rule and the Soviet war

In April 1978 the communist People's Democratic Party seized power in the Saur Revolution and launched radical reforms enforced with mass arrests and executions. Rebellions spread, and in December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded. For ten years, Soviet forces fought mujahideen, Islamist resistance groups armed by the United States, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and others. Perhaps a million Afghans were killed, and over five million fled, mostly to Pakistan and Iran. The Soviets withdrew in 1989.

## Civil war, the Taliban and the US war

After the communist government fell in 1992, mujahideen factions fought over Kabul. The Taliban, a movement of religious students supported by Pakistan, took Kandahar in 1994 and Kabul in 1996, imposing a harsh interpretation of Islamic law, banning girls' education and sheltering Osama bin Laden's al-Qaeda. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, a US-led coalition and the Northern Alliance overthrew the Taliban. A new republic was founded, with elections and a constitution in 2004, but the Taliban insurgency grew. The United States withdrew in August 2021, and the Taliban returned to power, again restricting women's education and work.
