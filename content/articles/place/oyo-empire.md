The Oyo Empire was the largest and most powerful of the Yoruba states, in what is now western Nigeria and Benin. Founded around the fourteenth century in the savanna north of the forest, it rose to dominate the region in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, thanks above all to its cavalry, before collapsing in the early nineteenth century.

## The Yoruba and Ife

The Yoruba people lived in many city-states, each ruled by a king, an oba. They considered the city of Ile-Ife the place where the world was created and the origin of their kings. Ife was famous for its naturalistic bronze and terracotta heads, made from the twelfth to the fifteenth centuries. According to tradition, Oranmiyan, a prince of Ife and grandson of Oduduwa, the founder of the Yoruba, founded Oyo and became its first king, the Alaafin, "owner of the palace".

## Exile and return

In the early sixteenth century, the Nupe, a neighbouring people to the north, sacked Oyo-Ile, the capital, and the court went into exile for decades, at Igboho. Around 1610 the Alaafin Abipa returned to Oyo-Ile. The experience led Oyo to build a strong army, especially cavalry.

## Cavalry and empire

Oyo lay in open savanna, where horses could survive, as tsetse flies, which kill horses, were fewer than in the forest. Oyo imported horses from the north and built a powerful cavalry, which, with archers and infantry, made it the dominant military power of the region. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Oyo conquered or brought into tribute many neighbouring peoples and states. From 1730, after repeated invasions, the powerful kingdom of Dahomey paid annual tribute to Oyo. Oyo's influence reached the coast at Porto-Novo and Badagry.

## Government

Oyo's government balanced power among several institutions. The Alaafin was considered sacred, almost divine, and lived secluded in his palace, with a large staff of officials and messengers, the ilari. A council of seven nobles, the Oyo Mesi, led by the Bashorun, represented the city's lineages and could, if they judged the king unfit, present him with an empty calabash or parrots' eggs, a sign that he must take his own life. The Ogboni, a secret society of elders and religious leaders, balanced the Oyo Mesi. The army was commanded by the Are Ona Kakanfo, who had to live outside the capital. Provinces were governed through local rulers and royal representatives.

## Trade

Oyo's wealth came from tribute, trade in cloth, kola nuts and other goods, and from the Atlantic slave trade. Captives taken in wars were sold to European traders through coastal ports, and Oyo became one of the major suppliers of enslaved people in the eighteenth century; many Yoruba people were taken to Brazil and Cuba.

## Collapse

In the late eighteenth century, power struggles weakened Oyo. The Bashorun Gaha dominated the state from 1754 to 1774, forcing several Alaafins to commit suicide, until the Alaafin Abiodun had him killed. Around 1817 Afonja, the Are Ona Kakanfo, based at Ilorin, rebelled, with help from Fulani Muslims and Hausa slaves inspired by the Fulani jihad. The Fulani then took Ilorin, which became part of the Sokoto Caliphate. Ilorin's forces attacked Oyo, and around 1835 Oyo-Ile was abandoned; the Alaafin moved south and founded a new Oyo.

## Aftermath

The collapse of Oyo led to decades of wars among Yoruba states, such as Ibadan, Ijebu and Egba, and refugees founded new cities, such as Abeokuta and Ibadan. Many captives from these wars were sold into slavery, and Yoruba religion and culture spread to Brazil, Cuba and Haiti, influencing Candomblé and Santería. In the 1890s Britain imposed rule over Yorubaland, which became part of Nigeria.
