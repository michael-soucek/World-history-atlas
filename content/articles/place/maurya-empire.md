The Maurya Empire was the first state to rule most of the Indian subcontinent. Its founder, Chandragupta Maurya, overthrew the Nanda kings of Magadha around 320 BCE, shortly after Alexander the Great's army had turned back from the Indus. Within a generation the Mauryas governed from Afghanistan to Bengal, from their capital at Pataliputra, near modern Patna.

## Chandragupta and the Greeks

Chandragupta fought Seleucus I, who inherited Alexander's eastern provinces, and the two made peace around 303 BCE: the Mauryas took the lands up to the Hindu Kush, and Seleucus received 500 war elephants. A Greek ambassador, Megasthenes, lived at the Maurya court and wrote an account of India that survives only in fragments quoted by later authors.

## Bindusara

Between Chandragupta and Ashoka came Chandragupta's son Bindusara, who ruled from about 297 to 273 BCE. Greek writers called him Amitrochates, "slayer of enemies". He probably extended the empire into the Deccan, and kept up diplomatic ties with the Seleucids and Ptolemies; one story says he asked the Seleucid king to send him figs, wine and a philosopher. Little else is known of his reign.

## Government

The Mauryas ran one of the largest administrations of the ancient world. The empire was divided into provinces governed by princes or senior officials, from capitals such as Taxila, Ujjain and Tosali. Officials supervised agriculture, irrigation, mines, forests, trade and markets. The Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft traditionally attributed to Chandragupta's adviser Kautilya, describes a state that controlled the economy closely and used spies extensively; how far it describes the actual Mauryan state is debated, since the text was revised over later centuries. Megasthenes described officials who measured land and supervised the city of Pataliputra in committees.

## Pataliputra

Megasthenes described the Maurya capital as a vast city along the Ganges, about fourteen kilometres long, surrounded by a timber palisade with 570 towers and 64 gates. Excavations near Patna have found remains of wooden walls and a great pillared hall. The royal palace impressed Greek visitors as rivalling those of Persia.

## Economy and society

The empire's economy was based on farming in the fertile Ganges plain, with rice as a main crop, and on trade along great roads such as the Uttarapatha, linking Bengal with the north-west. Punch-marked silver coins were in use. Society was structured by the varna system, though Megasthenes, misunderstanding it, described seven classes. Buddhism, Jainism and Brahmanical religion all flourished, along with smaller sects such as the Ajivikas, for whom Ashoka cut cave dwellings in the Barabar hills.

## Ashoka

Chandragupta's grandson Ashoka extended the empire south and east. His conquest of Kalinga, on the east coast, around 261 BCE was so bloody that, by his own account, it filled him with remorse. He turned to Buddhism and to a policy he called dhamma: non-violence, tolerance between religions, care for people and animals. He had his principles carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across his empire, in several languages and scripts. These edicts are among the oldest deciphered writing in India, and the lion capital from one of his pillars at Sarnath is now India's national emblem.

## Ashoka's dhamma in practice

Ashoka's edicts describe concrete measures: wells and rest houses along roads, shade trees, medical treatment for people and animals, officials called dhamma-mahamattas who toured the empire to promote moral behaviour and settle disputes, and restrictions on the killing of animals, including at the royal kitchens. He also warned forest tribes that his remorse did not mean weakness. His rock edicts in the north-west, written in Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar, show the empire's reach into Afghanistan.

## Art

Mauryan art is best known for its polished sandstone pillars, crowned with animal capitals: lions at Sarnath, a bull at Rampurva. The Didarganj Yakshi, a polished statue of a woman holding a fly-whisk, is often dated to this period. Ashoka is credited with building or enlarging many stupas, including the first stupa at Sanchi.

## The end

The empire weakened after Ashoka's death around 232 BCE. In 185 BCE the last Maurya king was killed by his own commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the Shunga dynasty.

## Legacy

The Maurya Empire showed that most of the subcontinent could be ruled as one state, an achievement not repeated until the Mughals. The modern Indian republic took Ashoka's lion capital as its emblem and his dhamma wheel for its flag.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on the 300 and 200 BCE maps, largest on the second at about 2.8 million square kilometres. Over 80 percent of that is in today's India, with the rest in Pakistan and Bangladesh. The cities on its ground on our maps include Delhi, Kolkata, Lahore and Karachi. The basemap's version is smaller than the empire at Ashoka's height, which also took in much of Afghanistan. On the 323 BCE map the region belongs to Magadha; by 100 BCE it is the Shunga Empire's.
