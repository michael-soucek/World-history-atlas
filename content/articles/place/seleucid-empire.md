The Seleucid Empire was the largest of the kingdoms carved out of Alexander the Great's conquests. Its founder, Seleucus I Nicator, was one of Alexander's officers who took control of Babylon in 312 BCE, the year from which the Seleucid era was counted. By the time he died in 281 BCE he ruled from the Aegean coast of Anatolia to the borders of India.

## Seleucus Nicator

Seleucus was a Macedonian officer who commanded the elite infantry guard under Alexander and married a Bactrian noblewoman, Apama, at the Susa weddings; unlike most of the other officers, he kept his Persian wife, and their son Antiochus succeeded him. After Alexander's death he became satrap of Babylon, was driven out by Antigonus, and returned in 312 BCE with a small force, which the dynasty treated as its founding moment. He fought his way east to Bactria and India, and his victory at Ipsus in 301 BCE gave him Syria. In 281 BCE he defeated Lysimachus and took Anatolia, but was murdered as he crossed into Europe.

## Antioch and the cities

The Seleucids were great founders of cities. Seleucus built Seleucia on the Tigris as an eastern capital, and in northern Syria a group of four cities, the Syrian Tetrapolis: Antioch, Seleucia Pieria, Apamea and Laodicea. Antioch grew into one of the largest cities of the ancient world. Settlers came from Greece and Macedon, and the cities had Greek institutions such as councils, gymnasiums and theatres, while local peoples lived in and around them.

## Ruling a diverse empire

The empire stretched across many cultures with very different traditions. In Babylon, the Seleucid kings performed ancient rituals in the temple of Marduk, and Babylonian priests continued to write cuneiform astronomical records under their rule. The Babylonian priest Berossus wrote a history of Babylon in Greek for Antiochus I. In Iran, local dynasts in Persis issued their own coins. The kings depended heavily on the army, built around a Macedonian-style phalanx and war elephants, and on settlers who held land in return for military service.

## Wars with the Ptolemies

The Seleucids and the Ptolemies of Egypt fought six Syrian Wars over the region of Coele-Syria, the southern Levant, between 274 and 168 BCE. Antiochus III finally won it at the Battle of Panium in 200 BCE, taking Judaea and Phoenicia. In the last of these wars, Antiochus IV invaded Egypt and was about to take Alexandria in 168 BCE when a Roman envoy, Popillius Laenas, drew a circle in the sand around him and demanded an answer before he stepped out of it. Antiochus withdrew.

## Antiochus the Great

Antiochus III, king from 223 to 187 BCE, led an expedition east from 212 to 205 BCE, imitating Alexander, which brought the Parthians and Bactrians back into vassalage and reached India. He then expanded into Anatolia and Europe, sheltering the exiled Hannibal. Rome defeated him at Thermopylae in 191 BCE and Magnesia in 190 BCE. The Treaty of Apamea in 188 BCE took away most of Anatolia, his war elephants and fleet, and imposed a huge indemnity. Antiochus was killed in 187 BCE while plundering a temple to raise money.

## The Maccabees

Antiochus IV, around 167 BCE, banned Jewish religious practices in Judaea and placed an altar to Zeus in the Temple in Jerusalem, perhaps in response to a civil conflict among Jews. A revolt led by the priest Mattathias and his son Judas Maccabeus retook Jerusalem and rededicated the Temple in 164 BCE, the origin of the festival of Hanukkah. Judaea later became independent under the Hasmonean dynasty.

## Decline

After 160 BCE, the dynasty was torn by rival claimants, often supported by Rome, Egypt or Parthia. The Parthian king Mithridates I took Media and Babylonia in the 140s BCE, and the Seleucids never recovered the east. Antiochus VII's attempt to win it back ended with his death in battle against the Parthians in 129 BCE. In 83 BCE Tigranes of Armenia took Syria. The Roman general Lucullus restored a Seleucid prince, but Pompey ended the kingdom in 63 BCE.

## The end

By the first century BCE the kingdom had shrunk to a strip of Syria fought over by rival claimants. In 64–63 BCE the Roman general Pompey deposed the last Seleucid king and made Syria a Roman province.

## Legacy

The Seleucid era, counted from 312 BCE, continued to be used for dating in the Middle East for over a thousand years, by Jews, Syrian Christians and others. Greek culture in the east, supported by the Seleucids, influenced later states, including the Parthians, who used Greek on their coins.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Seleucid Empire on the 300, 200 and 100 BCE maps. It is largest on the first of them, at about 3.5 million square kilometres, with more than 40 percent of that in today's Iran and most of the rest in Pakistan, Afghanistan and Iraq. Tehran's and Isfahan's sites stay Seleucid through the period on our maps, while Kabul and Samarkand are gone by 100 BCE. On the 323 BCE map the land belongs to Alexander; by 1 BCE it is Parthian.
