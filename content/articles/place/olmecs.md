The Olmecs created the first major civilisation of Mesoamerica, on the hot, wet Gulf Coast lowlands of today's Mexican states of Veracruz and Tabasco, from about 1200 to 400 BCE. Many features of later Mesoamerican cultures, from the ballgame to monumental art and perhaps the calendar, appear first or early among them. The name Olmec, "rubber people" in the Aztec language Nahuatl, was given to them by modern scholars; we do not know what they called themselves.

## The Olmec heartland

The Olmec heartland lay in the lowlands of southern Veracruz and western Tabasco, along the Gulf of Mexico, a region of rivers, swamps, rainforest and fertile floodplains. Farmers grew maize, beans and squash on the river levees, enriched by annual floods, and fished and hunted. The region lacked good stone, so the Olmecs brought basalt from the Tuxtla mountains, many kilometres away, probably by river rafts and by dragging, and imported jade and serpentine from Guatemala and elsewhere.

## San Lorenzo

San Lorenzo, the first major Olmec centre, flourished from around 1400 to 1000 BCE. Built on a plateau that its people partly reshaped with huge amounts of earth, it had elite residences, workshops and an elaborate system of stone drains. Ten colossal heads have been found there, as well as large stone thrones, later re-carved into heads. Around 1000 BCE, San Lorenzo declined, and many monuments were deliberately damaged and buried.

## La Venta

La Venta, from about 900 to 400 BCE, was built on an island in a swamp near the Tonalá river. Its central area included a large earthen pyramid, plazas and platforms aligned slightly west of north. Underground, the Olmecs buried offerings of jade celts and figurines, and massive pavements of serpentine blocks arranged in mosaic patterns, sometimes in the shape of a jaguar mask. Offering 4, a group of sixteen jade and serpentine figurines arranged in a scene, is one of the most famous finds.

## Art

The colossal heads, up to about three metres tall, depict men with full faces and headgear, each different, probably rulers. Olmec artists also carved thrones, called altars, showing figures emerging from caves, which may represent rulers' connections with the underworld or ancestors. Small jade figurines, masks and celts often show beings with features of jaguars and humans, called "were-jaguars", with snarling mouths and cleft heads. Olmec ceramic figures of babies are also distinctive.

## Religion and ideas

Olmec religion seems to have involved deities associated with maize, rain, the earth and the jaguar, and rulers who acted as intermediaries with the supernatural. The Mesoamerican ballgame, played with rubber balls, was already known; rubber balls dated to around 1600 BCE were found at El Manatí, a sacred spring. Some scholars believe that the Olmecs developed or used early forms of the calendar and writing later found across Mesoamerica.

## Olmec influence

Olmec-style objects and motifs appear across Mesoamerica, at sites such as Chalcatzingo in Morelos, with its carved reliefs, Teopantecuanitlan in Guerrero and sites in Oaxaca and Chiapas. Scholars debate whether this reflects Olmec political control, trade, migration or the shared ideas of elites across the region. The "mother culture" view, that the Olmecs were the source of later Mesoamerican civilisations, has been challenged by the "sister cultures" view, which stresses mutual influence among several regions.

## Rediscovery

The first colossal head was found at Tres Zapotes in 1862. The archaeologist Matthew Stirling excavated Olmec sites in the 1930s and 1940s. Many monuments from La Venta were moved to an open-air park in Villahermosa in the 1950s, when oil drilling threatened the site.
