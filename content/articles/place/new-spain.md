New Spain was the largest of Spain's American viceroyalties, governed from Mexico City from 1535 until the Mexican War of Independence ended in 1821. At its height it included Mexico, Central America, much of the western and south-western United States, the Caribbean islands and, across the Pacific, the Philippines.

## Conquest

After Hernán Cortés and his Indigenous allies took Tenochtitlan in 1521, Spain built Mexico City on its ruins, using the stones of the Aztec temples. Conquistadors spread out, conquering the Maya of Yucatán over decades and the peoples of western Mexico, where Nuño de Guzmán's campaigns were notoriously brutal. In 1535 Antonio de Mendoza arrived as the first viceroy. The Audiencia of Mexico, a high court, handled justice and administration.

## Disease and labour

The Indigenous population of central Mexico collapsed in the sixteenth century, from perhaps 15 to 25 million or more to around one to two million by 1600, mainly from epidemics of smallpox, measles and especially cocoliztli, possibly a form of salmonella, in 1545 and 1576, along with war, forced labour and disruption. Spanish settlers received grants of Indigenous labour, encomiendas, later replaced by the repartimiento, a labour draft, and wage labour. Enslaved Africans were brought to work in mines, plantations and cities.

## Missions and the Virgin of Guadalupe

Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and later Jesuits converted the Indigenous population, building churches and monasteries, and learning Indigenous languages; the friar Bernardino de Sahagún, with Nahua scholars, compiled the Florentine Codex, a vast record of Aztec culture. According to tradition, in 1531 the Virgin Mary appeared to an Indigenous man, Juan Diego, at Tepeyac; the Virgin of Guadalupe became the most important symbol of Mexican Catholicism and identity.

## Silver and the Manila galleons

Silver was found at Zacatecas in 1546 and Guanajuato in the 1550s, and New Spain became one of the world's largest silver producers. Mexican silver pesos became an international currency. From 1565, the Manila galleons sailed each year between Acapulco and Manila, carrying silver to Asia and bringing back Chinese silk and porcelain, linking Asia, America and Europe.

## A layered society

Colonial society was divided by origin and ancestry, in a system later called the sistema de castas. Spanish-born peninsulares held the highest offices, while American-born Spaniards, criollos, resented their exclusion. Mestizos, of mixed Spanish and Indigenous ancestry, mulattos, Indigenous people and Africans made up the majority. Paintings, casta paintings, depicted mixed families. The nun Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, in the seventeenth century, wrote poetry and defended women's right to learning.

## The northern frontier

New Spain expanded north, founding Santa Fe, in New Mexico, in 1610; missions and presidios, forts, in Texas, such as San Antonio, in 1718; and in California, where the Franciscan Junípero Serra founded missions from San Diego, in 1769, to San Francisco, in 1776. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, led by Popé, expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years.

## Bourbon reforms and independence

In the late eighteenth century, the Bourbon reforms increased taxes and royal control, and expelled the Jesuits in 1767, angering criollos. After Napoleon took over Spain in 1808, criollos debated self-rule. On 16 September 1810, the priest Miguel Hidalgo issued the Grito de Dolores, calling for revolt. After Hidalgo's execution in 1811 and Morelos's in 1815, Agustín de Iturbide, a royalist officer, allied with the rebel Vicente Guerrero, and Mexico became independent in 1821.

## On our maps

Our maps show New Spain from 1530 to 1815. Its largest outline, about 4.6 million square kilometres on the 1815 map, is half in today's United States, reflecting Spanish claims in the west, and most of the rest in Mexico. Mexico City lies inside it throughout, and San Antonio, Los Angeles and San Francisco from 1783. On the 1878 map, the region belongs to Mexico and the United States.
