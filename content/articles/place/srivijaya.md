Srivijaya was a maritime empire based at Palembang, on the island of Sumatra, that controlled the sea lanes of western Southeast Asia from the seventh century to the eleventh. Its power rested less on land than on the Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait, the narrow passages that ships between China and India had to pass through.

## Rediscovery

Srivijaya was almost forgotten until 1918, when the French scholar George Cœdès identified it from Chinese records, Arabic texts and Old Malay inscriptions. Chinese sources call it Shilifoshi and later Sanfoqi, and Arab writers called its ruler the Maharaja of Zabag, describing his great wealth. The Kedukan Bukit inscription of 682, found near Palembang, records a king's expedition with 20,000 troops and the founding of a settlement, and other inscriptions threaten curses on those who disobey the king.

## A Buddhist centre

Srivijaya was a major centre of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism. The Chinese monk Yijing stayed for months on his way to India in 671 and spent years there later, translating texts and recommending that Chinese monks study in Srivijaya before going to India. Over a thousand monks lived there, he wrote. The Indian scholar Atisha studied in Srivijaya with the teacher Dharmakirti in the early eleventh century before going to Tibet. Srivijaya's kings endowed monasteries at Nalanda in India and Nagapattinam in the Chola kingdom.

## Connections with Java

Srivijaya had close dynastic and religious ties with the Sailendra dynasty of central Java, the builders of Borobudur, the largest Buddhist monument in the world, completed around 825. A Sailendra prince, Balaputra, became king of Srivijaya in the ninth century. Srivijaya and the Javanese kingdom of Mataram, and later others, were often rivals.

## A sea kingdom

Srivijaya's power was based on control of the Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait, and of trade between China, India and the Middle East. It sent tribute missions to the Chinese court, which helped secure trade privileges. Its ports traded camphor, spices, aromatic woods, gold and tin. The kingdom's capital, Palembang, built on the Musi river, had houses on rafts and stilts; archaeologists have found little of the capital, perhaps because it was built of wood.

## The Chola attack

In 1025 the Chola king Rajendra I of southern India launched a naval expedition that attacked Srivijaya's ports, including Palembang and Kedah, and captured the king. The reasons are debated: possibly a trade dispute, or an attempt to control the routes to China. Srivijaya survived, but its dominance weakened, and the centre shifted to Jambi.

## Decline

In the thirteenth century, Srivijaya's vassals broke away, and the Javanese kingdom of Singhasari and then Majapahit extended influence over Sumatra. Chinese records say Palembang was conquered by Java in the 1370s or 1390s. According to Malay tradition, a prince of Palembang, Parameswara, fled to Temasek, today's Singapore, and then founded Malacca around 1400, which became the leading port of the region.

## On our maps

Our maps show Srivijaya on every map from 800 to 1400. It is largest on the 1000 map at about 720,000 square kilometres: over 70 percent in today's Indonesia, most of the rest on the Malay peninsula. The sites of Jakarta, Singapore and Kuala Lumpur lie inside it on our maps. The basemap keeps the name later than most historians would, since by the thirteenth century Srivijaya's power was largely gone.
