The Third Intermediate Period is the name Egyptologists give to the four centuries after the end of the New Kingdom, from about 1070 to 664 BCE. Egypt was often divided between rival rulers, and for long stretches it was governed by kings of foreign origin, first Libyans and then Nubians.

## Tanis and Thebes

At the start of the period, the Twenty-first Dynasty kings ruled from Tanis, in the north-eastern delta, which they built with stone reused from Pi-Ramesses. In 1939 the French archaeologist Pierre Montet found royal tombs at Tanis, with silver coffins and gold masks, including that of Psusennes I, rivalling Tutankhamun's treasures, though overshadowed by the outbreak of the Second World War. Meanwhile, the high priests of Amun at Thebes, such as Pinedjem I, ruled the south and sometimes took royal titles. Concerned by tomb robbery, they gathered the mummies of the great New Kingdom kings into caches, such as the one at Deir el-Bahari found in 1881.

## The Libyan dynasties

Libyans had settled in Egypt as soldiers, and their leaders, chiefs of the Meshwesh and Libu, became powerful. Shoshenq I founded the Twenty-second Dynasty around 945 BCE and reunited the country for a time, appointing his son as high priest of Amun. He campaigned in Palestine, recording the conquered towns on a wall at Karnak, the Bubastite Portal. Under his successors, Egypt fragmented: by the eighth century BCE, several dynasties ruled at once, from cities such as Tanis, Leontopolis, Herakleopolis and Sais, along with Libyan chiefs.

## The Kushite conquest

In Nubia, the kingdom of Kush, centred on Napata, had adopted Egyptian religion, especially the worship of Amun. Around 728 BCE, the Kushite king Piye marched north, took Memphis and received the submission of the Egyptian rulers, recording his victory on a stele at Jebel Barkal, in which he describes his anger at a ruler who mistreated his horses. His successors, Shabaka, Shebitku and Taharqa, ruled Egypt as the Twenty-fifth Dynasty.

## The Kushite pharaohs

The Kushite pharaohs restored temples, built at Karnak, and revived old traditions in art and religion, copying Old Kingdom styles. The Shabaka Stone records an ancient theology of Memphis. Taharqa, who ruled from 690 to 664 BCE, is mentioned in the Bible as coming to the aid of Judah against Assyria. The Kushite kings were buried in pyramids at el-Kurru and Nuri, in Sudan.

## The Assyrian invasions

The Kushites supported resistance against Assyria in the Levant. The Assyrian king Esarhaddon invaded Egypt in 671 BCE and took Memphis, and Ashurbanipal sacked Thebes in 663 BCE, a disaster remembered in the Bible. The Assyrians ruled through local princes, such as Necho I of Sais.

## The Saite revival

Necho's son, Psamtik I, used Greek and Carian mercenaries to unite Egypt and threw off Assyrian control. His Twenty-sixth Dynasty, ruling from Sais from 664 BCE, brought a revival known as the Saite period, with renewed prosperity, trade with Greece, the founding of the Greek trading town of Naukratis, and a return to classical art styles. Necho II began a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea. The Persians conquered Egypt in 525 BCE.

## On our maps

Our maps show this period on the 1000 and 700 BCE maps, with an outline of about 260,000 square kilometres, all within today's Egypt. The basemap draws the country as a single unit even though, for much of this time, it was divided among several rulers. On the 500 BCE map Egypt is part of the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire).
