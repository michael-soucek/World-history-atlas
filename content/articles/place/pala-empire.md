For about four hundred years, from the middle of the eighth century to the twelfth, eastern India was ruled by the Pala dynasty, whose name means "protector". The Pala kings governed from Bengal and Bihar, along the lower Ganges, and at their height in the early ninth century they were the strongest power in northern India.

## An elected king

The dynasty began unusually. After a long stretch of disorder in Bengal, local chiefs chose a ruler named Gopala, around 750, rather than accept one by conquest or inheritance. His successors, Dharmapala and Devapala, turned that regional kingdom into an empire. They pushed west along the Gangetic plain and competed for decades with two other great powers, the Gurjara-Pratiharas in the north-west and the Rashtrakutas in the Deccan, in a three-way struggle over the old imperial city of Kannauj.

## The great kings

Gopala's son Dharmapala, who ruled from roughly 770 to 810, was the empire's most ambitious king. He marched west along the Ganges, installed his own candidate on the throne of Kannauj, and held a great assembly there at which, according to a Pala inscription, the rulers of northern India acknowledged him as overlord. He lost Kannauj again to the Gurjara-Pratiharas, and the Rashtrakuta king Dhruva defeated both rivals in a raid from the Deccan, but Dharmapala kept his core lands and his prestige.

His son Devapala, who reigned for about forty years in the ninth century, pushed Pala power to its greatest reach. Inscriptions credit him with campaigns against the Utkalas of Odisha, the Pragjyotisha kingdom of Assam, the Huns of the north-west and the Dravida kings of the south. Court poets exaggerated freely, so historians read these lists with care, but there is little doubt that the Pala kingdom was then the leading power of eastern India and a serious contender for the whole Gangetic plain.

## Kannauj and the three-way struggle

For about two centuries northern Indian politics revolved around Kannauj, the old capital of Harsha's empire on the upper Ganges. Whoever held it could claim to be the paramount ruler of the north. The Palas from the east, the Gurjara-Pratiharas from Rajasthan and Malwa, and the Rashtrakutas from the Deccan fought for it repeatedly. Historians call this the tripartite struggle. None of the three managed to hold Kannauj and the rest of their kingdoms for long, and the drawn-out contest weakened all of them, which helped open the way for later invaders from the north-west.

## How the empire was governed

The Pala kings ruled through a mix of direct administration and alliances with local lords, called samantas. Copper-plate land grants, the main surviving documents, show a hierarchy of officials responsible for revenue, justice, police and the army, and villages organised into districts. Royal grants of land to Brahmins and monasteries were recorded in detail, with boundaries described field by field. The army relied on elephants, cavalry and a river fleet, important in the waterlogged delta. Over time, the samantas grew stronger, and by the eleventh century the kings depended on them for troops and loyalty.

## Religion and learning

The Pala kings were Mahayana Buddhists, though they also gave grants to Hindu temples and Brahmin scholars, and several of their queens and ministers were Hindu. Under Pala patronage, the great monastic universities of Nalanda and Vikramashila drew students from across Asia. Vikramashila, founded by Dharmapala, specialised in Tantric Buddhism. The Somapura Mahavihara at Paharpur, in today's Bangladesh, was one of the largest monasteries south of the Himalayas, built around a vast cross-shaped temple.

The most famous scholar of the age was Atisha Dipankara Srijnana, a teacher at Vikramashila who travelled to Tibet in 1042 at the invitation of a Tibetan king. His teaching shaped the revival of Buddhism there, and he is still revered by Tibetan Buddhists.

## Art and trade

Pala workshops produced bronze and black-stone sculptures of Buddhas, bodhisattvas and Hindu gods, with slender figures and elaborate ornament. Illustrated palm-leaf manuscripts from the period are some of the earliest surviving Indian paintings. Through monks, pilgrims and merchants, the Pala style spread to Nepal, Tibet, Burma and Indonesia; similar images have been found at Buddhist sites in Java. The empire's ports on the Bay of Bengal, such as Tamralipta, connected it with Southeast Asia, and the Palas exchanged envoys with the Srivijaya kings of Sumatra, one of whom endowed a monastery at Nalanda.

## The Chola raid and the last revival

In the early eleventh century, the Chola king Rajendra I of southern India sent an army north that reached the Ganges and defeated the Pala king Mahipala I. The raid did not lead to conquest, and Mahipala restored much of the kingdom's strength. Later in the century, the Kaivarta revolt in Varendra, northern Bengal, drove the Palas out of their own heartland for a time. Ramapala, the last strong king, recovered Varendra with the help of his vassals around 1070–1080, an event celebrated in the Sanskrit poem Ramacharita, which can be read as the story of both Rama and Ramapala at once.

## Decline

Pala power faded slowly. The kings came to depend on local lords, and a revolt in northern Bengal in the eleventh century exposed how little control they had left. In the twelfth century the Hindu [Sena dynasty](/place/sena-dynasty) took Bengal, and the last Pala rulers held on only in Bihar before disappearing from the record between about 1161 and 1174; sources differ on the date.

## Legacy

The Palas' long patronage of Buddhism made eastern India the last great centre of the religion in its homeland. When Turkish armies sacked Nalanda and Vikramashila around 1200, shortly after the Palas fell, Buddhist learning in India never recovered, and the traditions the Palas had supported survived mainly in Tibet and the Himalayas. In Bengal itself, the Pala era is remembered as a formative period for the region's language, art and identity.
