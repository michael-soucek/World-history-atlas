The Scythians were horse-riding nomads who spoke an Iranian language and dominated the steppe north of the Black Sea for roughly five centuries, from the eighth century BCE to the third. They never built an empire with fixed borders or a single capital. Their power came from mobility: mounted archers who could strike and withdraw faster than any settled army could follow.

## Who were the Scythians?

The Scythians belonged to a wider family of Iranian-speaking nomads who ranged across the Eurasian steppe, from the Danube to Mongolia, in the first millennium BCE. Persians called the eastern groups Saka; Greeks used Scythians for those north of the Black Sea and often for steppe peoples in general. Archaeologists group their material culture, with its horse gear, weapons and animal-style art, as the "Scythian triad", and similar finds appear as far east as the Altai mountains and Tuva. Genetic studies suggest that these steppe groups were mixed in origin, with ancestry from both western and eastern Eurasia.

## Life on the steppe

The Scythians lived largely on horseback and in wagons, moving with their herds of horses, sheep and cattle between summer and winter pastures. Herodotus described wagons with felt tents drawn by oxen. They drank mare's milk and ate meat and cheese. Some Scythian groups, and peoples living under their rule, farmed grain in the river valleys of Ukraine, which was exported through Greek ports. Both men and some women were warriors: burials of women with weapons and armour have been found, and these may lie behind the Greek legends of the Amazons.

## Raiders and neighbours

Arriving from Central Asia, the Scythians displaced the Cimmerians on the Pontic steppe and in the seventh century BCE crossed the Caucasus to raid the Near East, reaching as far as Assyria and the borders of Egypt. The Medes eventually drove them back north. Later, in the late sixth century BCE, the Persian king Darius I marched into their country, and the Scythians beat him by refusing battle, retreating and burning the land behind them.

## Warfare

The Scythians fought as mounted archers, using a short, powerful composite bow and arrows with bronze or iron heads, kept in a combined bow-case and quiver called a gorytos. They also used short swords, the akinakes, and battle-axes. Herodotus describes grim customs: warriors drank the blood of the first enemy they killed, brought heads to the king to claim a share of the booty, and made cups of skulls. Their tactics of retreat and harassment frustrated larger armies, as the Persian king Darius I found when he invaded around 513 BCE.

## Kings and burial mounds

Scythian society was led by kings and an aristocracy whose power is visible in their burial mounds, or kurgans. Royal kurgans such as Chertomlyk, Solokha and Tolstaya Mogila in Ukraine, and Arzhan in Tuva, contained the bodies of kings and their attendants, dozens of sacrificed horses, weapons, and objects of gold. A gold pectoral from Tolstaya Mogila, excavated in 1971, shows scenes of herders milking ewes and sewing a sheepskin shirt, with griffins attacking horses below. In the permafrost of the Altai, at Pazyryk, related burials preserved tattooed bodies, felt carpets, saddles and clothing.

## Religion

According to Herodotus, the Scythians worshipped a group of gods led by Tabiti, a goddess of the hearth, and honoured a war god, represented by an iron sword set on a great pile of brushwood, to which they sacrificed animals and prisoners. Diviners used bundles of willow rods. Herodotus also tells how they threw hemp seeds onto hot stones inside felt tents and "howled with joy" in the vapour; archaeologists have since found braziers with charred hemp seeds in Altai burials.

## The Greeks and the Scythians

Greek colonies on the northern Black Sea coast, such as Olbia, Panticapaeum and Chersonesus, traded with the Scythians, buying grain, hides and slaves, and selling wine, olive oil, pottery and fine metalwork. Greek craftsmen made many of the finest "Scythian" gold objects, combining Greek realism with Scythian themes. The Greeks were fascinated by the Scythians, and the Scythian prince Anacharsis was later counted among the wise men of Greece.

## Decline and later names

In the fourth century BCE the Scythian king Ateas built a large kingdom reaching the Danube, but he was killed in battle against Philip II of Macedon in 339 BCE. The Sarmatians, another Iranian-speaking people, then pushed into the steppe, and Scythian power contracted to Crimea, where a kingdom centred on Scythian Neapolis survived until the Goths and others overran it in the third century CE. Long afterwards, Byzantine and other writers went on calling various steppe peoples, including Huns, Slavs and Turks, Scythians.

## On our maps

Our maps label a huge stretch of the Eurasian steppe "Scythians" from 400 BCE to 100 CE, at its largest about 7 million square kilometres on the 200 BCE map, from Ukraine across Kazakhstan to Afghanistan. That label follows ancient usage rather than a single state: Greek and Persian writers called many related steppe peoples Scythians or Saka, and the shape stands for that whole world of nomads rather than one kingdom. The western Scythians proper were pushed out of the Pontic steppe by the Sarmatians in the third and second centuries BCE and survived in Crimea until the Goths overran them in the third century CE.
