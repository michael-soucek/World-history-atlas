The Early Dynastic Period is the first age of a united Egypt, covering the First and Second Dynasties, from around 3100 BCE to about 2686 BCE. In these four centuries the institutions that would last for three thousand years took shape: divine kingship, hieroglyphic writing, a royal administration and monumental tombs.

## The First Dynasty

The First Dynasty kings, beginning around 3100 BCE, included Narmer or Hor-Aha, Djer, Djet, the queen Merneith, who may have ruled as regent, Den, Anedjib, Semerkhet and Qa'a. Den, one of the most important, is shown on an ivory label smiting an eastern enemy and running in the Sed festival, a ritual of royal renewal. Kings led campaigns into Nubia, Sinai and the Levant to secure copper, turquoise, cedar wood and other goods.

## The Second Dynasty

The Second Dynasty, from about 2890 to 2686 BCE, is less well known. Its early kings were buried at Saqqara. In the middle of the dynasty, there seems to have been a period of conflict: King Peribsen replaced the falcon Horus on his serekh with the god Seth, and his successor Khasekhemwy used both Horus and Seth, perhaps symbolising the reconciliation of rival factions. Khasekhemwy built a huge mud-brick enclosure at Hierakonpolis and Abydos, and his reign led into the Third Dynasty.

## The capital

Memphis, near the apex of the delta, became the administrative capital, called "the White Walls". The site of Saqqara, on the desert edge above Memphis, became the burial place of high officials, in large mastaba tombs with recessed brick facades, once thought to be royal tombs. The royal tombs were at Abydos, in Upper Egypt, near the region of Thinis, the dynasty's home.

## Kingship and religion

The king was the living Horus, the falcon god, who guaranteed maat, the order of the universe, and defeated chaos. Royal names were written in the serekh. The Sed festival celebrated the king's renewal after many years of rule. Gods such as Ptah of Memphis, Neith, Hathor and Min appear in early records. Animal cults, such as the Apis bull of Memphis, were already important.

## Writing and administration

Hieroglyphic writing expanded from labels and royal names to longer inscriptions. Officials kept records of goods and taxes, and the Palermo Stone, a later annal carved in the Fifth Dynasty, lists the events of each year of the early kings, such as festivals, the height of the Nile flood and the making of statues. A biennial count of cattle served for taxation. The kings controlled large estates, and the economy was organised around royal and temple storehouses.

## Burials and sacrifice

The First Dynasty royal tombs at Abydos, in the area called Umm el-Qa'ab, were large underground chambers lined with wood and brick, surrounded by rows of subsidiary graves for servants, officials and even dogs, who seem to have been killed to accompany the king. King Djer's tomb had over 300 subsidiary burials. This practice ended by the Second Dynasty. Funerary enclosures, large brick structures near the cultivated land, may have been forerunners of the pyramid temples.

## Toward the pyramids

At the start of the Third Dynasty, around 2670 BCE, King Djoser's architect Imhotep built the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, the first large stone building in the world, a stack of six mastabas inside a vast enclosure. The Early Dynastic Period had laid the foundations of the Old Kingdom, the age of the great pyramids.

Our maps show Early Dynastic Egypt on the 3000 BCE map, with an outline of about 320,000 square kilometres along the Nile, nearly nine-tenths of it in today's Egypt and the rest in Sudan.
