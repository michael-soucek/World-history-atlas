Tibet, on the high plateau north of the Himalayas, is the homeland of the Tibetan people and the centre of Tibetan Buddhism. It has been a great empire, a patchwork of rival religious and noble houses, a theocracy under the Dalai Lamas, and, since 1950, part of the People's Republic of China. For the imperial period, see the [Tibetan Empire](/place/tibetan-empire).

## After the empire

The Tibetan Empire collapsed in the mid-ninth century, and Tibet broke into many local states, ruled by descendants of the royal family and noble clans. In the west, the kingdom of Guge invited Indian Buddhist teachers. The Indian monk Atisha arrived in 1042, and Tibetan translators such as Rinchen Zangpo brought new texts, beginning the "second diffusion" of Buddhism. Great monastic schools were founded: the Kagyu, with the poet-yogi Milarepa, and the Sakya, with its monastery founded in 1073.

## The Sakya and the Mongols

In the thirteenth century, the Mongols threatened Tibet. In 1247 the Sakya lama Sakya Pandita met the Mongol prince Köden and agreed to Tibet's submission. His nephew Phagpa became the religious teacher of Kublai Khan, who made the Sakya lamas rulers of Tibet under Mongol overlordship. Phagpa created a script for writing Mongolian and Chinese. After the Yuan fell, the Phagmodrupa family ruled central Tibet from 1354.

## The Dalai Lamas

In the early fifteenth century, the reformer Tsongkhapa founded the Gelug school, which emphasised monastic discipline and study, and its great monasteries, Ganden, Drepung and Sera, near Lhasa. In 1578 the Mongol ruler Altan Khan gave the Gelug leader Sonam Gyatso the title Dalai Lama, "ocean-like teacher". The Dalai Lamas were believed to be reincarnations of their predecessors and of the bodhisattva of compassion, Avalokiteshvara, and were identified as children after the death of the previous one.

## The Great Fifth

With the help of the Khoshut Mongol leader Güshi Khan, the fifth Dalai Lama, Ngawang Lobsang Gyatso, defeated rival rulers and became ruler of Tibet in 1642, establishing the Ganden Phodrang government. He built the Potala Palace in Lhasa, from 1645, and expanded the Gelug school. The Panchen Lama, a high lama based at Tashilhunpo, became the second most important figure.

## Qing overlordship

After a period of conflict, including a Dzungar invasion in 1717, the Qing dynasty sent troops to Lhasa in 1720 and installed the seventh Dalai Lama. From 1728 the Qing kept representatives, ambans, and a garrison in Lhasa. In 1793 the Qing introduced the Golden Urn to select reincarnations. Qing authority varied, and Tibet's government managed most internal affairs.

## The thirteenth Dalai Lama

In 1903–1904, a British expedition under Francis Younghusband invaded Tibet, killing hundreds of Tibetans at Guru, and reached Lhasa. After the fall of the Qing in 1912, the thirteenth Dalai Lama, Thubten Gyatso, expelled the Chinese troops and declared Tibet's independence in 1913. He attempted some modernisation. Tibet functioned as an independent state until 1950, though China did not recognise this and few countries formally recognised it.

## Chinese rule

In 1950 the People's Liberation Army invaded eastern Tibet, defeating the Tibetan army at Chamdo. In 1951 Tibetan representatives signed the Seventeen Point Agreement, accepting Chinese sovereignty with promises of autonomy. Resistance grew, especially in the eastern regions of Kham and Amdo. In March 1959 an uprising in Lhasa was crushed, and the fourteenth Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso, fled to India, where he set up a government in exile in Dharamsala; tens of thousands of Tibetans followed. During the Cultural Revolution, thousands of monasteries were destroyed. The Tibet Autonomous Region was created in 1965. Protests in 1987–1989 and 2008 were suppressed. The Dalai Lama received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1989. Tibet's status, and the selection of future Dalai Lamas, remain disputed between the Tibetan exile community and China.

## On our maps

Our maps show Tibet on most maps from 1000 to 1715 and from 1914 to 1945. Its largest outline, about 2.3 million square kilometres on the 1000 map, covers much of the Tibetan plateau, now mostly in China, with smaller shares in India and Nepal. Lhasa lies inside it for much of the period. Between 1783 and 1900 the basemap includes it in the Qing Empire; by 1960 it is part of China.
