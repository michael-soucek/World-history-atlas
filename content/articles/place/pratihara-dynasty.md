Between the 8th and early 11th centuries, the Gurjara-Pratihara kings rose from a regional power in what is now Rajasthan and Gujarat to become masters of Kannauj, the prized city of the Ganges plain, and the leading dynasty of northern India. Founded around 730 by Nagabhata I, they are remembered above all for holding back Arab armies based in Sindh, and for a long three-way contest with the Palas and the Rashtrakutas. Their rule at Kannauj ended in the 1030s.

## Name and origins

The kings called themselves Pratiharas, "door-keepers", claiming descent from Lakshmana, who in legend kept the door for his brother Rama. Neighbouring dynasties' inscriptions call them Gurjaras, and the meaning of that word is debated. Some historians take it to be the name of their homeland, Gurjaradesa; others see it as a tribal name, and a minority have argued that the Gurjaras arrived from outside India in the wake of the Huna invasions, a view critics say lacks firm evidence. Some scholars count the Pratiharas among the earliest Rajput dynasties. Even the site of their first capital, perhaps Jalor or Bhinmal, or Ujjain, is disputed.

## Resisting the Caliphate

Nagabhata I, perhaps once a vassal of the Chavdas of Bhinmal, came to the fore resisting the Arab forces who held Sindh. In a battle dated to 738 he defeated an Arab army, and a later inscription celebrates him for crushing the great host of a foreign king. He extended his rule across Malwa as far as Gwalior and the port of Bharuch. In 851 an Arab writer, Sulaiman, called the Pratihara king the greatest enemy of Islam among Indian princes, with the finest cavalry in India.

## The struggle for Kannauj

After [Harsha](/person/harsha) died without an heir in the 7th century, control of Kannauj became the great prize of northern Indian politics. Vatsaraja, who ruled from about 775, campaigned against the [Pala Empire](/place/pala-empire) of Bengal but was defeated by the [Rashtrakuta](/place/rashtrakuta-empire) king Dhruva. His son Nagabhata II, after early setbacks against Govinda III, took Kannauj and pushed east as far as Bihar; the city became the Pratihara capital. Historians call this long contest the Tripartite Struggle.

## Height under Mihira Bhoja

Mihira Bhoja (836–885) and his son Mahendrapala I (885–910) brought the dynasty to its peak. Bhoja crushed rebellious vassals in Rajasthan, pushed the Arabs out of Kutch, and fought both Palas and Rashtrakutas. At its widest the realm reached from the edge of Sindh to Bengal and from the Himalayas to beyond the Narmada, an extent compared to that of the [Gupta Empire](/place/gupta-empire). A 10th-century Persian geography credits the king of Kannauj with 150,000 cavalry and 800 war elephants. The Pratiharas built temples in an open pavilion style, and the Maru-Gurjara style of temple architecture developed under their rule.

## Decline

Disputes over the throne after 910 gave vassals their chance. The Paramaras, Chandelas, Kalachuris and Chahamanas broke away, and around 916 the Rashtrakuta king Indra III sacked Kannauj. The Pratiharas regained the city but never recovered their strength, and the Chandelas took the fortress of Gwalior about 950. As the 10th century closed, little more than the land around Kannauj remained.

## The end

When [Mahmud of Ghazni](/person/mahmud-of-ghazni) captured Kannauj in 1018, King Rajyapala fled. The Chandela ruler Vidyadhara had him killed for his flight and surrender, then installed his son Trilochanpala as king. Jasapala, the last of the line at Kannauj, died in 1036. Pratihara branches survived elsewhere, one ruling at Mandore into the 14th century.
