The Roman Empire began in 27 BCE, when Octavian, the victor of Rome's civil wars, took the name Augustus. He avoided calling himself king, and the senate and elected offices continued, but from then on one man commanded the armies and appointed the governors. For the next two centuries the empire enjoyed the long stability Romans called the pax Romana.

## Emperors good and bad

After Augustus, the Julio-Claudian dynasty included Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, who added Britain in 43 CE, and Nero, under whom Rome burned in 64 CE. Nero's suicide in 68 CE led to the "year of the four emperors", won by Vespasian, whose Flavian dynasty built the Colosseum. The second century saw the so-called Five Good Emperors: Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, who chose capable heirs by adoption. Under Trajan, who conquered Dacia and briefly Mesopotamia, the empire reached its greatest extent around 117 CE.

## The army and the frontiers

The empire was defended by about thirty legions of citizen soldiers, plus a similar number of auxiliary troops recruited from non-citizens, who gained citizenship after 25 years' service. Most were stationed along the frontiers: the Rhine and Danube rivers, the eastern frontier with Parthia and later Persia, the desert edges of Africa, and, in Britain, Hadrian's Wall, built from 122 CE. Military roads, forts and towns along the frontier spread Roman culture.

## Economy and trade

The Mediterranean became, in Roman eyes, "our sea", mare nostrum. Grain from Egypt and North Africa fed Rome, whose population may have reached a million. Wine, olive oil and pottery were shipped in huge quantities, and Roman goods reached India, while silk came from China. Mines in Spain produced silver and gold. A large share of the population was enslaved, especially in Italy, though many slaves were freed, and their descendants became citizens.

## Cities and daily life

Life in the empire centred on its cities, with forums, temples, public baths, aqueducts, theatres and amphitheatres. Gladiator fights and chariot races in the Circus Maximus were enormously popular. The eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE buried Pompeii and Herculaneum, preserving houses, paintings and graffiti that give a vivid picture of everyday life. Latin literature flourished with Virgil, Horace, Ovid, Livy, Seneca and Tacitus.

## Religion and Christianity

The empire tolerated many religions, but expected subjects to honour the emperor and the state gods. Jews, whose faith forbade this, were usually exempted, though Jewish revolts in Judaea were crushed, with the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. Christianity spread through the cities of the empire and was persecuted at times, most severely under Decius and Diocletian, before Constantine's conversion.

## The crisis of the third century

From 235 to 284 CE, the empire faced civil wars, invasions by Goths and other Germanic peoples, a powerful new enemy in Sasanian Persia, plague and inflation. Over twenty emperors ruled in fifty years, most dying violently. The emperor Valerian was captured by the Persians in 260, and for a time breakaway states formed in Gaul and in Palmyra under Queen Zenobia, before Aurelian reunited the empire.

## Diocletian and Constantine

Diocletian, from 284, divided power among four rulers, the tetrarchy, doubled the number of provinces, enlarged the army and bureaucracy, tried to fix prices and launched the last great persecution of Christians. Constantine restored rule by one emperor, moved the capital to Constantinople and favoured Christianity. Theodosius I banned pagan sacrifices in the 390s.

## Two endings

The western half lost its provinces to Germanic kingdoms in the fifth century, and the last western emperor was deposed in 476. The eastern half, which historians call the Byzantine Empire, survived until the Ottomans took Constantinople in 1453. Its people called themselves Romans throughout.

## Legacy

Roman law, Latin, roads, cities and the Church shaped Europe long after the empire's fall. Many later states claimed to be its heirs, from the Byzantine Empire and the Holy Roman Empire to Russia's tsars and the Ottomans, who called themselves rulers of Rum.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the united empire from 1 BCE to 300 CE. It is largest on the 200 map, at about 4.9 million square kilometres. The biggest shares now lie in Turkey, Egypt, France and Spain, with Italy itself only about 6 percent. Cities Roman on our maps across this period include London, Paris, Rome, Athens, Istanbul, Jerusalem, Alexandria and Damascus. On the 400 map the space is split between a Western and an Eastern Roman Empire.
