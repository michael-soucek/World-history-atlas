The Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and was one of the richest states in the world. Its founder, Babur, was a Timurid prince from Central Asia, descended from Timur on his father's side and from Genghis Khan on his mother's. The name "Mughal" comes from the Persian word for Mongol.

## Babur and Humayun

Babur, the founder of the dynasty, wrote one of the first autobiographies in Islamic literature, the Baburnama, in Chagatai Turkic, describing his battles, the gardens he built and his opinions of India, which he found strange and lacking good fruit. His son Humayun was driven out by the Afghan Sher Shah Suri, who built roads, introduced the silver rupee and reformed land revenue in the 1540s. Humayun spent fifteen years in exile, partly at the Safavid court, before retaking Delhi in 1555. He died soon after, falling down the steps of his library.

## The mansabdari system

Akbar organised the Mughal elite in a system of ranks, mansabs. Each mansabdar held a numerical rank that set his salary and the number of cavalry he had to maintain. Most were paid through jagirs, assignments of land revenue, which were rotated regularly so that nobles could not build local power bases. The nobility included Turks, Persians, Afghans, Indian Muslims and Hindu Rajputs. Land revenue was assessed through careful surveys by the minister Todar Mal.

## Religion

Akbar abolished the jizya and the pilgrim tax on Hindus, and held debates among scholars of many faiths at his Ibadat Khana in [Fatehpur Sikri](/site/fatehpur-sikri). His son Jahangir and grandson Shah Jahan continued a broadly tolerant policy, though with more emphasis on Islam. Dara Shikoh, Shah Jahan's eldest son, translated the Upanishads into Persian and wrote on the common ground between Islam and Hinduism. Aurangzeb, who defeated and executed Dara, followed a stricter religious policy, reimposing the jizya in 1679 and destroying some temples, though he also employed many Hindu officials.

## Art and architecture

Mughal painting combined Persian, Indian and, later, European influences, producing illustrated histories, portraits and nature studies. Jahangir was a connoisseur of painting and nature, and his memoirs, the Tuzuk-i Jahangiri, describe animals and plants in detail. Mughal architecture includes Humayun's Tomb in Delhi, Akbar's Fatehpur Sikri, Jahangir's gardens in Kashmir, and Shah Jahan's [Taj Mahal](/site/taj-mahal), [Red Fort](/site/red-fort) and Jama Masjid. Mughal gardens were designed as images of paradise, divided into four parts by water channels.

## Women at court

Several Mughal women were powerful. Jahangir's wife Nur Jahan effectively ran the government during his later years, issuing coins and orders in her own name. Shah Jahan's daughter Jahanara was one of the richest women in the empire, a patron of buildings and Sufi writer.

## Economy

The Mughal Empire was one of the largest economies in the world. India exported cotton textiles, indigo, saltpetre and spices, and imported silver and gold from Europe and the Americas. European trading companies, the Portuguese, Dutch, English and French, set up trading posts with Mughal permission. Cities such as Agra, Lahore, Delhi and Surat were among the largest in the world.

## Aurangzeb

Aurangzeb (1658–1707) extended the empire to its greatest size, conquering the Deccan sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda. But he spent his last decades fighting the Marathas in the Deccan, and his reimposition of the tax on non-Muslims and other policies alienated many of his subjects.

## Decline

After Aurangzeb's death in 1707, his successors faced wars of succession, rebellions by Marathas, Sikhs and Jats, and governors who became independent rulers, such as the Nizam of Hyderabad and the nawabs of Bengal and Awadh. Nader Shah of Persia sacked Delhi in 1739, carrying off the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond. The British East India Company took control of Bengal after Plassey in 1757 and Buxar in 1764, and the emperor became its pensioner. After the uprising of 1857, the British exiled the last emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, to Rangoon.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire from 1530 to 1715. It is largest on the 1700 map at about 3.9 million square kilometres, about 70 percent in today's India and most of the rest in Pakistan, Afghanistan, Nepal and Bangladesh. Delhi, Lahore and Kabul are Mughal throughout; Kolkata's site and Dhaka from 1650; Mumbai's and Chennai's sites from 1700. On the 1783 map most of the region belongs to the Marathas.
