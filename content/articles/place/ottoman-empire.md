The Ottoman Empire began around 1300 as one of many small Turkish principalities in north-western Anatolia, led by a chief named Osman. It grew into an empire spanning three continents, conquered Constantinople, and lasted until 1922, longer than almost any other dynasty in history.

## From frontier state to empire

Osman's successors expanded into Byzantine territory and across into Europe, taking Bursa, then Edirne, and defeating the Serbs at Kosovo in 1389. Timur's defeat of Sultan Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402 nearly destroyed the state, but it recovered. In 1453 Mehmed II took Constantinople, which became the Ottoman capital, Istanbul. His grandson Selim I conquered the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517, adding Syria, Egypt and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina.

## Suleiman and the classical age

Under Suleiman the Magnificent (1520–1566) the empire reached the height of its power. Ottoman armies took Belgrade, crushed Hungary at Mohács in 1526 and besieged Vienna in 1529, while its fleets dominated the Mediterranean. The empire ruled a vast population of Muslims, Christians and Jews, allowing religious communities to run many of their own affairs. The architect Sinan built mosques that still shape Istanbul's skyline.

## The sultan and his household

The Ottoman sultans ruled with absolute authority in theory, drawing their legitimacy from their dynasty, from Islam, and from their role as protectors of the holy cities. In practice they governed through a vast household. The devshirme system collected Christian boys from the Balkans, converted them to Islam and trained them as janissaries, the elite infantry, or as palace servants and administrators; many rose to become grand viziers. The Topkapı Palace in Istanbul was the centre of government, and the harem, home to the sultan's mother, wives and concubines, was also a centre of political power, especially in the seventeenth century, the "Sultanate of Women".

## The millet system

The empire ruled a highly diverse population. Non-Muslims, mainly Christians and Jews, were organised in religious communities, later called millets, under their own leaders, the Orthodox patriarch, the Armenian patriarch and the chief rabbi, who handled religious, family and educational matters. Non-Muslims paid a special tax and faced some restrictions, but they were generally allowed to practise their faiths. After the expulsion of Jews from Spain in 1492, many found refuge in the Ottoman Empire, especially in Thessaloniki and Istanbul.

## Law and administration

The empire combined Islamic law, sharia, with sultanic law, kanun, issued by the sultans. Provinces were governed by beylerbeys and sanjak beys. Many cavalrymen held timars, grants of land revenue in return for military service. Ottoman bureaucrats kept detailed tax registers. Suleiman the Magnificent was known in Turkey as Kanuni, "the Lawgiver".

## Culture

Ottoman architecture reached its height under Mimar Sinan, the chief architect of the sixteenth century, who designed over three hundred buildings, including the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul and the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne. Iznik tiles, calligraphy, miniature painting and carpets flourished. Coffee houses spread in the sixteenth century. The traveller Evliya Çelebi described the empire in a ten-volume travel book in the seventeenth century.

## The navy and the Mediterranean

Under Hayreddin Barbarossa and his successors, the Ottoman navy dominated the eastern Mediterranean and North Africa. The Ottomans took Rhodes in 1522, Cyprus in 1571 and Crete in 1669. A Christian alliance, the Holy League, defeated the Ottoman fleet at Lepanto in 1571, but the Ottomans rebuilt it within a year.

## Reform and the "sick man of Europe"

From the late seventeenth century, the empire lost wars against Austria and Russia. The Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699 gave up Hungary, and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 gave Russia influence over the Black Sea. Sultan Selim III tried to create a modern army; Mahmud II destroyed the janissaries in 1826. The Tanzimat reforms from 1839 introduced equality before the law, modern schools and administration. A constitution was introduced in 1876 but suspended by Abdulhamid II in 1878. European writers called the empire the "sick man of Europe".

## The last years

Nationalism broke the Balkans away: Greece became independent in 1830, and Serbia, Romania and Montenegro in 1878. The Young Turks restored the constitution in 1908. The empire lost Libya to Italy in 1911–1912 and most of its remaining European lands in the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913. In the First World War, it fought on Germany's side, won at Gallipoli in 1915, but lost the Arab lands. During the war, the government carried out the Armenian genocide, in which around a million or more Armenians were killed, as well as mass killings of Assyrians and Greeks.

## The end

After defeat in 1918 the empire was occupied and partitioned. A Turkish national movement led by Mustafa Kemal, later known as Atatürk, defeated the occupying powers, abolished the sultanate in 1922 and founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923.
