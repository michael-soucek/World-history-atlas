Tiwanaku was one of the great cities of the ancient Andes, built near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, in what is now Bolivia, nearly 3,850 metres above sea level. Between about 500 and 1000 CE it was the centre of a state or cultural sphere whose influence reached across the southern Andes, into what are now Peru, Chile and Argentina.

## The Titicaca basin

The Lake Titicaca basin, the high plain or altiplano, was cold and dry, but the lake moderated the climate, and people had grown potatoes, quinoa and other high-altitude crops and herded llamas and alpacas for thousands of years. Earlier cultures, such as Chiripa and Pukara, built sunken courts and temples. Tiwanaku began as a village around 1500 BCE and grew into a major ceremonial centre in the first centuries CE.

## The city

At its height, perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people lived in Tiwanaku, with more in the surrounding valley. The ceremonial core was surrounded by a moat. The Akapana, a large stepped platform mound faced with stone, had a sunken court on top and drains for water. The Kalasasaya, a raised enclosure with walls of large stone pillars, contains the Gateway of the Sun, carved from a single block of andesite, with a central figure, often called the Staff God, holding two staffs, surrounded by rows of winged attendants. The Semi-subterranean Temple has stone heads set into its walls. Large stone statues, such as the Bennett and Ponce monoliths, show figures holding cups and staffs.

## Puma Punku

The nearby Puma Punku complex has precisely cut stone blocks, some weighing over 100 tonnes, fitted together with metal clamps of copper alloy poured into carved notches. The blocks, with their standardised shapes, suggest sophisticated planning. The site has attracted speculative theories, but archaeology shows it was built by the people of Tiwanaku.

## Raised fields

Tiwanaku's farmers used raised fields, suka kollus, planting platforms separated by canals filled with water. The water absorbed the sun's heat during the day and released it at night, protecting crops from frost, and the canals provided nutrients from algae and fish. These fields could produce large harvests. In the 1980s, archaeologists and farmers experimented with restoring raised fields, with mixed success.

## Influence

Tiwanaku established colonies in lower valleys, such as Moquegua, in southern Peru, and Cochabamba, in Bolivia, to grow maize and other crops. Llama caravans carried goods, such as obsidian, textiles, ceramics, hallucinogenic snuff and copper, across the region, reaching the oases of San Pedro de Atacama in Chile. Tiwanaku pottery, such as the flared drinking cups called keros, and textiles spread its art. Its imagery is closely related to that of the Wari state, to the north, and scholars debate whether they were rivals, partners or shared a common religion.

## Decline

Around 1000 to 1150 CE, Tiwanaku declined and its centre was abandoned. Studies of lake sediments and ice cores suggest a long drought that would have made the raised fields unworkable, though some scholars emphasise political conflict and social breakdown. Some monuments were deliberately damaged. The region fragmented into smaller Aymara kingdoms, such as the Colla and Lupaca, later conquered by the Incas.

## Legacy

The Incas regarded Tiwanaku as a sacred place, linking it to the creation of the world by the god Viracocha, who was said to have created the sun and the first people at Lake Titicaca. Tiwanaku is a World Heritage Site and a symbol of Bolivian and Aymara identity; Bolivian presidents, such as Evo Morales, have held ceremonies there.
