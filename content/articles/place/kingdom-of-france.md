The Kingdom of France grew out of West Francia, the western part of Charlemagne's empire. Its usual starting date is 987, when the nobles elected Hugh Capet as king. His family, the Capetians, and their branches, the Valois and the Bourbons, ruled France for the next eight hundred years.

## The Capetians

Hugh Capet was elected king in 987, after the last Carolingian died. The early Capetians had little power outside the Île-de-France, but they had one great advantage: for over three centuries, each king had a son to succeed him, and they crowned their heirs during their own lifetimes. Louis VI and his adviser, Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis, strengthened royal authority in the early twelfth century; Suger's rebuilding of the abbey church of Saint-Denis began the Gothic style.

## Philip Augustus and Saint Louis

Philip II Augustus (1180–1223) fought the Plantagenet kings of England, who held Normandy, Anjou, Aquitaine and other lands. He took Normandy in 1204 and defeated an English-German alliance at Bouvines in 1214, transforming the monarchy's power. He built the walls of Paris and the Louvre fortress. The Albigensian Crusade against the Cathar heretics brought the south under royal control. Louis IX, Saint Louis (1226–1270), was revered for his piety and justice, built the Sainte-Chapelle and died on crusade near Tunis.

## The Valois and the Hundred Years' War

When the direct Capetian line ended in 1328, the throne passed to the Valois branch, but Edward III of England claimed it through his mother, beginning the Hundred Years' War. France suffered defeats at Crécy, Poitiers and Agincourt, the Black Death and peasant revolts, and a civil war between the Armagnacs and Burgundians. Joan of Arc's victories helped Charles VII, and by 1453 the English were driven out of all but Calais.

## The Renaissance and wars of religion

Charles VIII invaded Italy in 1494, beginning the Italian Wars. Francis I (1515–1547) brought the Renaissance to France, inviting Leonardo da Vinci, building the châteaux of the Loire and fighting Charles V. In the 1560s, conflict between Catholics and Protestant Huguenots led to the Wars of Religion, including the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre of 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed. Henry of Navarre, a Protestant, became Henry IV, converted to Catholicism, reportedly saying "Paris is well worth a mass", and issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598.

## Richelieu and Louis XIV

Under Louis XIII, Cardinal Richelieu crushed Huguenot political power and the nobility's independence and led France into the Thirty Years' War against the Habsburgs. Louis XIV, the Sun King, ruled personally from 1661, built Versailles, made France Europe's dominant power and revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685. His wars and building left huge debts.

## Colonies and trade

France founded Quebec in 1608 and claimed Louisiana in 1682. French colonies in the Caribbean, especially Saint-Domingue, produced sugar and coffee with enslaved labour, and France had trading posts in Senegal and India. In the Seven Years' War, ending in 1763, France lost Canada and most of its Indian possessions to Britain.

## The Ancien Régime

Under Louis XV and Louis XVI, French society was divided into three estates: clergy, nobility and commoners, with the nobility and clergy largely exempt from taxes. The Enlightenment flourished in Paris salons, with Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau and Diderot. Unable to reform the tax system, the monarchy fell into financial crisis, leading to the Revolution of 1789.

## Revolution

Debt from its wars, including support for the American Revolution, brought the monarchy to crisis. The meeting of the Estates-General in 1789 led to the French Revolution. A constitutional monarchy was set up in 1791, and in 1792 France became a republic; Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. The monarchy was restored from 1814 to 1848.

## On our maps

Our maps show the kingdom on every map from 1000 to 1783, largest on the 1715 map at about 1.1 million square kilometres. Nearly half of that figure lies in today's France and nearly half in Canada, because the basemap includes New France, along the St Lawrence and the Great Lakes, under the same name.
