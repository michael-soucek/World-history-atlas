The Almoravids were a Berber movement from the western Sahara that built an empire stretching from the Senegal river to Spain in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. They founded the city of Marrakesh, which gave its name to Morocco, and for half a century held back the Christian kingdoms in Iberia.

## Veiled men of the Sahara

The movement began among the Sanhaja Berber tribes of what is now Mauritania and Western Sahara, especially the Lamtuna, Gudala and Massufa, camel-herding nomads who controlled the caravan routes between the Sahel and Morocco. Their men wore a face veil, the litham, which later became a symbol of the Almoravid elite. Around 1040, a Gudala chief returning from pilgrimage brought back a religious scholar, Abdallah ibn Yasin, to teach his people Maliki Sunni Islam. Ibn Yasin imposed strict discipline. His followers were called al-Murabitun, probably meaning "the people of the ribat", a fortified religious community; the Spanish form of the name is Almoravids.

## Conquests in the Sahara and Morocco

Under the military leaders Yahya ibn Umar and his brother Abu Bakr ibn Umar, the Almoravids took the Saharan trading town of Sijilmasa, in southern Morocco, in 1054–1055 and Awdaghust, an important caravan town in the south, the same year. Ibn Yasin was killed in battle in 1059. Abu Bakr returned to the desert to deal with conflicts there and left his cousin Yusuf ibn Tashfin in command in Morocco. Yusuf founded Marrakesh around 1070, took Fez in 1075 and extended Almoravid rule to Tlemcen and Algiers. In the south, Abu Bakr was killed around 1087 fighting in the Sahel.

## Ghana

Arab sources report that the Almoravids conquered the Ghana Empire around 1076. Many historians now doubt that there was a military conquest, and see instead a gradual spread of Almoravid influence and Islam in the region, as well as conflict. Whatever happened, Almoravid control of the Saharan trade brought gold from West Africa to Morocco and Spain, and their gold dinars, the morabetinos, were widely used, even in Christian Spain.

## Al-Andalus

In 1085 Alfonso VI of Castile captured Toledo, alarming the rulers of the taifa kingdoms of al-Andalus. Al-Mutamid of Seville invited Yusuf to help, reportedly saying he would rather herd camels in Africa than pigs in Castile. Yusuf crossed in 1086 and defeated Alfonso at Sagrajas, or al-Zallaqa. Disgusted by the taifa kings' luxury, quarrels and payments of tribute to Christians, and with the support of religious scholars, Yusuf deposed them between 1090 and 1094 and annexed al-Andalus. Al-Mutamid, a poet-king, was exiled to Aghmat, near Marrakesh, where he wrote poems about his fall.

## Rule and religion

The Almoravids ruled through governors from the Lamtuna tribe and relied heavily on Maliki jurists, who gained great influence. They were strict in religious matters and burned the works of the theologian al-Ghazali, which they saw as dangerous. Andalusian culture influenced the rulers, and Andalusian craftsmen worked in Morocco. The Almoravid qubba in Marrakesh is one of the few surviving buildings of the dynasty. In 1094 the Castilian warrior Rodrigo Díaz, El Cid, took Valencia, holding it until his death; the Almoravids recovered it in 1102.

## Decline

Yusuf died in 1106, aged nearly a hundred, according to tradition. Under his son Ali ibn Yusuf, the Almoravids won at Uclés in 1108 but lost Zaragoza to Aragon in 1118. In the 1120s the Almohad movement rose in the Atlas mountains, attacking the Almoravids as corrupt. Christian kingdoms advanced, and revolts broke out in al-Andalus. The Almohads took Marrakesh in 1147, ending Almoravid rule; a branch survived in the Balearic Islands until 1203.
