Assyria was a Mesopotamian kingdom centred on the city of Assur on the Tigris, in what is now northern Iraq. Over more than a thousand years it grew from a city-state into the largest empire the world had yet seen, ruling at its height in the seventh century BCE from Egypt to Iran.

## Assur and its god

Assyria took its name from the city of Assur, on the Tigris, and its god, also called Ashur. The king was Ashur's chief priest and representative, and Assyrian wars were presented as campaigns to extend the god's rule. The Assyrian heartland lay in the fertile plains of northern Mesopotamia, between the cities of Assur, Nineveh, Arbela and Nimrud.

## The Old Assyrian merchants

In the twentieth and nineteenth centuries BCE, merchants of Assur ran a long-distance trade network in Anatolia. Donkey caravans carried tin, from Iran or Central Asia, and textiles from Assur to Kanesh, near modern Kayseri, where they were exchanged for silver and gold. Over 20,000 clay tablets found at Kanesh, letters between merchants and their families, contracts and accounts, are among the richest archives of the ancient world. Shamshi-Adad I, an Amorite ruler, built a large kingdom in northern Mesopotamia around 1800 BCE.

## The Middle Assyrian kingdom

After a period under the kingdom of Mitanni, Assyria became a great power under Ashur-uballit I in the fourteenth century BCE, corresponding with the pharaohs of Egypt, as the Amarna letters show. Tukulti-Ninurta I, in the thirteenth century, sacked Babylon and carried off its god's statue. Tiglath-Pileser I campaigned to the Mediterranean around 1100 BCE. The crisis around 1200 BCE and Aramaean migrations then weakened Assyria.

## The Neo-Assyrian Empire

From the ninth century BCE, Assyrian kings campaigned almost every year. Ashurnasirpal II built a new capital at Nimrud. Tiglath-Pileser III, from 745 BCE, created a professional army and turned conquered kingdoms into provinces under Assyrian governors. His successors destroyed the kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE, besieged Jerusalem under Sennacherib in 701 BCE, and conquered Egypt in the 670s and 660s. The Assyrians were notorious for their brutality and for deporting whole populations to break resistance, and their palace reliefs proudly display sieges and executions.

## The Neo-Assyrian army

The Neo-Assyrian Empire, from 911 BCE, built the most effective army of its time: a standing army of infantry, cavalry and chariots, with iron weapons, siege engines with battering rams, and engineers who built ramps and tunnels. Conquered peoples were forced to pay tribute and supply troops. Rebellions were punished brutally, and the kings boasted in inscriptions of flaying, impaling and mutilating rebels.

## Deportations

Assyrian kings deported conquered populations on a massive scale, perhaps several million people over three centuries, moving them to other parts of the empire to break resistance and supply labour. The deportation of the people of the kingdom of Israel, after the fall of Samaria in 722 BCE, gave rise to the tradition of the "ten lost tribes". The deportations helped spread the Aramaic language, which became the common language of the Near East.

## Palaces and art

The Assyrian kings built magnificent palaces decorated with carved stone reliefs showing royal hunts, battles and sieges, such as Sennacherib's siege of Lachish in Judah in 701 BCE, and Ashurbanipal's lion hunt. Huge winged human-headed bulls and lions, lamassu, guarded the gates. Many reliefs are now in the British Museum, the Louvre and other museums. In 2015 the militant group Islamic State destroyed parts of Nimrud and other Assyrian sites.

## Kings of the empire

Sargon II, who ruled from 722 to 705 BCE, built a new capital, Dur-Sharrukin, at Khorsabad. Sennacherib made Nineveh a great city, with a palace he called "the palace without rival", aqueducts and gardens; some historians suggest that the Hanging Gardens were at Nineveh rather than Babylon. Esarhaddon conquered Egypt in 671 BCE. Ashurbanipal, the last great king, was literate, and his library at Nineveh, with tens of thousands of tablets, was rediscovered in the 1850s.

## The fall

After Ashurbanipal, civil war and revolts weakened Assyria. Babylon, under Nabopolassar, and the Medes, under Cyaxares, attacked. Assur fell in 614 BCE and Nineveh in 612 BCE. The last king, Ashur-uballit II, held out at Harran with Egyptian help until around 609 BCE. Assyrian Christians today, who speak dialects of Aramaic, see themselves as descendants of the ancient Assyrians.
