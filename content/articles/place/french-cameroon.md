French Cameroon, or Cameroun, was the French-ruled eastern portion of Germany's former colony of Kamerun, administered by France from 1916 first under a League of Nations mandate and later as a United Nations trust territory. Governed from Yaoundé, it became independent on 1 January 1960 as the Republic of Cameroon, after a bitter conflict with the nationalist Union of the Peoples of Cameroon. Our maps call it the French Cameroons.

## From Kamerun to mandate

Germany had claimed the coast around Douala in 1884 through treaties with local chiefs, including Ndumbe Lobe Bell, and pushed steadily inland; in 1911 it gained further land from France after the Agadir Crisis. During [World War I](/event/world-war-i) British and French forces occupied [Kamerun](/place/kamerun), and on 20 July 1916 they divided it. In 1922 the League of Nations confirmed the arrangement, with France taking the east and Britain the western lands it administered as Northern and Southern Cameroons.

## French rule between the wars

Rather than merging the territory into [French Equatorial Africa](/place/french-equatorial-africa), France ran it as a separate commissariat. It set out to erase the German legacy, imposing French law and teaching French across the territory, while tolerating customary law but keeping the courts and police in its own hands. The colonial health service fought sleeping sickness, with Eugène Jamot leading research. Leading Africans were drawn into the French system: Charles Atangana, made paramount chief by the Germans, visited the Paris Colonial Exhibition of 1931. Racism in colonial society also bred resentment, and anti-French feeling grew.

## The Second World War

In 1940, early in [World War II](/event/world-war-ii), the territory rallied to Free France. Troops under Philippe Leclerc took Douala on 27 August and moved on to Yaoundé, where the governor loyal to Vichy surrendered.

## Trust territory and reform

After the war Cameroun became a UN trust territory within the French Union. It had about three million inhabitants. Officials pushed each region towards a single export crop, cotton in the north, cocoa in the south and coffee in the west, and new roads opened the forests to logging. A representative assembly was created in 1946 and became the Territorial Assembly in 1952; Cameroonians such as Alexandre Douala Manga Bell sat in the French National Assembly.

## The Union of the Peoples of Cameroon

Founded in 1948, the Union of the Peoples of Cameroon (UPC) under Ruben Um Nyobé demanded independence and the reunification of the French and British territories. The governor banned it on 13 July 1955, and Um Nyobé led a guerrilla war from hiding. France granted internal self-government in 1956, with a Cameroonian prime minister from 1957 and Ahmadou Ahidjo in that post from 1958, but the new government refused to legalise the UPC. Um Nyobé was killed by French troops on 13 September 1958, and his successor Félix-Roland Moumié was murdered in Geneva in 1960, a killing attributed to the French secret service. Estimates of the dead in the war run to several tens of thousands, most of them after independence, with abuses recorded on all sides.

## Independence

French Cameroon gained independence on 1 January 1960 as the Republic of Cameroon. After a plebiscite in February 1961 Southern Cameroons, the British-run south, joined it on 1 October 1961 to form a federal republic, while the northern part chose [Nigeria](/country/nigeria). The UPC insurgency was not finally crushed until the early 1970s. The territory's borders form most of today's [Cameroon](/country/cameroon).
