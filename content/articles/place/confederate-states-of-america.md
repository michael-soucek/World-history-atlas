The Confederate States of America was a breakaway state formed in 1861 by southern slaveholding states that left the United States. It existed for four years, fought the Civil War against the Union, and collapsed in defeat in 1865. No foreign government ever formally recognised it.

## The cotton South

By 1860, the South produced most of the world's cotton, grown on plantations by about four million enslaved people. Cotton was the United States' largest export, and southern planters were among the richest Americans. Southern leaders defended slavery as a "positive good" and demanded that it be allowed to spread into the western territories. The Compromise of 1850, the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 and the Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision of 1857, which said that Black Americans could not be citizens, deepened the crisis. John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry in 1859, an attempt to start a slave rebellion, alarmed the South.

## Secession

The election of Abraham Lincoln in November 1860, on a platform of stopping the spread of slavery into the western territories, triggered the crisis. South Carolina seceded in December 1860, and six more states of the Deep South followed within weeks. In February 1861 they formed the Confederacy, with Jefferson Davis as president, and its constitution explicitly protected slavery. After Confederate forces fired on Fort Sumter in Charleston harbour in April 1861 and Lincoln called for troops, four more states joined: Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina. Richmond, Virginia, became the capital.

## Government

The Confederate constitution closely followed the US Constitution, but explicitly protected slavery and gave the president a single six-year term. Jefferson Davis, a former US senator and secretary of war from Mississippi, was president, and Alexander Stephens of Georgia vice-president. In his "Cornerstone Speech" of 1861, Stephens declared that the new government rested on "the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man". The Confederate government struggled with states' rights: governors resisted central control over troops and supplies.

## The war effort

The Confederacy introduced conscription in 1862, the first in American history, which allowed wealthy men to hire substitutes and exempted owners of twenty or more slaves, causing resentment summed up as "a rich man's war and a poor man's fight". It printed paper money, leading to severe inflation, and food shortages caused bread riots in Richmond in 1863. The Union naval blockade cut off much trade; blockade runners brought in some supplies. Britain and France, though their textile industries needed cotton, never recognised the Confederacy, partly because of opposition to slavery after the Emancipation Proclamation.

## The war

The Confederacy had about nine million people, more than a third of them enslaved, against over twenty million in the Union states, and far less industry and railway mileage. It counted on European demand for its cotton to win foreign support, but Britain and France stayed neutral. Confederate armies under generals such as Robert E. Lee won major victories in Virginia, but Lee's invasions of the North were turned back at Antietam in 1862 and Gettysburg in 1863. The Union's capture of Vicksburg in July 1863 gave it control of the Mississippi and split the Confederacy in two. Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 declared enslaved people in Confederate-held areas free, and about 180,000 Black soldiers fought for the Union.

## Enslaved people and the war

As Union armies advanced, hundreds of thousands of enslaved people escaped to Union lines. About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army and navy. Near the war's end, in March 1865, the Confederacy authorised arming enslaved men, but the war ended before this had any effect.

## Defeat and memory

The fall of Richmond and Lee's surrender at Appomattox in April 1865 ended the Confederacy. Davis was captured in May and imprisoned for two years. After the war, the "Lost Cause" myth portrayed the Confederacy as a noble fight for states' rights rather than slavery. Confederate monuments built in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many during the Jim Crow era, have been the subject of debate, and many have been removed in recent years.
