The Odrysian kingdom was the first large state of the eastern Balkans, a union of Thracian tribes led by the Odrysians that rose in the fifth century BC across what is now [Bulgaria](/country/bulgaria), northern Greece, European Turkey and northern Dobruja. Founded by Teres I, it was for a time one of the strongest powers in Europe, an ally of Athens and a rival of Macedon. It split apart after 360 BC, was conquered by Philip II of Macedon in 340 BC, revived on a smaller scale under Seuthes III, and then fades from the record in the third century BC.

## Out of the Persian retreat

With no Thracian writings, the story rests on archaeology, coins and Greek authors. Around 513 BC the armies of the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire) crossed into Europe, and Persian garrisons held points on the Thracian coast. After Xerxes' failed invasion of Greece in 480–479 BC, Persian power in Europe collapsed, and Thracian peoples moved into the gap. The Odrysians, whose homeland lay along the Hebros (today's Maritsa) and its tributaries, were the most successful.

## Teres and Sitalces

The historian Thucydides names Teres I as the first powerful Odrysian king, who built a great realm over much of Thrace, though many tribes stayed independent. Teres married a daughter to the Scythian king Ariapeithes, securing his north-east. His son Sitalces ruled a realm reaching from the Strymon and Iskar rivers to the Black Sea and from the Aegean to the Danube; Thucydides judged it the richest kingdom between the Ionian Sea and the Black Sea. In 431 BC, at the start of the [Peloponnesian War](/event/peloponnesian-war), Sitalces allied with Athens. At the turn of the year 428 BC he led a huge army against Macedon, said to number 150,000, though the figure is probably inflated, but withdrew after about a month when the promised Athenian help never came.

## Kings, gifts and warriors

Odrysian kingship borrowed much from the Persian court. A king's authority rested on war, religion and the giving and receiving of gifts; under Seuthes I tribute reached 400 talents of gold and silver, shared with the leading nobles. There was no fixed capital: kings moved between fortified residences. The army relied on aristocratic horsemen and light infantry with crescent-shaped shields, the peltasts, who so impressed the Greeks that they hired them and copied them.

## Division and Macedonian conquest

Around 400 BC the kingdom showed signs of strain, with rival rulers, and the Athenian commander Xenophon briefly served one of them, Seuthes II. Cotys I, king from 383 BC, reunited the realm and took the Thracian Chersonese from Athens, but he was murdered in 360 or 359 BC. Three kings then divided the land, while the Getae of the north-east had by then gone their own way. [Philip II of Macedon](/person/philip-ii-of-macedon) picked them off one by one and, after a hard campaign from 342 to 340 BC, conquered the Odrysian interior.

## Seuthes III and Seuthopolis

Some time between the late 330s and mid-320s BC, an Odrysian named Seuthes rebelled against Macedonian rule. After Alexander's death he fought an indecisive battle against Lysimachus, the new governor of Thrace, and the two eventually settled for dividing the country. Seuthes founded Seuthopolis near modern Kazanlak, a small Greek-style town of perhaps 1,000 people that served as his capital. It was destroyed in the first half of the third century BC, perhaps by [Celtic](/place/celts) invaders in the 270s or, by a newer view, in the 250s.

## A fading memory

After Seuthopolis there is little firm evidence for an Odrysian state. A Thracian king Cotys who fought for Macedon against Rome in 171–168 BC was called an Odrysian, but this is doubted. By the first century BC the Sapaeans and Asti were the leading Thracian powers, and after about 31 BC Rome built up a Sapaean client kingdom that it turned into the province of Thracia in 45 or 46 AD.

## On our maps

Our 1 BC map still draws an Odrysian Kingdom bordering the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire), although an Odrysian state is not securely attested after the third century BC; by then much of inland Thrace belonged to Rome's Sapaean client kingdom.
