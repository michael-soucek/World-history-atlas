For more than three hundred years, from 1206 to 1526, a succession of Muslim dynasties ruled northern India from Delhi. Historians call their state the Delhi Sultanate. It brought Islam to political power across much of the subcontinent and laid the foundations of the Indo-Islamic culture later developed under the Mughals.

## Five dynasties

The sultanate grew out of the conquests of Muhammad of Ghor, a ruler from Afghanistan who defeated the Rajput king Prithviraj Chauhan at Tarain in 1192. After his death, his Turkic slave general Qutb ud-Din Aibak became an independent ruler in India. Aibak's successors are known as the Mamluk or Slave dynasty; they were followed by the Khaljis, the Tughlaqs, the Sayyids and the Lodis. The Qutb Minar, the tall minaret begun by Aibak in Delhi, is the best-known monument of the early sultanate.

## The Mamluk dynasty

The first sultans were Turkic slave soldiers, mamluks, of Muhammad of Ghor. Qutb ud-Din Aibak ruled from 1206 to 1210 and began the Qutb Minar. Iltutmish, his son-in-law and a former slave, ruled from 1211 to 1236, consolidated the sultanate, received recognition from the Abbasid caliph and kept the Mongols at bay. His daughter Razia Sultana ruled from 1236 to 1240, one of the few women to rule in the medieval Islamic world; she appeared in public dressed as a man, but was overthrown by the Turkish nobles. Balban (1266–1287) strengthened royal power and court ceremony.

## Alauddin Khalji

Alauddin Khalji, who ruled from 1296 to 1316, repelled several Mongol invasions, including a siege of Delhi in 1303. He controlled prices in Delhi's markets to feed a large army cheaply, collected land taxes directly from peasants and suppressed the nobility with spies. His general Malik Kafur led raids deep into southern India, reaching Madurai and bringing back enormous treasure. Alauddin also conquered Rajput fortresses such as Ranthambore and Chittor; the siege of Chittor is the subject of later legend.

## Muhammad bin Tughlaq

Muhammad bin Tughlaq, sultan from 1325 to 1351, was highly educated and ambitious. He moved the capital to Daulatabad in the Deccan in 1327, forcing many of Delhi's people to move, then moved it back. He introduced a token copper currency, which was widely forged and had to be withdrawn. His plans for campaigns into Central Asia and the Himalayas failed. Ibn Battuta, who served as a judge at his court, described him as both generous and cruel. Revolts broke out, and the Bahmani Sultanate and Vijayanagara Empire emerged in the south.

## Firuz Shah Tughlaq

Firuz Shah, from 1351 to 1388, built canals, towns, gardens and hospitals, and moved two Ashokan pillars to Delhi. He favoured Muslim orthodoxy and imposed the jizya tax on Brahmins. After his death, the sultanate weakened, and Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398 devastated the city.

## Society and culture

The sultanate brought Persian as the language of government and culture, and Sufi orders, such as the Chishti, spread widely; the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi remains a major pilgrimage site. The poet and musician Amir Khusrau, a disciple of Nizamuddin, wrote in Persian and Hindavi and is credited with contributions to Hindustani music. Indo-Islamic architecture developed, combining Islamic arches and domes with Indian craftsmanship. The sultanate also faced the challenge of ruling a mostly Hindu population, and its record ranges from destruction of temples to accommodation of Hindu nobles and officials.

## The Sayyids and Lodis

The Sayyid dynasty (1414–1451) ruled a reduced territory. The Lodi dynasty of Afghan origin, from 1451, revived the sultanate; Sikandar Lodi founded Agra in 1504. But the last sultan, Ibrahim Lodi, alienated his nobles, and the governor of Punjab invited Babur to invade. At Panipat in 1526, Babur's cannon and cavalry defeated Ibrahim's much larger army, which included war elephants, and Ibrahim was killed.
