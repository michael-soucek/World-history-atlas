Silla was one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea and the one that eventually unified most of the peninsula. Traditionally founded in 57 BCE in the south-east, around its capital at Gyeongju, it lasted almost a thousand years, until 935 CE, one of the longest-lived dynasties in world history.

## Origins

According to the Samguk Sagi, a twelfth-century history, Silla was founded in 57 BCE by Bak Hyeokgeose, who was born from an egg. Early Silla was a federation of six villages around Gyeongju, in the south-east of the Korean peninsula. Three royal clans, Bak, Seok and Kim, shared the throne at first, and later the Kim clan held it. Silla was at first weaker and less developed than its neighbours Goguryeo and Baekje.

## Bone ranks

Silla society was organised by the golpum, "bone rank" system. The "sacred bone" class, the royal family, could become kings; the "true bone" class included other royals and high aristocrats; and lower ranks, the head ranks, were restricted in the offices they could hold. The system limited social mobility and caused discontent among able officials of lower rank. Three queens, Seondeok, Jindeok and Jinseong, ruled Silla, because there were no male "sacred bone" heirs.

## Buddhism and the state

Silla adopted Buddhism officially in 527, after the martyrdom of the courtier Ichadon, whose blood, according to legend, flowed white. Buddhism became closely linked to the state, and the great temple Hwangnyongsa, with a nine-storey wooden pagoda, was built in Gyeongju. Queen Seondeok built the Cheomseongdae observatory, one of the oldest surviving observatories in East Asia.

## The hwarang

The hwarang, "flower youths", were groups of young aristocratic men trained in martial arts, poetry, music and moral values combining Confucianism, Buddhism and Daoism. They followed five commandments, including loyalty to the king, filial piety and never retreating in battle. Many famous generals, such as Kim Yu-sin, came from the hwarang.

## Unification

Silla expanded in the sixth century under King Jinheung, taking the Han river valley. Allied with the Tang dynasty, Silla conquered Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668. The Tang then tried to control the whole peninsula, setting up protectorates, but Silla fought the Silla-Tang War from 670 to 676 and drove the Tang out of the area south of the Taedong river. Kim Yu-sin and King Munmu led these campaigns; Munmu asked to be buried at sea, to become a dragon protecting Silla.

## Unified Silla

After unification, Silla built a centralised state with provinces and secondary capitals. Gyeongju had a population that later records put at nearly a million, though modern estimates are lower. Bulguksa temple and the Seokguram grotto, built in the mid-eighth century, are masterpieces of Buddhist art. The Bell of King Seongdeok, cast in 771, is one of the largest bells in Korea. Silla scholars studied in Tang China, and the merchant Jang Bogo dominated trade between Korea, China and Japan in the ninth century.

## Gold crowns

Silla royal tombs, large earth mounds in Gyeongju, contained gold crowns with tree and antler shapes, decorated with jade and gold spangles, as well as glass vessels from the Roman or Persian world, showing long-distance trade.

## Decline

From the late eighth century, aristocrats fought over the throne, and local lords and bandits gained power. Gyeon Hwon founded Later Baekje in 892, and Gung Ye founded Later Goguryeo. Wang Geon overthrew Gung Ye and founded Goryeo in 918. In 935 King Gyeongsun surrendered Silla to Goryeo.

## On our maps

Our maps show Silla on every map from 1 BCE to 900 CE. Its largest outline, about 150,000 square kilometres on the 900 map, extends into today's North Korea, with Pyongyang's site inside it, further north than the border of Unified Silla, which ran along the Taedong river.
