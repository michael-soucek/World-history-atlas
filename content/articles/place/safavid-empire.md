Safavid Iran, ruled by the Safavid dynasty from 1501 to 1722, and formally until 1736, is often seen as the beginning of modern Iran. It reunited the Iranian plateau under a single native dynasty for the first time in centuries, gave the country borders roughly like its present ones, and made Shia Islam its religion.

## A Shia state between Sunni powers

Shah Ismail I founded the state in 1501 with the backing of Turkmen tribal warriors, the Qizilbash. Its Shia identity set Iran apart from its Sunni neighbours, above all the Ottomans to the west and the Uzbeks to the north-east, and the Safavids fought both for most of their history. The Ottomans defeated Ismail at Chaldiran in 1514 and repeatedly took Tabriz and Baghdad, which pushed the Safavids to move their capital east, first to Qazvin and then to Isfahan.

## Government

The Safavid state combined Turkmen tribal military power, Persian bureaucratic traditions and Shia religious authority. In the early period, Qizilbash chiefs held provinces and commanded the army, while Persian officials, called Tajiks, ran the administration. Abbas I created crown provinces administered directly, and a slave army loyal to himself. Religious scholars, led by the mulla bashi, gained increasing influence in the later seventeenth century.

## Wars with the Ottomans

The Safavid-Ottoman rivalry lasted for over two centuries. At Chaldiran in 1514, Ottoman artillery and janissaries crushed Ismail's cavalry, and the Ottomans briefly took Tabriz. The two empires fought over eastern Anatolia, the Caucasus and Iraq, with Baghdad changing hands several times. The Treaty of Zuhab in 1639 fixed a border between them that roughly corresponds to today's border between Iran and Turkey and Iraq.

## Trade and Europe

Silk was Iran's main export, and Abbas I made it a royal monopoly. To move trade away from Ottoman routes, he encouraged trade through the Persian Gulf and with European companies. In 1622, with the help of the English East India Company, he expelled the Portuguese from Hormuz and built the port of Bandar Abbas. Europeans, such as the brothers Anthony and Robert Shirley, served at his court, and embassies went to Europe seeking alliances against the Ottomans.

## The Armenians of New Julfa

In 1604–1605, during war with the Ottomans, Abbas forcibly moved the Armenian population of Julfa, on the Aras river, to Isfahan, where they founded the suburb of New Julfa. Armenian merchants there built a trading network stretching from Amsterdam and Venice to India, Tibet and Manila, dealing in silk and other goods. The Vank Cathedral in New Julfa, with its frescoes, survives.

## Art

Safavid art is among the finest in Persian history. Painters such as Sultan Muhammad and Reza Abbasi created manuscripts and single-page paintings. Safavid carpets, such as the Ardabil carpets of 1539–1540, now in London and Los Angeles, are masterpieces. Tilework, metalwork and textiles flourished. In Isfahan, the architecture of the Shah Mosque, with its blue tiles, and the bridges over the Zayandeh river, such as the Si-o-se-pol, remain famous.

## Society and religion

The Safavid period saw the rise of Shia scholars, such as Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, who compiled a vast collection of Shia traditions and gained great influence at the end of the seventeenth century. Philosophers such as Mulla Sadra, of the School of Isfahan, developed influential ideas. Religious minorities, such as Zoroastrians, Sunnis and Jews, faced periods of persecution in the later period.

## The fall

Weak rulers and a declining economy left the state vulnerable. In 1722 an Afghan army from Kandahar besieged and took Isfahan. The general Nader Shah drove the Afghans out and ruled in the Safavids' name before taking the throne himself in 1736. For the dynasty itself, see the [Safavid dynasty](/place/safavid-dynasty) page.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Safavid Empire from 1530 to 1715. It is largest on the 1715 map, at about 2.5 million square kilometres, about 60 percent of it in today's Iran and most of the rest in Afghanistan, Pakistan and Turkmenistan. Tehran's site and Isfahan are Safavid throughout; Tbilisi, Yerevan and Baku appear in it on the 1715 map. Before it, the region is held by the Timurids and Turkmen; on the 1783 map it is shown as Persia.
