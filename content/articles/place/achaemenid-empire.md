The Achaemenid Empire was the first state to rule most of the ancient Near East from a single centre. Cyrus the Great, a Persian king from the south-west of the Iranian plateau, overthrew the Medes around 550 BCE, then took Lydia in Anatolia and the Neo-Babylonian Empire in 539 BCE. Within thirty years his family ruled from the Aegean to the Indus.

## Cambyses and the conquest of Egypt

Cyrus's son Cambyses II added Egypt to the empire in 525 BCE, defeating the pharaoh Psamtik III at Pelusium. Greek writers, especially Herodotus, painted Cambyses as a mad tyrant who insulted Egyptian gods, but Egyptian records show him adopting pharaonic titles and supporting temples. He died in 522 BCE on his way home to face a revolt, and the crisis that followed brought Darius to the throne.

## Ruling a world empire

Darius I, who took the throne in 522 BCE, gave the empire its lasting shape. He divided it into provinces called satrapies, each with a governor, fixed tribute, and royal inspectors who reported back to the king. A road network with relay stations, the most famous stretch being the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, let messages cross the empire in days. Darius built a ceremonial capital at Persepolis, while the court moved between Susa, Babylon, Ecbatana and Pasargadae with the seasons.

The Persians were unusually willing to rule through local customs. Cyrus let the Jewish exiles in Babylon return to Jerusalem and rebuild their temple, and Persian kings were crowned as pharaohs in Egypt. Aramaic served as a common administrative language from Egypt to Afghanistan.

## The King of Kings

The Achaemenid ruler called himself "great king, king of kings, king in Persia, king of countries". His power was presented as a gift of Ahura Mazda, the great god of the Iranians, and royal inscriptions stress the king's duty to uphold truth and order against "the Lie". At court, a strict ceremony surrounded him; visitors bowed low in his presence, a gesture Greeks found shocking. The king was protected by an elite guard of 10,000 infantry that Herodotus called the Immortals, because every fallen man was replaced at once.

## Peoples of the empire

The empire held perhaps a third or more of the world's population at the time, including Persians, Medes, Elamites, Babylonians, Egyptians, Jews, Phoenicians, Greeks, Lydians, Bactrians, Scythians and Indians. Reliefs on the stairways of the Apadana at Persepolis show delegations from about twenty-three lands bringing gifts: Lydians with golden bowls, Indians with gold dust, Ethiopians with an okapi and elephant tusks. Rather than imposing one culture, the kings let local temples, laws and elites carry on, as long as tribute was paid and revolts were avoided.

## Administration and economy

Clay tablets from Persepolis, written in Elamite, record rations paid to thousands of workers, men and women, including skilled craftsmen from across the empire, and the movement of travellers with sealed authorisations along the royal roads. Darius standardised weights and measures, issued gold darics and silver sigloi, and encouraged irrigation, including underground channels, qanats, in the Iranian plateau. He also ordered the completion of a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea. Satraps collected tribute, raised troops and ruled like kings in their provinces, which sometimes led to rebellions.

## Religion

The Achaemenid kings worshipped Ahura Mazda, though how far they followed the teachings of Zarathustra is debated. They also honoured other gods, Iranian and foreign: Cyrus presented himself in Babylon as chosen by Marduk. The Bible remembers the Persians kindly, crediting Cyrus with returning the Jews to Jerusalem and Darius with supporting the rebuilding of the Temple, completed around 516 BCE. The Book of Esther is set at the Persian court in Susa.

## Art and architecture

Achaemenid art blended traditions from the whole empire: Assyrian-style reliefs, Egyptian columns and doorways, Greek and Lydian stonework. At Pasargadae, Cyrus laid out palaces in formal gardens, the earliest known example of the Persian garden later called paradeisos, from which the word paradise comes. Persepolis, begun by Darius and expanded by Xerxes, had a vast audience hall, the Apadana, with 72 columns about 20 metres tall. Royal tombs were cut into a cliff face at Naqsh-e Rustam.

## Xerxes and his successors

Xerxes I (486–465 BCE) crushed revolts in Egypt and Babylon and led the great invasion of Greece in 480 BCE. After the defeats at Salamis and Plataea, Persia gave up conquering Greece but continued to influence Greek politics with gold, funding Sparta against Athens in the Peloponnesian War. The fourth century saw repeated revolts by satraps and a long struggle to recover Egypt, which broke away in 404 BCE and was retaken only in 343 BCE. Artaxerxes II and III held the empire together, but court intrigue, including the poisoning of kings by the eunuch Bagoas, weakened the dynasty shortly before Alexander's invasion.

## The end

Alexander of Macedon invaded in 334 BCE, won three great battles in four years, and burned Persepolis. Darius III was murdered by his own officials in 330 BCE, the date usually given for the empire's end. On our 323 BCE map the same space belongs to Alexander.

## Legacy

The Achaemenid model of a multi-ethnic empire ruled through provinces, roads and tolerance of local customs influenced Alexander, the Seleucids, the Parthians, the Sasanians and, through them, later Islamic states. Old Persian, written in a cuneiform script created for royal inscriptions, is the ancestor of modern Persian.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on the 500 and 400 BCE maps, and it is at its largest on the 400 BCE map at about 6 million square kilometres. Only about a quarter of that is in today's Iran; the rest spreads from Turkey and Egypt to Pakistan, Afghanistan and Uzbekistan. Babylon, Damascus, Jerusalem, Samarkand and Kabul all fall inside it on our maps, as does Egypt.
