The Mamluk Sultanate was ruled by former slave soldiers. Mamluks were boys bought on the steppes and in the Caucasus, converted to Islam and trained as an elite cavalry. In 1250 the mamluk regiments of Egypt's last strong Ayyubid sultan seized power for themselves, and for the next 267 years Egypt and Syria were governed from Cairo by sultans who had begun life in the barracks.

## Who the mamluks were

The word mamluk means "owned". Mamluks were boys bought as slaves, mostly from the Kipchak Turks of the Black Sea steppes and later from the Circassians of the Caucasus, brought to Egypt, converted to Islam and raised in military barracks. There they learned horsemanship, archery and lance work, and on completing their training they were freed and enrolled in the household of the master who had bought them. Loyalty to one's master and to comrades from the same barracks was the glue of the system.

Unusually, the status was not hereditary. The sons of mamluks were born free Muslims and, though many held posts and estates, they were mostly excluded from the elite cavalry. Each generation of rulers had to be renewed by fresh purchases from outside, and the slave trade from the Black Sea, carried on Genoese ships, supplied the regime for most of its history.

## The founding years

The mamluk regiments of the Ayyubid sultan al-Salih Ayyub defeated the crusade of Louis IX of France at Mansura in 1250. Weeks later they murdered al-Salih's heir, Turanshah, and the new state began under Shajar al-Durr, al-Salih's widow, who briefly ruled as sultan in her own name, one of the very few women to do so in Islamic history, before marrying the mamluk commander Aybak. A decade of instability followed, ended by the victory over the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260 and the rise of Baybars, who murdered the sultan Qutuz on the way home and took the throne.

## Baybars and Qalawun

Baybars, sultan from 1260 to 1277, built the institutions of the state. He set up a postal relay system that could carry messages from Cairo to Damascus in four days, organised the army, rebuilt fortresses and sent annual campaigns against the Crusader castles, taking Antioch in 1268 and the great fortress of Krak des Chevaliers in 1271. Qalawun, sultan from 1279 to 1290, defeated the Mongols again at Homs and founded a dynasty that ruled, with interruptions, for about a century. His son al-Ashraf Khalil took Acre in 1291.

## Two eras

Historians divide Mamluk rule into two periods named after the soldiers who dominated each. Under the Bahri sultans, mostly Turkic Kipchaks, the state reached its peak, above all during the long third reign of al-Nasir Muhammad (1310–1341). From 1382 the Burji sultans, mostly Circassians, held power through a more unstable cycle of short reigns and factional struggle. Plague, the loss of trade to Portuguese ships sailing round Africa, and the cost of constant rivalry all weakened the state.

## Government and the iqta system

The sultan ruled from the Citadel of Cairo, surrounded by his own mamluks. Senior emirs commanded regiments and received iqtas, grants of the tax revenue from particular villages, rather than land in permanent ownership. Civilian administrators, many of them from Coptic Christian or Muslim scholarly families, ran the treasury and chancery. The Abbasid caliph in Cairo gave each new sultan formal legitimacy, while the religious scholars, the ulama, staffed the courts and schools. In 1315 al-Nasir Muhammad carried out a great land survey that redistributed the iqtas in his own favour.

## Cairo in its prime

Under the Mamluks, Cairo was the largest city in the Middle East and the intellectual capital of the Arabic-speaking world. Sultans and emirs built mosques, madrasas, hospitals and mausoleums along its main streets, many of which still stand. The complex of Sultan Hasan, finished in the 1360s, and the Qalawun complex with its hospital are among the masterpieces of Islamic architecture. Scholars such as the historian al-Maqrizi, the encyclopaedist al-Nuwayri and Ibn Khaldun, who served as a judge in Cairo, worked here.

## Trade and economy

Egypt's wealth rested on the Nile's harvests and on the spice trade. Pepper, ginger and other goods from India came up the Red Sea to Egyptian ports and were sold in Alexandria to Venetian and Genoese merchants. The Karimi merchants, a network of wealthy traders, dominated this commerce until the sultans tried to monopolise it in the fifteenth century. Sugar, textiles and other crafts were also important.

## Plague and crisis

The Black Death reached Egypt in 1347 and killed perhaps a third of the population, with repeated outbreaks afterwards. Villages emptied, irrigation works decayed and tax revenues fell. Under the Circassian sultans, factional fighting among mamluk households became chronic, and Timur's sack of Damascus in 1401 was a further blow. The sultans raised taxes and debased the coinage to pay their troops.

## The Portuguese and the Ottomans

After Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, the Portuguese tried to cut the Red Sea route. A Mamluk fleet was destroyed at Diu in 1509, and spice revenues suffered. Meanwhile the Mamluks clashed with the rising Ottomans over the borderlands of southern Anatolia. The Mamluk army, proud of its cavalry traditions, had been slow to adopt firearms, and at Marj Dabiq in 1516 Ottoman artillery and musketeers proved decisive; the sultan al-Ghawri died on the battlefield. The last sultan, Tuman Bay, was hanged at one of Cairo's gates in 1517.

## On our maps

Our maps show the sultanate from 1279 to 1500. It is largest on the 1492 map, at about 1.47 million square kilometres: Egypt, a long strip of the Libyan coast, the Levant and the Hejaz. That last region gave the sultans control of Mecca and Medina, and with them the pilgrimage routes. Jerusalem, Damascus, Cairo, Alexandria, Beirut and Mecca are all Mamluk on our maps of this period. On the 1200 map the region belongs to the Ayyubids; by 1530 it is Ottoman.
