  - Q: Why was it called the Holy Roman Empire? A: Emperors claimed to inherit the power of ancient Rome's rulers through Charlemagne. "Holy", meaning consecrated, was attached under Frederick Barbarossa from 1157, and the full form "Holy Roman Empire" appears from 1254. From about 1500 "of the German Nation" was often added.
  - Q: How was the Holy Roman Emperor chosen? A: The emperor was elected. A group of leading princes gradually became a college of prince-electors, and the Golden Bull of 1356 fixed seven of them and allowed a majority vote. Elections were usually held in Frankfurt, and from 1438 the Habsburgs almost always won.
  - Q: Was Charlemagne the first Holy Roman Emperor? A: Historians disagree. Some date the empire from Charlemagne's coronation as emperor on 25 December 800; others start it with Otto I's coronation in 962, which made German kings heirs to Charlemagne's imperial title. Contemporaries at first simply called the realm the Roman Empire.
  - Q: Why did the Holy Roman Empire end? A: After Napoleon defeated Austria at Austerlitz in 1805, he organised much of western Germany into the Confederation of the Rhine under French protection. Francis II, already Emperor of Austria since 1804, then laid down the imperial crown on 6 August 1806, ending the empire.
  - Q: What religion was the Holy Roman Empire? A: It was a Latin Christian realm closely tied to the papacy. After Luther's protest in 1517 it split along religious lines: the Peace of Augsburg (1555) let rulers choose Catholicism or Lutheranism, and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) recognised Calvinism as well.

The Holy Roman Empire lasted for over eight hundred years, from 962 to 1806, and for most of that time it was not much like an empire at all. It was a patchwork of kingdoms, duchies, church lands and free cities in central Europe, held together by an elected emperor whose real power varied from considerable to almost none.

## Otto the Great

Otto I, duke of Saxony and king of Germany from 936, won his authority through victories over rebellious dukes and the Magyars, whom he crushed at the Lechfeld in 955. He used bishops and abbots as royal agents, granting them lands and powers, because their offices could not be inherited. He intervened in Italy, married the widowed Queen Adelaide of Italy, and in 962 was crowned emperor by Pope John XII in Rome.

## Emperors and popes

For centuries, emperors claimed authority over the papacy, and popes claimed the right to crown and judge emperors. In the Investiture Controversy, Pope Gregory VII and Emperor Henry IV fought over who had the right to appoint bishops. Excommunicated in 1076, Henry crossed the Alps in winter and stood barefoot in the snow at Canossa in 1077 to seek the pope's forgiveness. The Concordat of Worms of 1122 reached a compromise. The Hohenstaufen emperors, Frederick I Barbarossa and his grandson Frederick II, fought popes and Italian cities; Barbarossa drowned on the Third Crusade in 1190.

## A federation

After the fall of the Hohenstaufen in the mid-thirteenth century, the empire became a loose federation. Its members included great princes such as the electors and dukes, counts and knights, archbishops and bishops, abbeys, and free imperial cities such as Frankfurt, Nuremberg and Augsburg. The Golden Bull of 1356, issued by Charles IV, fixed seven prince-electors: the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and [Trier](/history-of/trier), the king of Bohemia, the count palatine of the Rhine, the duke of Saxony and the margrave of Brandenburg. Charles IV made Prague a great capital, founding its university in 1348.

## The Habsburgs

From 1438 to 1806 the emperors were, with one short interruption, members of the House of Habsburg. Maximilian I reformed the empire's institutions around 1495, creating an imperial court of justice and dividing the empire into regional circles. His grandson Charles V ruled Spain and its American empire as well as the Habsburg lands, and faced the Reformation.

## Reformation and war

Martin Luther's protest in 1517 began in the empire, and the Reformation spread across its princes and cities. The Peace of Augsburg of 1555 allowed each ruler to choose between Lutheranism and Catholicism for his lands. Religious tension exploded in the Thirty Years' War from 1618, which devastated Germany and drew in Denmark, Sweden and France. The Peace of Westphalia of 1648 gave the princes nearly full sovereignty and recognised Calvinism.

## Late flowering

After 1648 the empire continued as a framework for hundreds of states, from Austria and Prussia to tiny counties and free cities. The Imperial Diet sat permanently in Regensburg from 1663. Imperial courts settled disputes between rulers and even between rulers and subjects. Historians once dismissed the late empire as weak and irrelevant, but many now see it as a system that protected smaller states and kept the peace among its members for long periods.

## The end

Voltaire famously joked that it was "neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire". After Napoleon's victories, the Confederation of the Rhine was formed in 1806 under French protection, and Francis II dissolved the empire on 6 August 1806.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the empire on every map from 1000 to 1715, largest on the 1300 map at about 920,000 square kilometres. Germany makes up 37 percent of that, followed by Italy, Austria, the Czech Republic and Poland. Prague, Vienna, Cologne and Munich lie inside it on our maps for most of this period, while Milan and Florence drop out after 1530, as imperial control of Italy faded. On later maps the basemap often shows member states such as Austria or Prussia separately.
