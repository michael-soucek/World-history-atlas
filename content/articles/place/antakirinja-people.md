The Antakirinja, spelled Antikirinya by the Australian Institute of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Studies and sometimes called Ngonde, are an Aboriginal people of the far north of South Australia. Their country lies on the eastern edge of the Western Desert, between the Everard Ranges and the stony gibber plains around Coober Pedy. Today many Antakirinja people live in towns such as Coober Pedy and Oodnadatta, and many who once identified as Antakirinja now identify as Yankunytjatjara.

## Name and language

The name is generally thought to come from the neighbouring Arandic languages, with a meaning along the lines of "people from the west" or "from the south"; the anthropologist Norman Tindale favoured "westerners", while later linguists derive it from Lower Arrernte words for a direction and "originating from". Their speech is a variety of the Western Desert language, part of the Pama–Nyungan family, a vast chain of closely related dialects whose speakers can understand one another from Western Australia to the Northern Territory. It is close to [Yankunytjatjara](/place/yankunytjatjara), which is spoken today in Mimili, Indulkana and Fregon and eastward to Oodnadatta and Coober Pedy.

## Country

Tindale estimated their lands at about 24,500 square miles. They took in the upper reaches of the Hamilton, Alberga, Wintinna and Lora rivers and ran north over today's border to Kulgera, inside the Northern Territory. To the south they reached Mount Willoughby, Arckaringa and the Stuart Range, near the [Kokatha](/place/kokatha) country at Coober Pedy, and their boundary with the Matuntara roughly followed the northern edge of the bluebush plains. Local groups included the Walarangunja of the eastern Everard Ranges and the Kadjilaranda further north. Nineteenth-century accounts of a four-section marriage system, collected by a telegraph stationmaster at Charlotte Waters, were judged by Tindale to describe the [Arrernte](/place/arrernte-people) instead.

## Recording their own history

In 2000 an Antikirinja Social History Project recorded oral history interviews, translated by Bobby Brown, which are now held by the State Library of South Australia. In 2012 the Antakirinja man Ingkama Bobby Brown and the linguist Petter Naessan published Irrititja, The Past, an Antakirinja history centred on Ingomar Station that covers his upbringing, the foods and animals of the country and the language.

## On our maps

Our maps from 1600 to 1815 draw an approximate Antakirinja territory, labelled Antakarinja, in northern South Australia. It sits among the lands of the Yankunytjatjara, the [Luritja](/place/luritja), the Arrernte, the Kokatha and the [Arabana](/place/arabana-people), one small part of the patchwork of [Aboriginal Australian](/culture/aboriginal-australians) nations drawn on these maps.
