Champa was a group of kingdoms of the Cham people along the coast of what is now central and southern Vietnam. For over a thousand years, from around the second century CE until the nineteenth, the Cham built a seafaring culture shaped by Indian religion and art, and fought with the Vietnamese to their north and the Khmer to their west.

## Origins

The Cham spoke an Austronesian language, related to Malay and the languages of Indonesia and the Philippines, and their ancestors probably arrived by sea. The Sa Huỳnh culture, from around 1000 BCE, is thought to be connected to them. Chinese records describe a kingdom called Linyi, founded around 192 CE after a local official revolted against Han rule in the region around today's Huế. Linyi later became known as Champa.

## Coastal principalities

Champa was not usually a single unified state but a federation of principalities, each centred on a river valley and port along the narrow coastal plain between the mountains and the sea: Amaravati, around Da Nang and Quang Nam; Vijaya, around Quy Nhơn; Kauthara, around Nha Trang; and Panduranga, around Phan Rang. Rulers often competed, and sometimes one king dominated the others.

## Indian religion and art

The Cham adopted Hinduism, especially the worship of Shiva in the form of the linga, and later Mahayana Buddhism. Inscriptions in Sanskrit and Cham record royal donations. The sanctuary of Mỹ Sơn, in a valley near Da Nang, was the main religious centre from the fourth to the thirteenth century, with over seventy brick temples; it is now a World Heritage Site, though many temples were damaged by American bombing in 1969. Cham brick temples, built without visible mortar, and sandstone sculptures of dancers, gods and garudas, at sites such as Đồng Dương and Po Nagar in Nha Trang, are masterpieces. The Museum of Cham Sculpture in Da Nang holds many of them.

## Trade and the sea

Champa's ports traded with China, India, the Islamic world and the islands of Southeast Asia, exporting aloeswood, a fragrant wood prized for incense, ivory, rhinoceros horn and slaves. Cham sailors had a reputation as pirates. Arab and Persian merchants visited, and Islam spread gradually among the Cham from the tenth century.

## Wars

Champa fought Đại Việt, the Vietnamese kingdom, many times. In 982 the Vietnamese sacked the capital Indrapura, and in 1069 they captured a Cham king, who gave up territory to be released. Champa also fought the Khmer Empire: in 1177 a Cham fleet sailed up the Mekong and Tonle Sap and sacked Angkor, and from 1203 to 1220 the Khmer king Jayavarman VII occupied Champa. In the 1280s Champa resisted a Mongol invasion. In the fourteenth century King Chế Bồng Nga attacked Đại Việt repeatedly, sacking its capital Thăng Long in 1371 and 1377, before he was killed in 1390.

## Decline

In 1471 the Vietnamese emperor Lê Thánh Tông captured Vijaya, killing tens of thousands and taking the king prisoner. Champa lost its northern lands and shrank to the south. Vietnamese settlers moved steadily south. The last Cham principality, Panduranga, became a Vietnamese protectorate in the seventeenth century and was annexed by Emperor Minh Mạng in 1832.

## The Cham today

Around 200,000 Cham live in Vietnam today, and several hundred thousand in Cambodia, where many fled. Most Cham in Cambodia and southern Vietnam are Muslims, while the Cham of central Vietnam include Bani, a local form of Islam, and Balamon, who keep Hindu-derived traditions and worship at the remaining temples, such as Po Klong Garai.
