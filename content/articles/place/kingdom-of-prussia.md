The Kingdom of Prussia rose from a scattered collection of territories into a great power and, in the nineteenth century, united Germany under its leadership. Its kings were the Hohenzollerns, who ruled the Electorate of Brandenburg, around Berlin, from 1415.

## Brandenburg-Prussia

The name came from the Duchy of Prussia, a land on the Baltic coast that had belonged to the Teutonic Knights. In 1525 its last grand master became a Protestant duke, and in 1618 the duchy passed by inheritance to the Hohenzollerns of Brandenburg. Because Prussia lay outside the Holy Roman Empire, the elector Frederick III could crown himself king there in 1701 as Frederick I. His successor, Frederick William I, built up an army far larger than the kingdom's size would suggest.

## The Great Elector

Frederick William, elector of Brandenburg from 1640 to 1688, known as the Great Elector, rebuilt his lands after the devastation of the Thirty Years' War. He created a standing army, centralised administration and taxes, and in 1657 gained full sovereignty over the Duchy of Prussia, previously held as a vassal of Poland. His Edict of Potsdam in 1685 welcomed about 20,000 Huguenot refugees from France, who brought skills and trades.

## The soldier king

Frederick William I, king from 1713 to 1740, was obsessed with his army, doubling it to about 80,000 men, a huge force for a state of about 2.5 million people. He collected especially tall soldiers, the "Potsdam Giants". He was frugal, ran a highly efficient administration, and treated his son, the future Frederick the Great, harshly; when the young Frederick tried to flee, his friend Hans Hermann von Katte was executed in front of him.

## Frederick the Great

Frederick II, who reigned from 1740 to 1786, was an accomplished flautist, composer and writer who corresponded with Voltaire and hosted him at his palace of Sanssouci in Potsdam. In 1740 he seized Silesia from Austria, starting the War of the Austrian Succession. In the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), Prussia fought Austria, France, Russia and Sweden, with British support, and survived near-defeat when Russia's new tsar, Peter III, an admirer of Frederick, made peace in 1762. Frederick promoted religious tolerance, abolished torture, and encouraged agriculture, including potato cultivation.

## Defeat and reform

Prussia was crushed by Napoleon at Jena and Auerstedt in 1806 and lost half its territory. Reformers, such as Baron vom Stein and Karl August von Hardenberg, abolished serfdom in 1807, reformed local government and allowed Jews more rights. Gerhard von Scharnhorst and August von Gneisenau reformed the army, introducing conscription. Wilhelm von Humboldt founded the University of Berlin in 1810. Prussian troops under Blücher helped defeat Napoleon at Leipzig and Waterloo.

## Prussia in the nineteenth century

The Congress of Vienna gave Prussia the Rhineland and Westphalia, which became centres of industry. Prussia led the Zollverein, a customs union of German states, from 1834. In 1848 revolutionaries in Berlin demanded a constitution; King Frederick William IV granted one, but refused the crown of a united Germany offered by the Frankfurt Parliament. Bismarck's wars of 1864, 1866 and 1870–1871 made Prussia the leader of a united Germany.

## Militarism and legacy

Prussia was known for its efficient bureaucracy, its army and its Junker aristocracy, landowners of the east who dominated the officer corps. Its legacy is debated: some admire its administration, education and culture, others see it as a source of German militarism. After 1945 its eastern lands, including Königsberg, Silesia and Pomerania, were given to Poland and the Soviet Union, and millions of Germans were expelled.

## The end

The Prussian monarchy ended with the German revolution of 1918. Prussia continued as a state within Germany until the Allies formally abolished it in 1947.

Our maps show Prussia from 1530 to 1815, including its earlier history as a duchy.
