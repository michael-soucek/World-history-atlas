The Emirate of Granada was the last Muslim state in western Europe. For about two and a half centuries, from 1238 to 1492, the Nasrid dynasty ruled a small mountainous kingdom in southern Spain, while the Christian kingdoms took the rest of the Iberian peninsula. Its greatest legacy is the Alhambra.

## Founding

After the Almohad Caliphate collapsed in the early thirteenth century, al-Andalus broke into small states, and the Christian kingdoms advanced. Muhammad ibn Yusuf ibn Nasr, known as Ibn al-Ahmar, a leader from Arjona, established himself as ruler of Jaén, Granada and Almería. In 1238 he entered Granada, which became his capital. In 1246 he became a vassal of Ferdinand III of Castile, giving up Jaén and paying tribute, and even sent troops to help Castile besiege Seville in 1248.

## The kingdom

The Emirate stretched from Gibraltar to Almería, including Málaga, the main port, and the mountains of the Sierra Nevada and the Alpujarras. Its population was swollen by Muslim refugees from the conquered lands. It was densely populated and productive, with irrigated farming in the vega, the plain around Granada, and in the mountains, growing fruit, sugar cane and mulberry trees for the silk industry. Granada's silk was exported through Genoese merchants, who had a strong presence in Málaga.

## Survival by diplomacy

Granada survived by balancing between Castile and the Marinid sultans of Morocco, who sent troops and sometimes held Gibraltar and other towns, and by taking advantage of Castile's frequent civil wars. It paid tribute to Castile in gold when necessary. There were periods of war: in 1340, Castile and Portugal defeated a combined Marinid and Nasrid army at the Río Salado, and Castile took Algeciras in 1344. Frontier warfare, raids and truces were constant, and the frontier ballads, romances fronterizos, are part of Spanish literature.

## The Alhambra

The Nasrid sultans built the Alhambra, the "red fortress", on the Sabika hill above Granada. Muhammad I began the fortress; Yusuf I, who ruled from 1333 to 1354, built the Comares Palace, with its Court of the Myrtles and the Hall of the Ambassadors; and Muhammad V, from 1354 to 1391, built the Palace of the Lions, with its fountain of twelve lions and rooms with stalactite-like muqarnas ceilings. Walls are covered in carved stucco, tiles and Arabic inscriptions, including poems by the vizier Ibn Zamrak. The motto of the Nasrids, "There is no victor but God", appears thousands of times. The Generalife, a summer palace with gardens, lies nearby.

## Learning

Granada was a centre of Islamic learning. The vizier and polymath Lisan al-Din ibn al-Khatib wrote histories, poetry and a treatise on the plague, arguing that it spread by contagion. The Madrasa of Granada was founded in 1349.

## Decline

In the fifteenth century, Granada was weakened by civil wars within the Nasrid family and between factions, such as the Abencerrajes. In 1469 Ferdinand of Aragon married Isabella of Castile. In 1481 Granada attacked the Castilian town of Zahara, and in 1482 Castile began a war of conquest. Sultan Abu al-Hasan Ali, his brother Muhammad al-Zaghal and his son Muhammad XII, known as Boabdil, fought each other. Boabdil was captured in 1483 and released as a Castilian ally. Town after town fell: Ronda in 1485, Málaga in 1487 after a long siege, whose inhabitants were enslaved, and Baza and Almería in 1489.

## The fall

In 1491 the Catholic Monarchs besieged Granada, building a camp-city, Santa Fe. Boabdil negotiated surrender, and on 2 January 1492 handed over the keys of the city. The Treaty of Granada promised Muslims the right to keep their religion, laws and property. According to legend, as Boabdil left, he wept at a mountain pass, now called "the Moor's last sigh", and his mother told him to weep like a woman for what he could not defend like a man. He went into exile in Morocco. The promises were soon broken: forced conversions began in 1499, leading to a revolt, and in 1502 Muslims in Castile were ordered to convert or leave. The Moriscos, converted Muslims, rebelled in the Alpujarras in 1568–1571 and were finally expelled from Spain between 1609 and 1614.
