In antiquity the western half of the Balkans, from the Adriatic coast inland to the Drava and Morava rivers, was home to peoples whom Greek and Roman writers called Illyrians. Together with the [Thracians](/place/thracians) and the Greeks they are counted among the three principal Paleo-Balkan peoples. They spoke Indo-European languages that are now lost, formed a string of warrior kingdoms, fought Macedon and Rome, and in the Roman era gave the empire soldiers and emperors.

## A label applied from outside

The name probably began with one tribe on the south-eastern Adriatic, in today's Albania and Montenegro, and was then stretched by Greek writers to cover their neighbours with similar speech and customs. The first surviving mention comes from Hecataeus of Miletus around 500 BCE. Modern scholars doubt that these tribes ever called themselves Illyrians as a group, and they were not one people in culture or language. Studies of personal names point to a southern Illyrian zone and a separate Dalmatian-Pannonian zone, overlapping in the land of the Dardani in modern Kosovo. Theories from the 1920s that placed Illyrians across central Europe have been abandoned.

## Bronze Age roots

Archaeologists trace the Illyrians to Bronze Age cultures of the western Balkans, such as the Cetina culture, formed when steppe-derived newcomers mixed with older Neolithic farmers. Ancient DNA from Croatia, Montenegro and Albania shows this mixture and a common paternal line, J2b-L283, attested in northern Albania as early as the nineteenth century BCE. By the seventh century BCE, at the start of the Iron Age, distinct Illyrian groups appear under influence from the [Hallstatt culture](/place/hallstatt-culture) to the north, burying their elite in earth and stone mounds.

## Kingdoms, pirates and Rome

Southern Illyria produced the earliest states. The kingdom of the Enchelei peaked in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE; the Taulantii controlled the coast around Epidamnus, a colony founded by Corcyra in 627 BCE. In 358 BCE [Philip II of Macedon](/person/philip-ii-of-macedon) defeated the Illyrian king Bardylis. In the third century BCE a coastal state under King Agron, and then his widow Teuta, grew rich on piracy in the Adriatic. Rome answered with the Illyrian Wars of 229, 219 and 168 BCE, the last ending with the defeat of King Gentius at Scodra. A great revolt in the early first century CE was crushed under [Augustus](/person/augustus), and the region was divided into the provinces of Dalmatia and Pannonia.

## Society and belief

Ancient sources describe kingdoms, elected chiefs and self-governing communities. The Illyrians farmed and raised livestock, built hill forts such as Daorson in Herzegovina, and sailed swift ships, the lembus and liburna, that Macedon and Rome later copied. Their religion was polytheistic, with strong solar symbols, the serpent as a sacred animal and many local gods. Greek and Roman portrayals of them as savages are uniformly hostile and are read today with care.

## Roman Illyrians and what came after

Under Rome, Illyrians joined the army in large numbers. From the third century CE soldiers from the Danube provinces rose to the throne, the so-called Illyrian emperors, a line later counted as including [Justinian I](/person/justinian-i). The name Illyrian last appears for a people in the seventh century. Many scholars think Albanian may descend from an Illyrian dialect, while others propose a Thracian origin; the evidence is too thin to settle it. The idea of Illyrian descent is central to Albanian national identity, and a 2026 genetic study reported strong continuity between ancient western Balkan populations and modern Albanians, though linking archaeological finds to ethnic labels remains contested. In the nineteenth century Croatian reformers also adopted the Illyrian name for their national movement.

## On our maps

Our maps for 1500, 1000, 700 and 500 BCE draw an approximate Illyrian area along the eastern Adriatic, an outline that is broad and uncertain. In the earlier years it sits beside Thrace and the Greek city-states, and by 500 BCE beside the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire) in the southern Balkans.
