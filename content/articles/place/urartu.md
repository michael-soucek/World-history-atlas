Urartu was an Iron Age kingdom in the highlands around Lake Van, in what is now eastern Turkey, Armenia and north-western Iran. From the ninth to the seventh century BCE it was one of the major powers of the Near East and the chief rival of Assyria in the north. Its name survives, through Hebrew, in the biblical "mountains of Ararat".

## The kingdom of Van

The Urartians called their land Biainili, from which the name Van comes. Urartu is the Assyrian name, and the biblical "Ararat" derives from it. The kingdom arose in the ninth century BCE, partly in response to Assyrian raids, uniting highland chiefdoms. Its first known king, Arame, fought the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III. Sarduri I built the citadel of Tushpa on the Rock of Van, beside Lake Van, with its first inscriptions, in Assyrian.

## Expansion

Under Ishpuini, Menua and Argishti I, in the late ninth and eighth centuries BCE, Urartu expanded north into the Ararat plain and around Lake Sevan, east towards Lake Urmia and west towards the Euphrates. Argishti I founded Erebuni in 782 BCE and Argishtihinili in the Ararat valley. Sarduri II, around 750 BCE, made Urartu the strongest power in the region and controlled parts of northern Syria, until he was defeated by Tiglath-Pileser III of Assyria in 743 BCE.

## Engineering

Urartian kings were great builders. Menua's canal, about 70 kilometres long, brought water to Tushpa and is still in use, known locally as the Shamiram canal, after a legendary queen. The Urartians built reservoirs, dams and terraced fields, and planted vineyards and orchards. Their fortresses, such as Teishebaini, now Karmir Blur near Yerevan, and Ayanis, had massive stone walls and large storerooms for grain, wine and oil, with huge jars capable of storing hundreds of thousands of litres.

## Religion

The chief god of Urartu was Haldi, a warrior god, whose main temple was at Musasir, a holy city on the border with Assyria. Other important gods were Teisheba, the storm god, and Shivini, the sun god. Urartian temples were square towers, and inscriptions list offerings of animals to the gods. When Sargon II sacked Musasir in 714 BCE, he recorded the vast treasures he took, including thousands of bronze objects and a statue of Haldi.

## Metalwork and art

Urartian bronze work was highly developed: decorated shields, helmets, quivers, belts, cauldrons with bull's head attachments and furniture fittings. Urartian art was influenced by Assyria but had its own style. Many objects were found at Toprakkale near Van, and at Karmir Blur.

## Wars with Assyria

Assyria and Urartu fought for control of the trade routes and smaller states, such as Mannaea and Shubria. Sargon II's campaign of 714 BCE, described in a long letter to the god Ashur, defeated King Rusa I, who, according to Assyrian accounts, took his own life. Rusa II, in the early seventh century BCE, rebuilt Urartian power and founded new fortresses.

## The end

Urartu disappeared in the early sixth century BCE. The Medes, Scythians or Cimmerians may have destroyed it; the final destruction of Teishebaini, with arrowheads of Scythian type, suggests a violent end. Armenians, speaking an Indo-European language, became dominant in the region and are considered by many scholars to have absorbed much of the Urartian population.
