The Milograd culture is an Early Iron Age archaeological culture of the forests and marshes of southern [Belarus](/country/belarus) and northern [Ukraine](/country/ukraine), centred on the Pripyat basin and the upper and middle Dnieper. It is usually dated from about the seventh century BCE, with its end placed by different scholars anywhere from the third century BCE to the first century CE. On Ukrainian territory it is often called the Pidhirtsi culture, and it is frequently linked with the Neuri, a people described by the Greek historian [Herodotus](/person/herodotus).

## Discovery and definition

The culture takes its name from a hillfort near the village of Milograd (Milahrad) in the Gomel region of Belarus. It was identified in the mid-twentieth century: the archaeologist O. N. Melnikovskaya studied the Milograd settlement in the 1950s, while V. Danylenko recorded similar sites at Pidhirtsi near Kyiv. Its territory ran roughly from the middle Berezina in the north to the Ros river in the south, and from the Western Bug in the west to the Iput in the east. Neighbouring cultures included the Dnieper–Dvina culture to the north, the Yukhniv culture to the east and the [Pomeranian culture](/place/pomeranian-culture) to the west, with the [Scythians](/place/scythians) on the steppe to the south. Scholars have divided it into as many as six local variants.

## Settlements and economy

Milograd communities lived both in fortified hillforts and in open villages. Their houses were half-sunken pit dwellings with timber walls or surface buildings with lowered floors, heated by open hearths. People kept livestock, farmed by slash-and-burn clearance, hunted forest animals and fished. They smelted iron and worked bronze and other metals, making sickles, axes, knives, spearheads, arrowheads, horse harness, needles and razors.

## Pottery, ornaments and burial

The pottery is mostly small, round-bottomed and plain. A typical find is the clay spindle whorl, often cross-shaped. Bronze and iron pins and pendants carry ring and spiral ornament, and patterns such as circles and stars have been read as signs of sun or star cults. Imported or imitated objects, among them Scythian-style daggers and harness fittings, La Tène bracelets and Greek glass beads, show contact with the steppe and beyond; Scythian influence is strongest in the south, while influence from the Lusatian world appears in the north. Cremation was the usual burial rite, with burned remains placed in small round pits, while barrow burials are known only in the west.

## Who were they?

Since Melnikovskaya's work, the Milograd people have often been equated with the Neuri, a people living beyond Scythia who, in the account of Herodotus, refused to join the Scythians against the Persian king [Darius the Great](/person/darius-the-great) around 513 BCE. Not all scholars accept this identification; one has proposed the Androphagi instead. Their language is equally debated: some archaeologists consider them [Balts](/place/balts), others early Slavs or their ancestors, and at least one, P. N. Tretyakov, changed his view from Slavic to Baltic.

## Successors

Origins are also unclear. One view traces the culture to eastern groups of the Bronze Age Trzciniec culture, though more recent work sees a gap, in the ninth to seventh centuries BCE, between the two. Towards the end, newcomers linked with the Pomeranian tradition moved in from the north-west, and the Zarubintsy culture replaced Milograd across much of its area. Whether Zarubintsy grew from Milograd is disputed, but Milograd traditions are visible in some Zarubintsy sites, and in the north part of the area joined the late [Brushed Pottery culture](/place/brushed-pottery-culture).
