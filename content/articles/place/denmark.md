Denmark is one of the oldest kingdoms in Europe. Its royal line is traditionally traced back to Gorm the Old in the tenth century, whose son Harald Bluetooth, according to a carved runestone at Jelling, "made the Danes Christian". For much of the Middle Ages and early modern period Denmark was the leading power of Scandinavia.

## The Jelling kings

The Jelling stones, carved runestones in Jutland, are often called Denmark's birth certificate. The larger was raised by Harald Bluetooth around 965 to honour his parents, Gorm and Thyra, and declares that Harald "won for himself all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian". Harald also built ring fortresses, such as Trelleborg, and the Danevirke, an earthwork across southern Jutland, was strengthened against the Germans.

## The North Sea empire

Harald's son Sweyn Forkbeard conquered England in 1013, and Sweyn's son Cnut the Great ruled England from 1016, Denmark from 1018 and Norway from 1028, creating a North Sea empire. He was a Christian king who made a pilgrimage to Rome. His empire broke apart after his death in 1035.

## The Valdemar era

In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the Valdemar kings expanded Danish power around the Baltic. Bishop Absalon founded Copenhagen around 1167 and led campaigns against the Wends. Valdemar II conquered northern Estonia in 1219 and issued the Jutland Code, a law code, in 1241. After his death, royal power declined, and the nobility and the Hanseatic League gained influence.

## Margaret and the Kalmar Union

Margaret I, daughter of Valdemar IV, became ruler of Denmark, Norway and Sweden, and created the Kalmar Union in 1397. Denmark was the leading partner, and the union lasted, in some form, until Sweden broke away in 1523. Denmark controlled the Øresund, the strait between the North Sea and the Baltic, and from 1429 collected the Sound Dues from passing ships, a major source of royal revenue until 1857.

## Reformation and wars with Sweden

Christian III introduced Lutheranism in 1536, after a civil war, the Count's Feud. Christian IV, king from 1588 to 1648, built Copenhagen's Rosenborg Castle, the Round Tower and the Børsen stock exchange, but lost wars against Sweden. In 1658 Denmark lost Skåne, Halland and Blekinge. In 1660 Frederick III became absolute monarch, and the Royal Law of 1665 was the only written constitution of absolute monarchy in Europe.

## Reforms and the nineteenth century

In the late eighteenth century, land reforms freed peasants from feudal obligations. Denmark lost Norway in 1814. After revolutions in 1848, the constitution of 1849 ended absolutism. Denmark lost Schleswig and Holstein to Prussia and Austria in 1864, a national trauma. The philosopher Søren Kierkegaard and the writer Hans Christian Andersen lived in the nineteenth century. Cooperative dairies and folk high schools, inspired by N. F. S. Grundtvig, transformed rural society.

## Modern Denmark

Denmark sold its West Indian islands to the United States in 1917. Women gained the vote in 1915. Northern Schleswig voted to return to Denmark in 1920. Germany occupied Denmark from 1940 to 1945; in October 1943, Danes helped almost all of the country's roughly 7,000 Jews escape by boat to neutral Sweden. After the war, Denmark joined NATO and later the European Community, and built a strong welfare state. Greenland gained home rule in 1979 and self-government in 2009, and the Faroe Islands have had home rule since 1948.
