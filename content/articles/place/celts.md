The Celts were a family of peoples across ancient Europe who spoke related languages, now called Celtic. In the last centuries BCE they lived from Ireland and Spain through Gaul and the Alps to the Danube, and one group, the Galatians, even settled in Anatolia. Today Celtic languages survive in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany and, in revived form, Cornwall and the Isle of Man.

## Celtic languages

The Celtic languages form a branch of the Indo-European family. Continental Celtic languages, such as Gaulish, Celtiberian, Lepontic and Galatian, are known from inscriptions and place names, and are extinct. Insular Celtic languages divide into two groups: Goidelic, including Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx, and Brittonic, including Welsh, Breton and Cornish. Place names across Europe, such as Paris, from the Parisii, London, from Londinium, Vienna, from Vindobona, and the rivers Rhine and Danube, have Celtic origins.

## Hallstatt

The Hallstatt culture, named after a site in the Austrian Alps where a large prehistoric salt mine and cemetery were found, flourished from around 800 to 450 BCE. Its elites buried their dead with wagons, weapons, gold and imported Greek and Etruscan goods. The Hochdorf burial, in Germany, contained a chieftain on a bronze couch, with gold-covered shoes and a huge Greek cauldron. The hillfort of the Heuneburg traded with the Greek colony of Massalia, Marseille.

## La Tène

The La Tène culture, from about 450 BCE, spread across much of Europe. Its art is famous for flowing, curving designs of plants and animals on metalwork: torcs, neck rings worn by warriors and gods, shields such as the Battersea Shield, and mirrors. The Gundestrup cauldron, found in Denmark, depicts gods and rituals, including a figure with antlers, often identified as the god Cernunnos.

## Expansion and contact

In the fourth and third centuries BCE, Celtic groups moved into Italy, sacking Rome around 390 BCE, into the Balkans, attacking Delphi in 279 BCE, and into Anatolia, where the Galatians settled; Paul wrote his Letter to the Galatians to their descendants. Celtic warriors served as mercenaries for Greek and Carthaginian armies. Greek and Roman writers described the Celts as brave, quarrelsome and fond of feasting, and noted their tradition of taking heads as trophies.

## Society and religion

Celtic society was led by warrior aristocracies and kings or chieftains. The druids, according to Caesar, were priests, judges and teachers who trained for up to twenty years, memorising knowledge orally, and believed in the transmigration of souls. Celtic religion involved many gods, sacred groves, springs and lakes, into which offerings such as weapons were thrown. Large towns, oppida, such as Bibracte and Manching, had walls, workshops and coinage.

## Conquest by Rome

The Romans conquered Cisalpine Gaul, in northern Italy, in the third and second centuries BCE, then southern Gaul. Caesar's conquest of Gaul in 58–50 BCE was devastating: ancient writers claimed a million killed and a million enslaved. Vercingetorix united the Gauls but surrendered at Alesia in 52 BCE. In Britain, Roman conquest from 43 CE met resistance from Caratacus and Boudica. Gaulish culture gradually merged with Roman culture, and Latin replaced Gaulish.

## The medieval Celts

In Ireland, never conquered by Rome, Celtic society survived, with its kings, laws, the Brehon laws, and poets. After Saint Patrick's mission in the fifth century, Ireland became Christian, and Irish monks produced illuminated manuscripts such as the Book of Kells and founded monasteries across Europe. Welsh, Breton and Cornish communities preserved Brittonic languages and traditions, including the legends of King Arthur.
