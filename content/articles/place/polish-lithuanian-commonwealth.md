The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was one of the largest states in early modern Europe. Formed by the Union of Lublin in 1569, it joined the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania under a single elected monarch and a common parliament, the Sejm, while each kept its own army, treasury and laws.

## A union long in the making

The two countries had shared a ruler since 1386, when the Lithuanian grand duke Jogaila married Queen Jadwiga of Poland, became King Władysław II Jagiełło and converted Lithuania to Catholic Christianity. Together they defeated the Teutonic Order at Grunwald in 1410. When the Jagiellonian line was about to die out, the nobility turned the personal union into a permanent one.

## A union of two nations

The Commonwealth was formally a union of the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. They shared a king, elected jointly, a parliament and a currency, but each kept its own army, treasury, offices and laws. Lithuania, which covered much of today's Belarus and, until 1569, much of Ukraine, had its own legal code, the Lithuanian Statutes. In the Union of Lublin, the Ukrainian lands were transferred from Lithuania to the Polish Crown.

## Many peoples, many faiths

The Commonwealth was one of the most diverse states in Europe. Its population included Poles, Lithuanians, Ruthenians (ancestors of Ukrainians and Belarusians), Germans, Jews, Armenians and Tatars. Catholics, Orthodox Christians, Protestants, Jews and Muslims lived within its borders. The Union of Brest of 1596 created the Uniate, or Greek Catholic, Church, which recognised the pope while keeping Orthodox rites, causing tensions. The Jewish community, governed by its own Council of Four Lands, was the largest in the world.

## Golden freedom

The Henrician Articles of 1573, which every elected king had to accept, guaranteed the nobles' rights: free election of kings, regular sessions of the Sejm, the nobles' consent for taxes and war, and the right to resist a king who broke his promises. The nobility saw their system as the freest in Europe. Elections were often contested by foreign candidates, including Henry of Valois, later Henry III of France, and the Swedish Vasa dynasty.

## The breadbasket of Europe

The Commonwealth's economy was based on exporting grain, via the Vistula river and the port of Gdańsk, to western Europe, especially the Netherlands. Large noble estates relied on serf labour, and serfdom became harsher in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Great magnate families, such as the Radziwiłłs and Wiśniowieckis, owned enormous lands, especially in Ukraine, and became more powerful than the king.

## Victories and the Deluge

In the early seventeenth century, the Commonwealth's armies, with their famous winged hussars, won great victories: over the Swedes at Kircholm in 1605, over the Russians at Klushino in 1610, when Polish troops occupied Moscow. But from 1648 the Commonwealth suffered a series of disasters: the Khmelnytsky Uprising of Ukrainian Cossacks, the Russian invasion and the Swedish invasion known as the Deluge, from 1655 to 1660. Its population fell sharply, and it lost left-bank Ukraine and Kyiv to Russia.

## Sobieski

John III Sobieski, king from 1674, was a famous military leader. In September 1683, he led the combined army that relieved the Ottoman siege of Vienna, the last great Ottoman advance into Europe. But the Commonwealth was weakened. In the eighteenth century, under the Saxon kings, Russia increasingly controlled its politics, and the Great Northern War devastated its lands.

## Reform and partition

The last king, Stanisław August Poniatowski, a former lover of Catherine the Great, supported Enlightenment reforms. The Constitution of 3 May 1791 was a great achievement, but neighbours used Polish conservatives, who formed the Targowica Confederation, as a pretext to intervene. After the partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795, the Commonwealth disappeared, though its memory inspired Polish and Lithuanian nationalism.

## On our maps

Our maps use the name from 1400 to 1715, including the period of the personal union before 1569. The outline is largest on the 1492 map at about 1.2 million square kilometres, more than a third of it in today's Ukraine and the rest spread across Poland, Belarus, Russia and Lithuania. Warsaw, Kraków, Vilnius, Lviv and Minsk are inside it throughout; Kyiv until 1700.
