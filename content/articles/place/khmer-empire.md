The Khmer Empire ruled much of mainland Southeast Asia from the early ninth century to the fifteenth. Centred on Angkor, in what is now northern Cambodia, it built some of the largest religious monuments in the world, and its capital was one of the largest pre-industrial cities anywhere.

## Before Angkor

The Khmer people's earlier states, known from Chinese sources as Funan and Chenla, flourished in the Mekong delta and valley from the first centuries CE. They adopted Indian religion, Sanskrit and ideas of kingship. Angkor Borei and the port of Oc Eo, in today's Vietnam, traded with India and China. When Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch in 802, he united several of these Khmer territories.

## The god-king

The empire's traditional starting date is 802, when a prince named Jayavarman II declared himself universal ruler in a ceremony on the Kulen hills, freeing the Khmer from the overlordship of Java. His successors moved the capital to the Angkor area, north of the great lake of Tonlé Sap. Khmer kings were closely linked to the Hindu gods Shiva and Vishnu, and later to the Buddha, and each great king built a temple-mountain as a centre of the state and his own cult.

## Kings and temples

Khmer kings saw themselves as devaraja, linked to a god, usually Shiva, and each great king built a state temple at the centre of his capital, symbolising Mount Meru, the mountain at the centre of the universe in Hindu cosmology. Yasovarman I built Phnom Bakheng around 900 and founded the city of Yasodharapura at Angkor. Rajendravarman built Pre Rup, and the delicate pink sandstone temple of Banteay Srei was built by a royal adviser in 967.

## Water and rice

Angkor's prosperity was based on rice farming in the plains around Tonlé Sap, the great lake whose flow reverses each year with the monsoon floods of the Mekong. The kings built huge reservoirs, barays: the West Baray, built in the eleventh century, is about eight kilometres long and two kilometres wide. Canals, embankments and moats managed water across the region. Whether these served mainly for irrigation or for religious symbolism is debated, but they show a remarkable capacity to organise labour.

## Angkor Wat

Suryavarman II, who ruled from 1113 to around 1150, built Angkor Wat, dedicated to Vishnu, with its five lotus-shaped towers, vast moat and galleries carved with bas-reliefs of the Hindu epics, the churning of the ocean of milk, and the king himself leading his army. He also fought the Champa kingdom and Dai Viet, and sent embassies to China.

## Jayavarman VII

After the Cham sacked Angkor in 1177, Jayavarman VII, a Mahayana Buddhist, drove them out and conquered Champa in 1203. He built Angkor Thom, a walled city with five gates, and at its centre the Bayon temple, famous for its smiling stone faces, perhaps representing the king as a bodhisattva. He also built Ta Prohm, now overgrown with giant trees, and Preah Khan, along with over a hundred hospitals and rest houses along the roads.

## Zhou Daguan

The Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan visited Angkor in 1296–1297 and wrote the only surviving eyewitness account of daily life there. He described the king's processions, markets run by women, houses, festivals, bathing in rivers and the presence of Chinese merchants.

## Decline and rediscovery

In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Theravada Buddhism spread among the population, temple building declined, and the Thai kingdoms grew stronger. After Ayutthaya's capture of Angkor in 1431, the Khmer court moved south. Angkor Wat was never fully abandoned, remaining a Buddhist monastery, and in the 1860s the French explorer Henri Mouhot made it famous in Europe. Today it appears on Cambodia's flag.
