Spain as a single kingdom emerged from the union of the crowns of Castile and Aragon in the late fifteenth century, after the marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella. This page covers modern Spain; for the medieval kingdoms and the overseas empire, see the [Crown of Castile](/place/crown-of-castile) and the [Spanish Empire](/place/spanish-empire).

## The Habsburgs and the Bourbons

Under the Habsburg kings Charles I and Philip II, Spain ruled a global empire in the sixteenth century, and its Golden Age produced Cervantes, Velázquez and El Greco. In the seventeenth century, Spain declined, losing wars and facing revolts in Catalonia and Portugal in 1640. When the last Habsburg, Charles II, died childless in 1700, the War of the Spanish Succession brought the French Bourbon Philip V to the throne. He abolished the separate institutions of Aragon, Catalonia and Valencia, centralising the state; Barcelona fell to his forces on 11 September 1714, now Catalonia's national day.

## Napoleon and the Peninsular War

In 1808 Napoleon forced Charles IV and Ferdinand VII to abdicate and placed his brother Joseph on the throne. On 2 May 1808, the people of Madrid rose up, and French executions the next day were painted by Goya. A long war followed, with Spanish guerrillas, a word coined then, and British forces under Wellington. In 1812 the Cortes of Cádiz adopted a liberal constitution. Most of Spain's American colonies became independent between 1810 and 1826.

## A troubled century

Ferdinand VII returned in 1814 and abolished the constitution. After his death in 1833, the Carlist Wars between supporters of his daughter Isabella II and his brother Carlos divided the country. Spain went through revolutions, a short-lived First Republic in 1873–1874 and the restoration of the Bourbons in 1874. In 1898 Spain lost Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines to the United States, a trauma that inspired the "Generation of '98" writers.

## The Second Republic and civil war

After the dictatorship of Miguel Primo de Rivera, from 1923 to 1930, the Second Republic was proclaimed in 1931, introducing women's suffrage, land reform and Catalan autonomy, and reducing the power of the Church and army. Spain was deeply divided. In July 1936 army officers, led by Francisco Franco, rebelled. In the civil war, the Nationalists were supported by Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, whose planes bombed Guernica in 1937, and the Republicans by the Soviet Union and the International Brigades. Both sides committed atrocities. Franco won in 1939; perhaps 500,000 people died.

## Franco's Spain

Franco ruled as dictator until his death in 1975. Tens of thousands of opponents were executed after the war, and Catalan, Basque and Galician languages were repressed. Spain stayed out of the Second World War. From the late 1950s, economic reforms and tourism brought rapid growth, the "Spanish miracle". The Basque separatist group ETA began violent attacks in the 1960s.

## Democracy

After Franco's death, King Juan Carlos I supported the transition to democracy. The constitution of 1978 created a parliamentary monarchy with autonomous communities. On 23 February 1981, a coup attempt, in which civil guards stormed parliament, failed after the king opposed it on television. Spain joined NATO in 1982 and the European Community in 1986, and hosted the Olympics in Barcelona in 1992. ETA announced the end of its armed campaign in 2011. In 2017 Catalonia held an independence referendum declared illegal by Spanish courts, causing a political crisis.

## On our maps

Our maps label the country Spain from 1900 to 2010. Its largest outline, about 790,000 square kilometres on the 1938 map, includes Spanish colonies in Africa: Western Sahara makes up a third of it, with a small part in Equatorial Guinea. Earlier maps show Spain under the names Castile, Aragon or the Spanish Empire.
