Kievan Rus' was the first East Slavic state, a federation of principalities centred on Kyiv that flourished from the late ninth century to the Mongol invasion of 1240. Ukraine, Russia and Belarus all trace their history back to it, and the question of who are its true heirs is still politically charged.

## The Varangians and the Rus'

The name Rus' probably came from Scandinavian traders and warriors, called Varangians, who travelled along the rivers of Eastern Europe in the ninth century. The Primary Chronicle tells how Slavic and Finnic tribes, tired of conflict, invited Rurik to rule them. The Scandinavian elite soon merged with the Slavic population, and later rulers had Slavic names. The Rus' attacked Constantinople in 860 and later made trade treaties with Byzantium, in 911 and 944, whose texts survive.

## Olga and Sviatoslav

Princess Olga ruled as regent for her son Sviatoslav after her husband Igor was killed by the Drevlians in 945, taking terrible revenge, according to the Chronicle. She visited Constantinople and was baptised around 957. Sviatoslav, a warrior prince, destroyed the Khazar khaganate in the 960s and campaigned in Bulgaria, but was defeated by the Byzantines and killed by the Pechenegs in 972, who made a cup from his skull.

## Volodymyr and Christianity

Volodymyr the Great, ruler from about 980, considered several religions. According to the Chronicle, he sent envoys to observe Islam, Judaism, Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, and his envoys reported that in Hagia Sophia in Constantinople they did not know whether they were in heaven or on earth. In 988 he was baptised, married the Byzantine princess Anna, and had the people of Kyiv baptised in the Dnieper. Christianity brought church buildings, icons, monasteries, writing in Church Slavonic and closer ties with Byzantium.

## Yaroslav the Wise

Yaroslav, who ruled Kyiv from 1019 to 1054, built the Cathedral of St Sophia and the Golden Gate in Kyiv, founded schools and libraries, and issued the Russkaya Pravda, a law code. His family married into the royal houses of Europe: his daughters married kings of Norway, Hungary and France; Anne of Kyiv became queen of France. The Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, the Monastery of the Caves, was founded in this period.

## Cities and trade

Kievan Rus' had many towns: Kyiv, Novgorod, Chernihiv, Smolensk, Polotsk, Vladimir and others. Novgorod's birch-bark letters show widespread literacy. Trade with Byzantium, Scandinavia and the Islamic world brought wealth. The towns had assemblies, veche, that sometimes challenged the princes.

## Fragmentation

Yaroslav divided his lands among his sons, establishing a system in which princes moved between cities by seniority, which led to conflicts. Volodymyr Monomakh, prince of Kyiv from 1113 to 1125, restored unity for a time and wrote an Instruction for his children. After his son Mstyslav died in 1132, Rus' broke into principalities such as Vladimir-Suzdal, Galicia-Volhynia, Novgorod, Smolensk and Chernihiv. The Polovtsians, or Cumans, raided from the steppe; a failed campaign against them in 1185 inspired the Tale of Igor's Campaign.

## The Mongol conquest

The Mongols defeated the Rus' princes and Cumans at the Kalka river in 1223. In 1237–1240, Batu's armies destroyed Ryazan, Vladimir, Chernihiv and Kyiv. The principalities became tributaries of the Golden Horde.

## Legacy

Ukrainians, Russians and Belarusians all claim Kievan Rus' as part of their heritage. The question of its legacy has become politically charged, especially in the conflict between Russia and Ukraine.

## On our maps

Our maps show Kievan Rus' on the 800, 900, 1000 and 1100 maps. Its largest outline, about 1.6 million square kilometres on the 1100 map, is two-thirds in today's Russia and most of the rest in Ukraine and Belarus. Kyiv lies inside it from 900, Moscow's site from 1000, and the site of Saint Petersburg throughout. By 1200, our maps show the Rus' as separate principalities.
