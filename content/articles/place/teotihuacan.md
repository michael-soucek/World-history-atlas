Teotihuacan was the largest city in the Americas in the first half of the first millennium CE, and one of the largest in the world. At its height, around 450 CE, perhaps 100,000 people or more lived there, in a valley north-east of today's Mexico City. Its ruins, dominated by the Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon, are among the most visited archaeological sites in the world.

## Origins

The Valley of Mexico had several growing towns in the late first millennium BCE, including Cuicuilco, in the south. Around the turn of the millennium, a volcanic eruption of Xitle covered Cuicuilco with lava, and many people may have moved to Teotihuacan, in the north-east of the valley, which grew rapidly. Its location gave it access to obsidian from the nearby Pachuca and Otumba sources, fertile soils and springs.

## The city plan

Teotihuacan was laid out on a grid, oriented about 15.5 degrees east of north, perhaps aligned to astronomical events or the mountain Cerro Gordo. The Avenue of the Dead, over two kilometres long, ran north to the Pyramid of the Moon. The Pyramid of the Sun, about 65 metres high, was built over a cave, which may have been a sacred place. The Ciudadela, a great enclosure, held the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, decorated with carved serpent heads and headdresses.

## Apartment compounds

Most of the city's people lived in about 2,000 walled apartment compounds, each housing perhaps 60 to 100 people, with rooms around courtyards, drains and shrines. Some compounds, such as Tetitla and Atetelco, have colourful murals of gods, priests, animals and plants. The Tepantitla murals show a paradise of the rain god, with people playing, swimming and singing.

## Neighbourhoods

Teotihuacan was a multi-ethnic city. The Oaxaca Barrio housed people from the Zapotec region, who kept their own pottery styles and burial customs, and the Merchants' Barrio had people from the Gulf Coast and Maya lowlands. Craft workshops produced obsidian tools, pottery, figurines and textiles on a large scale.

## Religion

Important gods included the Storm God, related to the later Tlaloc, the Feathered Serpent and the Great Goddess, often shown in murals. Human sacrifice was practised: excavations at the Temple of the Feathered Serpent and the Pyramid of the Moon found remains of sacrificed people, many dressed as warriors, buried during construction.

## Teotihuacan and the Maya

Teotihuacan's influence reached the Maya region. Maya inscriptions at Tikal record that in 378 CE a man named Siyaj K'ak', "Fire Is Born", arrived from the west, and the same day the Tikal king died. A new dynasty, possibly linked to Teotihuacan, took power. Teotihuacan-style art and architecture appear at Kaminaljuyu, in Guatemala, and Copán, in Honduras.

## Government and decline

How Teotihuacan was governed remains a mystery. There are no clear images of rulers, and some scholars suggest it was ruled by a council or collective leadership. Around 550 CE, the main temples and elite buildings along the Avenue of the Dead were burned, perhaps in a revolt against the elite. The population declined, though the city was not abandoned. When the Aztecs found its ruins, they named it Teotihuacan, "where the gods were made", and believed that the sun and moon were created there. Its pyramids are a World Heritage Site.
