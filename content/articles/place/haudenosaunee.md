The Haudenosaunee, "people of the longhouse", are a confederacy of Indigenous nations in north-eastern North America, often called the Iroquois, a name probably derived from a term used by their enemies. Originally five nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca, in what is now upstate New York, they became six when the Tuscarora joined in the 1720s. For two centuries they were one of the most powerful forces in the region.

## The Great Law of Peace

According to Haudenosaunee tradition, the nations were once trapped in cycles of war and revenge. A figure called the Peacemaker, born among the Huron, travelled to their lands with a message of peace, helped by Hiawatha, an Onondaga or Mohawk man grieving the loss of his family. They overcame the opposition of the powerful Onondaga leader Tadodaho and persuaded the five nations to accept the Great Law of Peace, Gayanashagowa. The nations planted a Tree of Peace, a white pine, and buried their weapons beneath it. The date of the founding is debated; some argue for the twelfth century, citing an eclipse in 1142, and others for the fifteenth or sixteenth.

## Government

The Great Law set up a Grand Council of fifty chiefs, hoyaneh, from the five nations, meeting at Onondaga, the central nation, which kept the council fire. Chiefs were chosen by clan mothers, senior women of each clan, who could also remove them. Decisions required consensus. The Mohawk and Seneca were the "elder brothers", the Oneida and Cayuga the "younger brothers", and the Onondaga the "firekeepers". Wampum belts, made of shell beads, recorded laws, treaties and agreements.

## Longhouses and the Three Sisters

The Haudenosaunee lived in villages of longhouses, long bark-covered buildings shared by several related families of the same clan, sometimes over 60 metres long, often protected by palisades. Women owned the houses and fields and farmed maize, beans and squash, the Three Sisters, planted together. Men hunted, fished, traded and fought. Descent passed through the mother's line.

## The Beaver Wars

In the seventeenth century, the Haudenosaunee traded furs with the Dutch at Fort Orange, now Albany, and later the English, gaining guns. In a series of conflicts known as the Beaver Wars, they fought to control the fur trade and to replace population lost to epidemics by adopting captives. In 1649 they destroyed the Huron, or Wendat, confederacy, and later defeated the Neutral, Erie and others. They fought the French and their allies for decades, until the Great Peace of Montreal in 1701.

## Diplomacy between empires

In the eighteenth century, the Haudenosaunee balanced Britain and France, making treaties, such as the Covenant Chain with Britain. The Mohawk leader Hendrick Theyanoguin and the British official Sir William Johnson, who married the Mohawk Molly Brant, played important roles. At the Albany Congress of 1754, colonial delegates discussed the confederacy as a model of union.

## The American Revolution

The Revolution split the confederacy. Most Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga and Onondaga sided with Britain, led by Joseph Brant, Molly's brother, while the Oneida and Tuscarora largely supported the Americans. In 1779 the Sullivan Expedition destroyed dozens of Haudenosaunee villages and crops. After the war, Brant led many to Canada, where the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve was founded in 1784. Those who stayed lost most of their land through treaties and sales.

## Today

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy continues, with communities in New York, Ontario, Quebec, Wisconsin and Oklahoma. It issues its own passports. Historians debate how much the Great Law influenced the US Constitution; in 1988 the US Congress acknowledged the confederacy's influence. The game of lacrosse comes from Haudenosaunee and other Indigenous traditions, and the Haudenosaunee Nationals compete internationally.
