faq:
  - Q: Who founded the Kushan Empire? A: Kujula Kadphises, chief of the Guishuang, one of the five tribes of the Yuezhi. According to the Chinese Book of the Later Han, he made the other Yuezhi clans submit and founded the empire in the first century CE. An earlier ruler, Heraios, was the first to call himself Kushan on coins.
  - Q: Where did the Kushans come from? A: They descended from the Yuezhi, nomads who lived in what is now Gansu and eastern Xinjiang until the Xiongnu drove them west from about 176 BCE. Scholars have proposed Indo-European, Tocharian or Iranian Saka origins for the Yuezhi, but the question is unresolved.
  - Q: What language did the Kushans speak? A: Their rulers at first used Greek, inherited from the Greek kingdoms of Bactria, for coins and administration. The Rabatak inscription records that Kanishka replaced it with Bactrian, an Iranian language written in Greek letters, around 127 CE. In India, Gandhari Prakrit and Sanskrit were also used.
  - Q: What religion did the Kushans follow? A: Many. Their coins honour Greek, Iranian and Indian deities as well as the Buddha, and their subjects included Hindus, Buddhists and Zoroastrians. Kanishka is remembered in Buddhist tradition for a council in Kashmir, and Kushan patronage helped Buddhism travel along the Silk Road to China.
  - Q: Why did the Kushan Empire decline? A: After Vasudeva I died around 225 the empire split. The Sasanian Persians took the western half and installed vassal rulers, while the Guptas took Mathura. Smaller Kushan kings in the Punjab survived, probably as Gupta vassals, until the Kidarites ended their rule in the later fourth century.

The Kushan Empire ruled a vast territory from Central Asia to northern India in the first to the third centuries CE. At the crossroads of the Silk Road, it brought together Greek, Iranian, Indian and Chinese influences, and played a key role in spreading Buddhism to Central Asia and China.

## From Bactria to India

When the Yuezhi conquered Bactria in the second century BCE, they found a Greek kingdom with cities such as Ai-Khanoum, with its theatre, gymnasium and Greek inscriptions. The Yuezhi settled there and absorbed Greek culture. Kujula Kadphises, in the first century CE, united the five Yuezhi tribes and crossed the Hindu Kush, taking Gandhara and Kabul. His son Vima Takto and grandson Vima Kadphises extended the empire into northern India and issued gold coins, using gold from Roman trade.

## Kanishka

Kanishka I, whose reign began around 127 CE, ruled an empire from Central Asia to the Ganges plain. The Rabatak inscription, found in Afghanistan in 1993, written in Bactrian, lists his ancestors and the cities he ruled, including cities in the Ganges valley. Kanishka built a great stupa at Peshawar, which Chinese pilgrims described as very tall. Buddhist tradition says he convened a council of monks in Kashmir. He also honoured Iranian and Hindu gods on his coins.

## Coins and gods

Kushan coins show an extraordinary mix of gods: Greek gods such as Helios and Heracles; Iranian gods, such as Mithra, Ardochsho and Oesho; Indian gods, such as Shiva; and the Buddha, labelled "Boddo", one of the earliest images of him. The kings were shown in Central Asian dress, with boots, long coats and swords, sometimes with flames rising from their shoulders.

## Gandhara art

In Gandhara, around Peshawar and the Swat valley, Buddhist art developed a distinctive style influenced by Greco-Roman art, with realistic figures, draped robes and wavy hair. The Buddha was depicted as a human figure, rather than through symbols such as footprints or a wheel. Monasteries and stupas, such as Takht-i-Bahi, were decorated with reliefs of the Buddha's life. In Mathura, sculptors created Buddhist, Jain and Hindu images in a local Indian style.

## Royal portraits

At Mat, near Mathura, and at Surkh Kotal in Afghanistan, the Kushans built dynastic sanctuaries with large statues of their kings. A headless statue of Kanishka from Mat shows him in heavy boots and a long coat, holding a sword and a mace.

## Trade and Buddhism

The Kushans controlled routes linking the Roman Empire, India, Central Asia and China. The Bagram hoard, found near Kabul, contained Roman glass and bronzes, Indian ivories and Chinese lacquer. Buddhist monks from Kushan lands, such as Lokaksema, travelled to China in the second century CE and translated Buddhist texts into Chinese. Buddhism spread along the Silk Road to the Tarim basin and China.

## Decline

From the mid-third century, the Sasanian Persians took the Kushans' western territories, and Kushano-Sasanian rulers governed Bactria. Smaller Kushan kingdoms survived in the Punjab and Gandhara until the fourth century, when the Guptas and the Kidarites took over.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Kushan Empire on the 100 CE map, with an outline of about 1.7 million square kilometres. A third of it lies in today's Afghanistan and nearly a third in Pakistan, with the rest in Central Asia, Iran and a little in India. Kabul and Samarkand lie inside it.
