Morocco is one of the oldest states in the Islamic world. A series of dynasties has ruled it since the eighth century, and the present royal family, the Alaouites, has been on the throne since the 1660s. Unlike the rest of North Africa, it was never part of the Ottoman Empire.

## Berbers, Phoenicians and Romans

The Amazigh, or Berber, peoples have lived in Morocco since prehistoric times. Phoenician and Carthaginian traders founded ports on the coast, and the Berber kingdom of Mauretania became a Roman client. Rome annexed it in 40 CE as Mauretania Tingitana, with cities such as Volubilis, whose ruins survive. After the Arab conquest at the end of the seventh century, many Berbers converted to Islam, but rebelled against Arab rule in the Great Berber Revolt of 740.

## The Idrisids

In 788 Idris ibn Abdallah, a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad who had fled the Abbasids, was welcomed by Berber tribes and founded the first Moroccan Islamic state. His son Idris II developed Fez, which became a great religious and intellectual centre; the Qarawiyyin mosque and university, founded in 859 by Fatima al-Fihri, is often called the oldest continuously operating university in the world.

## The Berber empires

From the eleventh to the fifteenth century, Berber dynasties built empires. The Almoravids, from the Sahara, founded Marrakesh around 1070 and ruled Morocco and al-Andalus. The Almohads, from the Atlas, replaced them in 1147 and ruled from Morocco to Tunisia. The Marinids, a Zanata Berber dynasty, took Marrakesh in 1269, made Fez their capital and built beautiful madrasas, such as the Bou Inania. Ibn Battuta, born in Tangier in 1304, travelled the world in the Marinid era.

## The Saadis

The Saadis, an Arab dynasty of sharifs from the Draa valley, took power in the sixteenth century. In 1578 at the Battle of the Three Kings, or Alcácer Quibir, they defeated a Portuguese invasion, killing King Sebastian of Portugal. Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur, "the victorious", grew rich from ransoms and sugar, built the El Badi palace in Marrakesh and, in 1591, sent an army across the Sahara that defeated the Songhai Empire at Tondibi and took Timbuktu and Gao. He corresponded with Elizabeth I of England.

## The Alaouites

The Alaouites, another family of sharifs, from Tafilalt, took power in the 1660s. Sultan Moulay Ismail, who ruled from 1672 to 1727, built a strong state, with a large army of enslaved Black soldiers, the Abid al-Bukhari, and a vast palace complex at Meknes. He expelled the English from Tangier in 1684 and the Spanish from several ports. Morocco recognised the United States in 1777, one of the first countries to do so.

## Colonial rule

In the nineteenth century, France, Spain and Britain competed for influence. After the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911, involving Germany, the Treaty of Fez in 1912 made Morocco a French protectorate, with Spain controlling the north and the far south. The French resident-general, Hubert Lyautey, kept the sultan as a figurehead and built new European cities beside the old medinas. In the Rif War of 1921–1926, the Berber leader Abd el-Krim defeated a Spanish army at Annual in 1921 and declared a Rif Republic, before French and Spanish forces crushed him.

## Independence

The nationalist movement, led by the Istiqlal party, grew around Sultan Mohammed V. In 1953 France exiled him to Madagascar, causing widespread protests and violence. He returned in 1955, and Morocco became independent in 1956. Mohammed V took the title of king. His son Hassan II, from 1961 to 1999, survived two coup attempts, in 1971 and 1972, and repressed opposition in the "years of lead". In 1975 he organised the Green March of 350,000 Moroccans into Spanish Sahara; Morocco has since controlled most of Western Sahara, whose status remains disputed with the Polisario Front. King Mohammed VI, from 1999, introduced a new constitution in 2011 and reforms of family law in 2004.

## On our maps

Our maps show Morocco on most maps from 1300 to 2010. Its largest outline, about 1.3 million square kilometres on the 1650 map, reaches deep into the Sahara, and today's Mali and Algeria together make up most of it, because the Saadi conquest of the Songhai Empire put Timbuktu under Moroccan control on our maps from 1650 to 1715. Casablanca and Marrakesh lie inside it for most of the period.
