For about two centuries, from the early seventh century to the mid-ninth, Tibet was one of the great military powers of Asia. The Tibetan Empire, known in Chinese sources as Tubo, ruled the high plateau and pushed out into the lowlands around it, fighting the Tang Chinese, the Abbasid Arabs and the Uyghurs for control of Central Asia.

## The Yarlung kings

The kings of the Yarlung valley, south-east of Lhasa, claimed descent from a divine king who descended from the sky on a cord. Before Songtsen Gampo, they had built up their power through alliances with clans, and his father, Namri Songtsen, expanded their territory. Songtsen Gampo, who reigned from about 618 to 650, built a powerful state through conquest and marriage, including the conquest or absorption of Zhangzhung in the west and the Sumpa in the north.

## Writing and law

Tibetan tradition says that Songtsen Gampo sent a minister, Thonmi Sambhota, to India to develop a script for the Tibetan language, based on an Indian script. The oldest Tibetan documents, including the Old Tibetan Annals and Chronicle, found at Dunhuang, record events of the imperial period year by year. The empire had a sophisticated administration, with a council of ministers, a military organised into regional "horns", a census and taxation, and law codes.

## Buddhism and the old religion

Tibetan Buddhism traces its beginnings to the imperial period. Songtsen Gampo is said to have built the Jokhang and Ramoche temples in Lhasa for the statues brought by his foreign wives. Under Trisong Detsen, the Indian scholar Shantarakshita and the tantric master Padmasambhava, revered by Tibetans as Guru Rinpoche, came to Tibet, and Samye monastery was founded around 779. According to tradition, a great debate held at Samye between Indian and Chinese Buddhists was won by the Indian side. The old religion, linked to the cult of the kings, continued alongside Buddhism, and tensions between the two shaped imperial politics.

## A great military power

Tibetan armies were renowned for their armoured cavalry. They fought the Tang for control of the Tarim basin and the Gansu corridor for over a century, taking the four garrisons of Anxi in the 670s and again later. In 763, after the An Lushan rebellion, they briefly occupied the Tang capital Chang'an and set up a puppet emperor. They also fought the Abbasid Caliphate in Central Asia and the Uyghurs in the north, and extended their power south over Nepal and into northern India. From 786 they held Dunhuang, whose caves preserve many Tibetan documents.

## The treaty of 821–822

After decades of war, Tibet and the Tang made a peace treaty, recorded in Chinese and Tibetan on stone pillars erected in Lhasa and Chang'an. The Lhasa pillar, which still stands in front of the Jokhang temple, declares that "Tibetans shall be happy in Tibet and Chinese shall be happy in China", and fixes the border between them.

## Collapse

Under King Ralpachen (815–838), heavy support for Buddhist monasteries and translation projects angered parts of the nobility. He was assassinated, and his brother Langdarma came to the throne. Later Buddhist histories say Langdarma persecuted Buddhism until he was assassinated by a monk around 842. A dispute over his succession split the dynasty, rebellions broke out, and the empire disintegrated. Its descendants ruled small kingdoms, such as Guge in the west.

## Legacy

The Tibetan Empire gave Tibet its written language, its first Buddhist institutions and a lasting sense of political identity. Later Tibetans looked back to its three great "dharma kings", Songtsen Gampo, Trisong Detsen and Ralpachen.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Tibetan Empire on the 700 and 800 maps. It is largest on the second, at about 4.5 million square kilometres. Most of that lies in today's China, which includes the Tibetan plateau, Qinghai and Xinjiang, with smaller shares in India, Nepal and Myanmar. Lhasa and Kashgar are Tibetan on both maps, Kathmandu on the 800 map. By 900 our maps show a smaller Tibet.
