The medieval Kingdom of Croatia was the first Croatian monarchy, a state on the eastern shore of the Adriatic that lasted from around 925 until 1102. Its kings, mostly of the Trpimirović dynasty, ruled the bulk of present-day [Croatia](/country/croatia) and much of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and dealt by turns in war and alliance with Bulgaria, Byzantium, Hungary and Venice. In 1102 the crown passed to the Hungarian king Coloman, beginning a link with Hungary that lasted in some form until 1918.

## From duchy to kingdom

Slavs settled the region in the early seventh century, and a Duchy of Croatia emerged under rival families, at times subject to the Franks or to Byzantium. In 879 the pope recognised Duke Branimir as an independent ruler. Tomislav, a descendant of Trpimir I, united the Croats of Pannonia and Dalmatia some time between 923 and 928. Records of the Council of Split in 925 and a letter from Pope John X call him king, although nobody knows whether he was ever crowned, or when and by whom. His realm was organised into eleven counties and one banate, each with a fortified royal town.

Allied with Byzantium, Tomislav sheltered Serbs driven out by [Simeon I of Bulgaria](/person/simeon-i-of-bulgaria), and in 926 his forces defeated a Bulgarian army sent against him. Peace followed Simeon's death in 927, with papal legates as go-betweens.

## The tenth century

Under Tomislav's successors, local chiefs were gradually replaced by royal retainers holding land, and free peasants slid into serfdom. King Miroslav was killed by his own ban, Pribina, in a power struggle, and order was restored by Michael Krešimir II, who ruled from 949 to 969. His queen Helen, buried at Solin in 976, was described on her tomb as "mother of the kingdom". Their son Stephen Držislav, king until 997, received royal insignia from Byzantium.

## Venice, Byzantium and the peak

A violent contest among Držislav's three sons let others move in. From 1000 the Venetian doge Pietro II Orseolo took control of the Adriatic islands and coastal cities, and Croatia was briefly a Byzantine vassal from 1018 to 1025. Peter Krešimir IV, who reigned from 1058 to 1074, brought the kingdom to its greatest extent, winning Byzantine recognition as overlord of the Dalmatian cities; from 1060 the realm was styled the Kingdom of Croatia and Dalmatia. He was captured by Norman forces in 1074, and the [Republic of Venice](/place/republic-of-venice) soon took the coastal cities.

## Zvonimir and the church

Demetrius Zvonimir, crowned at Solin on 8 October 1075 with the backing of [Pope Gregory VII](/person/pope-gregory-vii), tied Croatia closely to Rome and to western Europe; noble titles were now modelled on western ones. He married Helen, daughter of King Béla I of Hungary. The Baška tablet, which names him as king, is one of the oldest surviving texts in Croatian. The kingdom had no fixed capital; royal seats included Nin, Biograd, Knin, Šibenik and Solin.

## Succession crisis and union with Hungary

Zvonimir died in 1089, and his successor Stephen II, the last of the Trpimirović line, died without an heir early in 1091. Helen's brother, Ladislaus I of Hungary, claimed the throne, overran the region known as Slavonia and was halted near the Gvozd mountains. His nephew Coloman defeated the last native king, Petar, at Gvozd in 1097 and was crowned in Biograd in 1102.

Historians still debate the terms. A document called the Pacta conventa, setting out the privileges of twelve Croatian noble families, is now widely considered a fourteenth-century forgery, yet it reflects how rule worked in practice. Croatia kept its own ban and assembly of nobles, the Sabor, under the [Kingdom of Hungary](/place/kingdom-of-hungary). Croatian historians, and a number of Hungarian ones, describe a personal union of two kingdoms under one king, while nationalist historians, especially in the nineteenth century, presented it as annexation.
