Poland's history as a state begins in the tenth century, when Mieszko I of the Piast dynasty ruled the tribes between the Oder and the Vistula and accepted Christianity in 966. Over the following thousand years Poland grew into one of Europe's largest countries, disappeared from the map entirely for over a century, and was rebuilt twice, each time with different borders.

## The Piasts

Mieszko's son Bolesław the Brave was crowned the first king of Poland in 1025. After 1138 the kingdom was divided among Piast princes for nearly two centuries, a period that also saw the Mongol invasion of 1241. Władysław the Elbow-High reunited it and was crowned in 1320, and his son Casimir the Great strengthened the state, founding the university in Kraków in 1364. In 1385–1386 Poland's queen, Jadwiga, married the Lithuanian grand duke Jogaila, beginning the union that became the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.

## The Jagiellonian age

The union with Lithuania under the Jagiellonian dynasty from 1386 made Poland a great power. The victory at Grunwald over the Teutonic Knights in 1410, the Thirteen Years' War, ending in 1466, which gave Poland access to the Baltic through Gdańsk, and dynastic links to Bohemia and Hungary marked its rise. Kraków was a centre of the Renaissance: Nicolaus Copernicus studied at its university, and Sigismund I rebuilt Wawel Castle in the Italian style. The Union of Lublin in 1569 created the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.

## The nobles' democracy

The Polish nobility, the szlachta, made up as much as 8 to 10 percent of the population, an unusually large share. They elected the king, sat in the Sejm, the parliament, and enjoyed extensive rights, which they called the "golden freedom". The 1573 Warsaw Confederation guaranteed religious toleration among the nobility. But the liberum veto, which allowed a single deputy to dissolve a session of the Sejm, increasingly paralysed government from the seventeenth century.

## Partitions and the Constitution of 3 May

In the eighteenth century, Russia, Prussia and Austria interfered in Polish politics. After the first partition in 1772, reformers tried to modernise the state, creating a Commission of National Education, Europe's first ministry of education. The Constitution of 3 May 1791, Europe's first modern written constitution and the world's second after the American, abolished the liberum veto. Russia invaded in 1792, leading to the second partition, and after the Kościuszko Uprising of 1794 was crushed, the third partition of 1795 erased Poland from the map.

## Under foreign rule

For 123 years, Poles lived under Russian, Prussian and Austrian rule. Napoleon created a Duchy of Warsaw, and the Congress of Vienna created a Kingdom of Poland under the Russian tsar. Uprisings against Russia in 1830–1831 and 1863–1864 were crushed. Polish culture survived through literature, by writers such as Adam Mickiewicz, music, by Chopin, and the Catholic Church. Maria Skłodowska-Curie, born in Russian-ruled Warsaw, studied abroad because women could not attend university there.

## The Second Republic

Poland regained independence on 11 November 1918, under Józef Piłsudski. It won a war against Soviet Russia in 1920, including the Battle of Warsaw. The interwar republic was home to Europe's largest Jewish community, about three million people, as well as Ukrainians, Belarusians and Germans. Piłsudski ruled as an authoritarian leader after a coup in 1926.

## War and occupation

On 1 September 1939 Germany invaded, followed by the Soviet Union on 17 September. Occupied Poland suffered terribly: the Nazis built extermination camps, including Auschwitz and Treblinka, on Polish soil, and murdered about three million Polish Jews and millions of other Polish citizens. The Soviets deported hundreds of thousands and murdered about 22,000 Polish officers and others at Katyn in 1940. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943 and the Warsaw Uprising of 1944 were crushed, and Warsaw was almost completely destroyed.

## Communism and freedom

After 1945, Poland's borders moved west, and a communist government took power under Soviet influence. Protests in 1956, 1970 and 1980 challenged the regime. The election of the Polish cardinal Karol Wojtyła as Pope John Paul II in 1978 inspired many. In 1980 the Solidarity trade union, led by Lech Wałęsa, was founded in Gdańsk. After martial law in 1981 and years of struggle, the Round Table talks led to partly free elections in 1989, and Poland became a democracy. It joined NATO in 1999 and the European Union in 2004.

## On our maps

Our maps use the name Poland for several different states. From 1000 to 1300 it is the medieval Piast kingdom. On the 1783 map it is the remnant of the Commonwealth after the first partition, at about 500,000 square kilometres; that is its largest outline on our maps, and most of it lies in today's Belarus, Ukraine and Lithuania. From 1920 on, it is the modern Polish state: Vilnius and Lviv are Polish on our interwar maps, while Wrocław becomes Polish only on the 1945 map.
