The Han dynasty ruled China for four centuries, from 202 BCE to 220 CE, with a short interruption in the middle. It was so formative that most Chinese people still call themselves the Han, and Chinese characters are known as Han characters.

## From peasant to emperor

The dynasty's founder, Liu Bang, was a minor village official who rose in the revolts that toppled the harsh Qin dynasty. After defeating his rival Xiang Yu, he became emperor in 202 BCE. The Han kept the Qin system of a centralised state run by appointed officials, but softened its laws. Under Emperor Wu (141–87 BCE), Confucianism became the official philosophy, and an imperial academy trained officials in the Confucian classics, the start of a link between learning and government that lasted two thousand years.

## The early Han

Liu Bang, known as Emperor Gaozu, rewarded his supporters with kingdoms, while keeping the west under direct rule. His widow, Empress Lü, dominated the court after his death in 195 BCE, one of the first women to rule China in practice. Under Emperors Wen and Jing, the government kept taxes low and promoted Daoist ideas of non-interference. In 154 BCE, the Rebellion of the Seven States, led by royal princes, was crushed, strengthening the emperor's power.

## Emperor Wu

Emperor Wu, who ruled for 54 years from 141 to 87 BCE, made the Han a great power. He made Confucianism the official ideology, founded an imperial academy in 124 BCE and recruited officials based on recommendation and learning. To fund his wars, he created state monopolies on salt and iron, debated later in the Discourses on Salt and Iron. His armies, led by generals such as Wei Qing and Huo Qubing, drove back the Xiongnu, conquered northern Korea, northern Vietnam and the south-west, and reached Ferghana in Central Asia in search of its famous horses.

## Wang Mang

In 9 CE Wang Mang, a relative of an empress, usurped the throne and declared the Xin dynasty. He attempted radical reforms, nationalising land and freeing slaves, which provoked opposition. A disastrous change in the course of the Yellow River caused floods and famine, and peasant rebels known as the Red Eyebrows rose. Wang Mang was killed in 23 CE, and in 25 CE a member of the Liu family, Emperor Guangwu, restored the Han at Luoyang.

## Society and economy

Han China had a population of about 57 million in the census of 2 CE, one of the earliest such counts. Most people were farmers paying land tax and providing labour and military service. Iron tools and ploughs were widely used, and large landowning families grew powerful. Cities such as Chang'an and Luoyang had markets supervised by officials. Merchants could become rich, but Confucian values looked down on them.

## Science and invention

The Han invented paper; the court eunuch Cai Lun is traditionally credited with improving it in 105 CE. Zhang Heng, in the second century CE, built a seismoscope to detect distant earthquakes and an armillary sphere. Han craftsmen made cast-iron tools, wheelbarrows and the first rudders. Han physicians compiled medical texts, and Han mathematicians wrote the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.

## History and thought

Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, completed around 94 BCE, set the model for Chinese official histories, followed by Ban Gu's History of the Former Han, completed by his sister Ban Zhao, who also wrote advice for women. Buddhism reached China in the first century CE, traditionally under Emperor Ming, and the first Buddhist temple, the White Horse Temple, was founded near Luoyang.

## The Silk Road

Han expansion into Central Asia opened routes for trade with Parthia, India and Rome. Han envoy Gan Ying reached the Persian Gulf in 97 CE, and Chinese silk became a luxury in Rome. Han silks have been found as far away as Egypt.

## The fall

In the second century CE, child emperors, powerful empresses' families and eunuchs fought for control of the court. In 184 the Yellow Turban rebellion, inspired by a Daoist movement, spread across the north. Generals raised armies to suppress it and then turned on each other. The warlord Cao Cao controlled the last emperor, Xian, and his son Cao Pi forced Xian to abdicate in 220, founding the Wei dynasty.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Han from 200 BCE to 200 CE. It is largest on the 100 CE map, at about 4.1 million square kilometres, 95 percent of it in today's China and most of the rest in Vietnam. The sites of Xi'an (then Chang'an), Luoyang's region, Beijing and Guangzhou are Han on our maps throughout, and Pyongyang's site and Hanoi's appear inside it in the later maps. Before the Han our maps show the Zhou-era states; after it, the Jin.
