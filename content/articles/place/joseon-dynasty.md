Joseon was Korea's last and longest-lasting dynasty, ruling for just over five hundred years, from 1392 to 1897. It moved the capital to Hanseong, today's Seoul, shaped Korean society along Confucian lines, and gave Korea its own alphabet.

## Founding

The dynasty was founded by Yi Seong-gye, a general of the previous Goryeo dynasty, who was ordered to attack Ming China in 1388 and instead turned his army back to seize power. He took the throne as King Taejo in 1392. The new state made Neo-Confucianism its official ideology and reduced the wealth and influence of the Buddhist monasteries. Government was in the hands of the yangban, a scholar-official class chosen through civil-service examinations.

## The Confucian state

Joseon's founders, guided by the scholar Jeong Do-jeon, designed a state based on Neo-Confucian principles. The king ruled with the advice of officials chosen through civil-service examinations, and censorate offices could criticise the king. The Gyeongguk daejeon, a legal code completed in 1485, set out the structure of government. Buddhist monasteries lost land and influence, and Confucian rituals, ancestor worship and family rules, such as the importance of the eldest son, were promoted.

## Society

Joseon society was strictly hierarchical. At the top were the yangban, the scholar-aristocracy who held office. Below them were the jungin, technical specialists such as interpreters and doctors; the sangmin, commoners, mostly farmers; and the cheonmin, lowborn people, including slaves, butchers and entertainers. Slavery was widespread, and in some periods a large share of the population were slaves. Women's status declined under Neo-Confucian rules.

## Sejong's achievements

King Sejong (1418–1450) founded the Hall of Worthies, a royal research institute. In 1443 his scholars created Hangul, a phonetic alphabet of 28 letters, designed so that ordinary people could learn to read. It was promulgated in 1446 in a document called Hunminjeongeum, "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people". Many yangban scholars resisted it, preferring Chinese characters, and Hangul did not become widely used until the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Sejong's court also invented rain gauges and water clocks.

## The Imjin War

In 1592, the Japanese ruler Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded Korea with about 150,000 troops, intending to conquer China. Japanese forces took Seoul within weeks. Admiral Yi Sun-sin, using turtle ships covered with iron spikes, won a series of naval victories that cut Japanese supply lines; at Myeongnyang in 1597, with thirteen ships, he defeated a much larger fleet. With help from Ming China and guerrilla forces, the Japanese were driven out by 1598, after Hideyoshi's death. Yi was killed in the last battle. The war devastated Korea.

## The Manchu invasions

The Manchus invaded in 1627 and 1636. King Injo surrendered at Samjeondo in 1637, bowing to the Qing emperor, and Joseon became a Qing tributary. Joseon scholars continued to regard the Ming as the true China.

## Later Joseon

In the eighteenth century, kings Yeongjo and Jeongjo promoted reform and learning. The Silhak, or "practical learning", movement studied agriculture, science and administration. Jeongjo built the Hwaseong Fortress at Suwon. In the nineteenth century, powerful in-law families dominated the court, and peasant revolts and the Donghak movement, a new religion, challenged the order.

## The opening of Korea

The regent Heungseon Daewongun persecuted Catholics and fought off French and American expeditions in 1866 and 1871. In 1876 Japan forced Korea to open its ports. After the Donghak Peasant Revolution of 1894 and the Sino-Japanese War, and the murder of Queen Min by Japanese agents in 1895, King Gojong declared the Korean Empire in 1897.

## The end

In the late nineteenth century, Korea became the object of rivalry between China, Japan and Russia. In 1897 King Gojong proclaimed the Korean Empire, ending the Joseon name, but Japan made Korea a protectorate in 1905 and annexed it in 1910.
