The Xiongnu were a confederation of nomadic peoples who dominated the steppes north of China, centred on what is now Mongolia, from the late third century BCE to the first century CE. They were the first steppe empire to confront a unified China, and their long struggle with the Han dynasty shaped the history of both.

## Life on the steppe

The Xiongnu were pastoral nomads who herded horses, sheep, cattle and camels across the grasslands of Mongolia and Inner Mongolia, moving with the seasons. They lived in felt tents and fought as mounted archers. Chinese sources describe their customs: young men were honoured above the old, warriors were rewarded with plunder, and the chanyu held ceremonies at sacred sites. They had no writing, though some Chinese defectors served them as advisers.

## Organisation

The Xiongnu empire was a confederation of tribes under the chanyu, the supreme ruler, whose title meant something like "vast". The empire was divided into a centre, ruled by the chanyu, and left and right wings, the east and the west, led by the "Wise Kings", usually the chanyu's sons or brothers. Officials were organised in units of ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand. Great assemblies were held three times a year. The ruling clan was the Luanti.

## Modu Chanyu

The Xiongnu empire was founded by Modu, who according to Chinese sources killed his father to become chanyu, or supreme ruler, in 209 BCE, the same years in which the Qin dynasty was collapsing. He defeated the Donghu to the east and the Yuezhi to the west, creating an empire that stretched across the Mongolian plateau and into Central Asia. In 200 BCE he surrounded the first Han emperor at Baideng, and the Han had to agree to a treaty under which China sent silk, grain and princesses as brides to the chanyu.

## The heqin system

After Modu's victory over the first Han emperor at Baideng in 200 BCE, the Han adopted the heqin policy, "harmonious kinship": the Han sent princesses, or women given that title, to marry the chanyu, and paid annual gifts of silk, wine, grain and other goods, in return for peace. The policy did not prevent raids, and Chinese officials criticised it as humiliating.

## War with Emperor Wu

Emperor Wu of Han ended the heqin policy in 133 BCE and launched massive campaigns. The generals Wei Qing and Huo Qubing won major victories in 127, 121 and 119 BCE, pushing deep into Mongolia. The Han took the Hexi corridor, settled it with colonists and built walls and watchtowers. The Han also sought allies, such as the Wusun, and cut the Xiongnu off from the oasis states of the Tarim basin. The wars exhausted both sides.

## Division

In 57 BCE, the Xiongnu split between rival chanyus. One, Huhanye, submitted to the Han in 51 BCE, visiting the Han court. The other, Zhizhi, moved west and was killed by a Han expedition in Central Asia in 36 BCE. The beauty Wang Zhaojun, a Han palace lady, married Huhanye in 33 BCE and became a famous figure in Chinese literature. In 48 CE, the Xiongnu split into a southern group, which settled inside the Han frontier, and a northern group, which the Han and its allies defeated around 89–91 CE.

## Archaeology

Xiongnu tombs, such as those at Noin-Ula and Gol Mod in Mongolia, contain Chinese silks, lacquerware and bronze mirrors, Greco-Bactrian textiles and gold objects, showing their contacts across Eurasia. Large walled settlements, such as Ivolga in Buryatia, show that some Xiongnu farmed and lived in towns.

## Legacy

Some southern Xiongnu later founded states in northern China, such as the Han Zhao, which sacked Luoyang in 311 CE. The relationship between the Xiongnu and the Huns of Europe remains debated. The Xiongnu set the pattern for later steppe empires, such as the Turks and Mongols, in their relations with China.
