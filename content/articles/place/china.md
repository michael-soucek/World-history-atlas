The People's Republic of China was proclaimed by Mao Zedong in Beijing on 1 October 1949, after the Communist Party won a long civil war against the Nationalists. China's history as a civilisation goes back thousands of years, through the dynasties covered elsewhere on this site, such as the [Han dynasty](/place/han-dynasty), [Tang dynasty](/place/tang-dynasty), [Ming dynasty](/place/ming-dynasty) and [Qing dynasty](/place/qing-dynasty). This page covers the People's Republic.

## The founding

After the Qing dynasty fell in 1912, China was divided among warlords and then fought over by the Nationalists, the Kuomintang, and the Communists. During the war against Japan from 1937 to 1945, the Communists built their strength in the countryside, and in the civil war that followed they defeated the Nationalists, who retreated to Taiwan. The new government took control of mainland China, Tibet in 1950–1951 and Hainan.

## The early years

The new state carried out land reform, in which landlords were often attacked and killed in public trials; estimates of deaths range from one to several million. It nationalised industry, launched campaigns against "counter-revolutionaries" and fought in the Korean War from 1950 to 1953. With Soviet help, the first five-year plan built heavy industry. In the Hundred Flowers campaign of 1956–1957, Mao invited criticism, then launched the Anti-Rightist Campaign against critics.

## The Great Leap Forward

In 1958 Mao launched the Great Leap Forward, an attempt to industrialise rapidly through mass mobilisation and to collectivise agriculture into huge people's communes. Peasants were sent to make steel in backyard furnaces, harvests were exaggerated, and grain was taken for the cities and exports. The result was the deadliest famine in history; estimates of deaths range from about 15 to 45 million. China also split with the Soviet Union in the early 1960s.

## The Cultural Revolution

In 1966 Mao launched the Cultural Revolution to purge his rivals and revive revolutionary zeal. Young Red Guards attacked teachers, officials and "class enemies", destroyed temples and historical sites, and fought each other. Millions were persecuted, and hundreds of thousands, perhaps more, died. Liu Shaoqi, the head of state, died in custody, and Deng Xiaoping was purged. Millions of young people were sent to the countryside. In 1971 Mao's designated successor, Lin Biao, died in a plane crash while fleeing. In 1972 President Nixon visited China. Mao died in 1976, and the Gang of Four, led by his widow Jiang Qing, was arrested.

## Reform and opening

Deng Xiaoping, who became the leading figure in 1978, launched "reform and opening". Communes were dismantled and farmers allowed to sell surplus crops, special economic zones, such as Shenzhen, attracted foreign investment, and private enterprise grew. The one-child policy, introduced in 1980, restricted births until 2015. In spring 1989, students and workers protested in Tiananmen Square and other cities for political reform; on 3–4 June, the army cleared the square, killing hundreds or more.

## A rising power

Since the 1990s, China's economy has grown at extraordinary rates, making it the world's largest manufacturer and exporter and second-largest economy, and lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty. Hong Kong returned in 1997 and Macau in 1999. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, hosted the Olympics in 2008, and built high-speed railways, a space programme and the Belt and Road Initiative. Xi Jinping, leader since 2012, has strengthened the party's control and removed presidential term limits in 2018. China's treatment of Uyghurs in Xinjiang, its policies in Tibet and Hong Kong, and tensions over Taiwan and the South China Sea have drawn international criticism.
