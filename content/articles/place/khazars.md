The Khazars were a semi-nomadic Turkic people who built a powerful state on the steppes between the Black Sea and the Caspian from the seventh to the tenth century. Their khaganate controlled the trade routes linking the Baltic and Scandinavia with Byzantium, the Islamic world and Central Asia, and its ruling class is famous for having adopted Judaism.

## Origins and rise

The Khazars were a Turkic people who emerged from the Western Turkic Khaganate in the seventh century, possibly ruled by members of the Ashina clan. They defeated the Bulgars, some of whom moved west to found Bulgaria, and took control of the steppes between the Caspian and the Black Sea, the North Caucasus and Crimea. The Khazars allied with the Byzantine emperor Heraclius against Persia in the 620s.

## Wars with the Arabs

From the 640s to the 730s, the Khazars fought the Arab caliphate for the Caucasus in the Arab-Khazar wars. The Khazars raided Armenia and Azerbaijan, and the Arabs pushed north through Derbent. In 737 the Umayyad general Marwan ibn Muhammad invaded deep into Khazaria and forced the khagan to accept Islam, but the conversion did not last. The wars stabilised the frontier along the Caucasus, and historians suggest that the Khazars helped prevent Arab expansion into Eastern Europe.

## Government

The Khazars had a system of dual kingship. The khagan was a sacred ruler with religious functions, kept largely secluded, and a second ruler, the bek, commanded the army and ran the government. According to Arab writers, the khagan's reign was limited, and he could be killed if disasters struck. The Khazars ruled tributary peoples, including Volga Bulgars, Burtas, Magyars and some East Slavic tribes.

## Atil and trade

The Khazar capital, Atil, on the lower Volga, was a city of tents and wooden houses with a brick palace for the khagan. It had separate judges for Jews, Christians, Muslims and pagans, and mosques, churches and synagogues. The Khazars controlled trade along the Volga and Don rivers, between Scandinavia and the Baltic, the Islamic world, Byzantium and Central Asia, taxing goods such as furs, honey, wax and slaves. The fortress of Sarkel, on the Don, was built with Byzantine help in the 830s.

## Judaism

The conversion of the Khazar elite to Judaism, probably in the eighth or early ninth century, is attested by Arab, Byzantine, Hebrew and Rus' sources. The Khazar Correspondence, letters between the Spanish Jewish statesman Hasdai ibn Shaprut and King Joseph of Khazaria in the tenth century, describes the conversion of King Bulan after a debate among representatives of Judaism, Christianity and Islam. The extent of conversion among ordinary Khazars is unknown. Coins inscribed "Moses is the messenger of God" were found in Sweden. The Jewish philosopher Judah Halevi's book The Kuzari, written around 1140, used the story of the conversion as a framework.

## Fall

In 965, Prince Sviatoslav of Kyiv attacked Khazaria, took Sarkel and later destroyed Atil. The Khazar state collapsed, and Rus', Pechenegs and Oghuz took its lands. Khazar communities survived for some time in Crimea and the Caucasus.

## The Khazar theory

In the twentieth century, some writers claimed that Ashkenazi Jews descend mostly from Khazars. Most historians and genetic studies reject this, finding that Ashkenazi Jews have mainly Middle Eastern and European ancestry. The theory has sometimes been used to promote antisemitic claims.
