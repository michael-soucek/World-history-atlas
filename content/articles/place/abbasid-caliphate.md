The Abbasid Caliphate was the third Islamic caliphate and the longest-lived. The Abbasids, descendants of the Prophet Muhammad's uncle al-Abbas, overthrew the Umayyads in 750 with a revolt that began in Khurasan, in north-eastern Iran. They held the title of caliph for over five centuries, though for much of that time their real power was far smaller than their prestige.

## The revolution

The Abbasid movement grew out of opposition to the Umayyads, promising rule by a member of the Prophet's family. Its propaganda was spread secretly in Khurasan, where many Arab settlers and Persian converts felt neglected. Abu Muslim, a mysterious organiser, raised the revolt in 747, and Abbasid armies swept west. In 749 Abu al-Abbas, a descendant of Muhammad's uncle, was proclaimed caliph in Kufa, taking the title al-Saffah. Many Shia supporters were disappointed that power went to the Abbasids rather than Ali's descendants.

## The caliphal court

The Abbasids moved the centre of the caliphate east to Iraq, closer to Persia, and adopted much of Sasanian court ceremony: the caliph was surrounded by guards and ritual, distant from his subjects. A chief minister, the vizier, headed the administration, which was divided into departments, diwans. Persian families, such as the Barmakids, served as viziers until Harun al-Rashid destroyed them in 803. Non-Arab Muslims rose to high positions, and the empire became more cosmopolitan.

## The Round City

Al-Mansur's Baghdad, built between 762 and 766, was a perfectly circular walled city about two kilometres across, with four gates, and the caliph's palace and the great mosque at its centre. Markets and suburbs soon spread outside, and Baghdad may have had several hundred thousand people. It was a hub of trade linking the Persian Gulf, India and China with the Mediterranean.

## Learning and science

The Abbasid period saw the flowering of Islamic scholarship. Grammarians, jurists and theologians developed the Islamic sciences; the four Sunni schools of law took shape, and al-Bukhari and others collected the hadith. Translators turned Greek works of philosophy, medicine and science into Arabic, and scholars such as al-Kindi, al-Razi and al-Khwarizmi built on them. Paper mills spread from Samarkand to Baghdad in the late eighth century. Literature flourished, with poets such as Abu Nuwas and prose writers such as al-Jahiz.

## Civil war and the Mihna

After Harun's death, his sons al-Amin and al-Mamun fought a civil war, and Baghdad suffered a long siege. Al-Mamun won in 813 and supported scholarship, but he also launched the Mihna, an inquisition requiring officials and scholars to accept the doctrine that the Quran was created. The scholar Ahmad ibn Hanbal refused and was flogged; he became a hero to traditionalists, and the Mihna was abandoned in 848.

## Turks and Samarra

Al-Mutasim, from 833, built an army of Turkish slave soldiers, mamluks, and moved the capital to a new city, Samarra, north of Baghdad, where its spiral minaret still stands. The Turkish commanders soon controlled the caliphs, murdering several of them during the "anarchy at Samarra" in the 860s. The Zanj rebellion of enslaved East Africans working in the marshes of southern Iraq, from 869 to 883, devastated the region.

## A shrinking caliphate

The empire was too large to govern directly. Spain never accepted the Abbasids, North Africa and Egypt drifted away, and in the east governors founded hereditary dynasties. The caliphs came to depend on Turkish slave soldiers, who soon controlled them. From 945 the Persian Buyids, and from 1055 the Seljuk Turks, ruled Baghdad in the caliph's name. The Fatimids of Cairo even set up a rival caliphate in 909.

## The end

In 1258 a Mongol army under Hülegü took Baghdad, sacked it and executed the caliph al-Mustasim. A branch of the family was installed as ceremonial caliphs in Mamluk Cairo, where it survived until the Ottoman conquest of 1517. On our 1279 map Baghdad belongs to the Ilkhanate.

## Legacy

The Abbasid era is remembered as a golden age of Islamic civilisation, celebrated in the stories of the Thousand and One Nights, set in the Baghdad of Harun al-Rashid. Its legal, theological and scientific achievements shaped the Islamic world for centuries.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Abbasid Caliphate on the 800, 900 and 1200 maps. It is largest on the 800 map at about 8.4 million square kilometres, stretching from Libya to Pakistan and down most of the Arabian peninsula, which accounts for nearly a quarter of the area. Baghdad, Damascus, Jerusalem, Cairo, Mecca and Samarkand all fall inside it at that date. By 1200 our maps show only the much smaller state the caliphs ruled around Baghdad, where they had regained some real power.
