The Ghaznavid Empire was founded by Turkish slave soldiers who rose to rule an empire stretching from eastern Iran to northern India. Based at Ghazni, in what is now Afghanistan, it reached its height under Mahmud of Ghazni in the early eleventh century and is remembered both for its raids into India and for its patronage of Persian culture.

## Founding

Alp Tigin, a Turkish slave who became a general of the Samanid Empire, seized Ghazni in 962. His former slave and son-in-law Sabuktigin took power there in 977 and founded the dynasty, expanding into Khurasan and towards India.

## Ghazni

Ghazni, in eastern Afghanistan, on the route between Kabul and Kandahar, became the capital of the Ghaznavids. Under Mahmud it grew into a magnificent city, filled with palaces, mosques and gardens built with the wealth of the Indian campaigns. The minarets of Masud III and Bahram Shah, built in the twelfth century, still stand. The city was later sacked by the Ghurids in 1150 and by the Mongols.

## Mahmud's Indian campaigns

Mahmud, sultan from 998 to 1030, led around seventeen expeditions into northern India between 1000 and 1027. He defeated the Hindu Shahi kings of the Punjab, took Lahore, and raided cities such as Nagarkot, Thanesar, Mathura and Kannauj, carrying off enormous treasure. In 1025 he attacked the temple of Somnath, in Gujarat, and broke its idol. Persian historians celebrated him as a holy warrior; in modern India, Somnath became a symbol of destruction. Historians debate the balance between religious motives and the pursuit of plunder.

## A Persian court

Although of Turkish slave origin, the Ghaznavids were patrons of Persian culture. Mahmud's court gathered poets, such as Unsuri and Farrukhi. Ferdowsi completed the Shahnameh around 1010 and, according to tradition, dedicated it to Mahmud. The historian Bayhaqi wrote a detailed history of Masud's reign. The scholar al-Biruni, brought to Ghazni after Mahmud conquered Khwarazm, accompanied campaigns to India, learned Sanskrit and wrote the Kitab al-Hind, a study of Indian religion, science and society.

## The army

The Ghaznavid army included Turkish slave soldiers, ghulams, Indian troops, Afghans and Arabs, and used war elephants captured in India. Administration followed the Samanid model, with Persian officials.

## The loss of Khurasan

Mahmud's son Masud I faced the Seljuk Turks, who entered Khurasan in the 1030s. At Dandanaqan in 1040, near Merv, the Seljuks defeated the Ghaznavid army, which was exhausted by the desert. Masud fled to India and was murdered by his own troops in 1041. The Ghaznavids lost Iran and Central Asia to the Seljuks.

## The later Ghaznavids

The later Ghaznavids ruled eastern Afghanistan and the Punjab. Ibrahim, who ruled from 1059 to 1099, made peace with the Seljuks and consolidated the state. Lahore became a centre of Persian culture; the poet Masud Saad Salman wrote there, and the Sufi saint Ali Hujwiri, known as Data Ganj Bakhsh, whose shrine in Lahore is a major pilgrimage site, wrote one of the earliest Persian treatises on Sufism.

## The end

The Ghurids, from the mountains of central Afghanistan, rose in the twelfth century. Ala al-Din Husayn, called "the world burner", sacked Ghazni for seven days in 1150. The Ghaznavids moved to Lahore, which Muhammad of Ghor took in 1186, capturing the last Ghaznavid, Khusrau Malik. The Ghurid conquests opened the way to the Delhi Sultanate.

## On our maps

Our maps show the Ghaznavid Empire on the 1000, 1100 and 1200 maps. Its largest outline, about 1.2 million square kilometres on the 1200 map, is half in today's Afghanistan, with the rest in Turkmenistan, Iran and Pakistan. As the dynasty ended in 1186, that last outline is closer to the Ghurid state that replaced it. Kabul lies inside it on all three maps.
