Elam was an ancient civilisation in what is now south-western Iran, centred on the lowlands of Khuzestan and the highlands around Anshan, near modern Shiraz. For more than two thousand years it was the eastern neighbour, rival and sometimes conqueror of the Mesopotamian states, and its capital, Susa, was one of the great cities of the ancient Near East.

## Susa and the Iranian highlands

Elam united two very different regions: the lowland plain of Susiana, in today's Khuzestan, an extension of the Mesopotamian plain watered by the Karun and Karkheh rivers, and the mountain valleys of the Zagros and Fars, around Anshan, near modern Shiraz. Susa was settled around 4200 BCE, and its early inhabitants made fine painted pottery. The highlands supplied timber, stone, metals and other resources sought by Mesopotamia.

## Proto-Elamite writing

Around 3100 BCE, scribes at Susa developed a writing system now called Proto-Elamite, used mainly for administrative records on clay tablets. Tablets have been found across the Iranian plateau, as far east as Shahr-e Sukhteh. Proto-Elamite remains largely undeciphered. Later, a script called Linear Elamite was used, which scholars have recently made progress in deciphering. Most later Elamite texts used cuneiform.

## Rival of Mesopotamia

The kings of Akkad, such as Sargon and Naram-Sin, conquered Susa, and the Third Dynasty of Ur controlled it for a time. Around 2004 BCE, Elamites and their allies sacked Ur and took its last king, Ibbi-Sin, captive, an event mourned in the Lament for Ur. In the early second millennium, the Sukkalmah dynasty ruled Elam, and Elamite kings intervened in Mesopotamian politics until Hammurabi of Babylon defeated them.

## The Middle Elamite period

The Middle Elamite period, from about 1500 to 1100 BCE, was Elam's golden age. King Untash-Napirisha built a new religious city, Dur-Untash, at Chogha Zanbil, with a great ziggurat dedicated to the god Inshushinak, which still stands. In the twelfth century BCE, King Shutruk-Nakhunte invaded Babylonia, ended the Kassite dynasty and carried off monuments, including the law code of Hammurabi, the victory stele of Naram-Sin and the statue of Marduk. His son Kutir-Nahhunte continued the conquests. Around 1100 BCE, Nebuchadnezzar I of Babylon defeated Elam and recovered Marduk's statue.

## Religion and culture

Elamite gods included Inshushinak, protector of Susa; Humban, a great god; and the goddess Kiririsha. Elamites built temples and ziggurats, and made fine metalwork, such as the bronze statue of Queen Napir-Asu, weighing over 1,700 kilograms. The Elamite language is an isolate, unrelated to any known language. Royal succession sometimes passed from brother to brother.

## Destruction and survival

In the Neo-Elamite period, Elam allied with Babylonian rebels against Assyria. Ashurbanipal's armies invaded, defeated the Elamite king Teumman at the Ulai river around 653 BCE, depicted in Assyrian palace reliefs, and sacked Susa around 647 BCE, desecrating royal tombs and sowing salt. Elam survived in weakened form until the rise of Persia. Under the Achaemenids, Susa was a royal capital, Darius built a palace there, and Elamite was used in administration, as the Persepolis tablets show.

## On our maps

Our maps show Elam on every map from 5000 BCE to 700 BCE, entirely within today's Iran. Its largest outline, about 410,000 square kilometres on the 1500 BCE map, stretches over much of western and south-western Iran. The earliest maps use the name for the region's cultures before any Elamite state existed. On the 500 BCE map the region is part of the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire).
