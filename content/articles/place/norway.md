Norway emerged as a kingdom in the late ninth century, when Harald Fairhair is said to have united the petty kingdoms along its coast. Norwegian Vikings raided and settled across the North Atlantic, and the medieval kingdom ruled Iceland, Greenland and the islands north of Scotland.

## The Viking kingdom

Harald Fairhair's victory at Hafrsfjord, traditionally dated around 872, is said to have united Norway, though his power probably covered only the west. According to the sagas, his harsh rule drove many chieftains to settle in Iceland. Norwegian Vikings settled in Shetland, Orkney, the Hebrides, the Isle of Man, Ireland, where they founded Dublin, Iceland, Greenland and, briefly, North America. The Oseberg and Gokstad ship burials, found near Oslo, show the skill of Viking shipbuilders.

## Christian kings

Olaf Tryggvason, king from 995 to 1000, and Olaf Haraldsson, king from 1015 to 1028, forced the conversion of Norway to Christianity, sometimes violently. Olaf Haraldsson was killed at the Battle of Stiklestad in 1030 fighting rebel farmers allied with Cnut, but was soon revered as a saint, Saint Olaf, and his shrine at Nidaros, Trondheim, became a great pilgrimage site. Harald Hardrada, king from 1046, was killed at Stamford Bridge in England in 1066.

## The medieval kingdom

After a century of civil wars, Haakon IV, who ruled from 1217 to 1263, brought stability and expanded the realm to include Iceland and Greenland. His son Magnus the Law-Mender issued a national law code in 1274. Stave churches, wooden churches with carved decorations, such as those at Urnes and Borgund, date from this period. Norwegian trade, especially in dried fish from the Lofoten Islands, was dominated by the Hanseatic League at Bergen.

## Under Denmark

After the Black Death killed perhaps half the population in 1349–1350, Norway was weakened. From 1380 it shared a king with Denmark, and after 1536 it was governed from Copenhagen. Danish became the written language, and many officials were Danish. Norway kept its own laws. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the timber, fishing and shipping trades grew.

## 1814

When Denmark was forced to cede Norway to Sweden in 1814, Norwegian leaders met at Eidsvoll and adopted a liberal constitution on 17 May 1814, one of the most democratic of its time. After a short war, Norway entered a union with Sweden under the Swedish king, but kept its constitution and parliament, the Storting.

## National revival

In the nineteenth century, Norway developed a strong national identity. Writers such as Henrik Ibsen and Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson, the composer Edvard Grieg and the painter Edvard Munch, whose The Scream became world-famous, gained international renown. The language question led to two written standards, Bokmål and Nynorsk. Polar explorers Fridtjof Nansen and Roald Amundsen, who reached the South Pole first in 1911, became national heroes.

## Independence and war

Norway peacefully dissolved the union with Sweden in 1905. Women gained the vote in 1913. Germany invaded on 9 April 1940; the king and government escaped to London, while Vidkun Quisling led a collaborationist government, his name becoming a word for traitor. The Norwegian resistance sabotaged the German heavy water plant at Vemork. After the war, Norway joined NATO in 1949.

## Modern Norway

The discovery of the Ekofisk oil field in 1969 made Norway one of the richest countries in the world. Its sovereign wealth fund, the Government Pension Fund Global, invests oil revenue for future generations. In 2011 a far-right terrorist killed 77 people in Oslo and on Utøya island.
