Goryeo ruled Korea for almost five centuries, from 918 to 1392, and gave the country its name in Western languages: "Korea" comes from Goryeo, through Arab and later European traders. It is remembered for its Buddhist culture, its celadon pottery, early printing and its long resistance to the Mongols.

## Unification

As the kingdom of Silla declined in the late ninth century, rebels founded rival states: Later Baekje in the south-west, led by Gyeon Hwon, and Taebong, or Later Goguryeo, in the north, led by Gung Ye. In 918 Gung Ye's generals overthrew him and made Wang Geon king. Wang Geon, from a merchant family of Songak, now Kaesong, named his state Goryeo, after the ancient kingdom of Goguryeo, and made Kaesong his capital. Silla surrendered peacefully in 935, and Wang Geon defeated Later Baekje in 936. He welcomed refugees from Balhae, the northern kingdom destroyed by the Khitans, and married women from many powerful families to bind them to him.

## Government

Goryeo adopted Chinese-style institutions, with central government departments, and introduced civil-service examinations in 958 under King Gwangjong, who also freed many slaves and weakened the great families. Power was shared between the king and aristocratic families, which dominated government through the examinations and marriage. Buddhism was the state religion, and Confucianism guided government.

## Wars with the Khitans and Jurchens

The Khitan Liao dynasty invaded in 993, 1010 and 1018. The diplomat Seo Hui negotiated the first withdrawal, and in 1019 General Gang Gam-chan won a great victory at Gwiju. Goryeo built a long wall, the Cheolli Jangseong, along its northern frontier. In the twelfth century, Goryeo fought and later submitted to the Jurchen Jin dynasty.

## Military rule

In 1170 military officers, angered by their treatment by civilian officials, staged a coup and massacred many officials. A period of military dictatorship followed, and from 1196 the Choe family ruled as military dictators for four generations, with kings as figureheads.

## The Mongols

The Mongols invaded in 1231. The Choe moved the court to Ganghwa Island, off the west coast, in 1232, where it held out for nearly thirty years, protected by the sea, while the Mongols devastated the mainland in repeated invasions. The Tripitaka Koreana, the Buddhist canon carved on more than 80,000 wooden blocks between 1237 and 1248, was made to seek Buddha's protection. After the Choe family fell in 1258, Goryeo submitted to the Mongols in 1259. Some military units, the Sambyeolcho, fought on until 1273. For about a century, Goryeo kings married Mongol princesses and spent time at the Yuan court, and Goryeo supplied ships and troops for Kublai's invasions of Japan.

## Culture

Goryeo celadon, with its soft blue-green glaze and inlaid designs, sanggam, of cranes and clouds, was admired even in China. Goryeo printers produced the Jikji, a Buddhist text printed with movable metal type in 1377, now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, the oldest surviving book printed this way. Kim Busik compiled the Samguk Sagi, a history of the Three Kingdoms, in 1145.

## Fall

As the Yuan declined, King Gongmin, from 1351, restored independence and attacked pro-Mongol families. Goryeo suffered raids by Red Turban rebels and Japanese pirates, wokou. In 1388, ordered to attack Ming China at Liaodong, General Yi Seong-gye turned his army back from Wihwa Island and seized power. In 1392 he founded the Joseon dynasty.

## On our maps

Our maps show Goryeo on the 1000, 1100 and 1200 maps, with an outline of up to about 190,000 square kilometres split almost evenly between today's South and North Korea. Seoul's site and Pyongyang lie inside it. On the 1279 map Korea is drawn under the Mongol Great Khanate; by 1400, as [Joseon](/place/joseon-dynasty).
