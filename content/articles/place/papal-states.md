For over a thousand years the popes were not only the leaders of the Catholic Church but also kings of a state in central Italy. The Papal States, officially the State of the Church, existed from the eighth century until 1870, and survive today in miniature as Vatican City.

## Origins

From the time of Constantine, the bishops of Rome acquired land around the city through donations. In the eighth century, threatened by the Lombards and receiving little help from Byzantium, the popes turned to the Franks. In 756 Pippin the Short, king of the Franks, defeated the Lombards and gave the pope the lands he had taken from them, around Rome and Ravenna. The event is known as the Donation of Pippin. A forged document, the Donation of Constantine, was later used to claim even wider papal authority; the scholar Lorenzo Valla showed it was a forgery in 1440.

## Governing the papal lands

The pope ruled the Papal States as a monarch, through cardinals and officials known as legates and governors. The territories were divided into provinces such as the Patrimony of Saint Peter around Rome, the Duchy of Spoleto, the March of Ancona, the Romagna and, later, the Legations of Bologna and Ferrara. Many cities, such as Bologna and Perugia, had strong traditions of self-government and were often ruled in practice by local families, such as the Bentivoglio of Bologna and the Baglioni of Perugia.

## Avignon

In 1309, Pope Clement V, a Frenchman, moved the papal court to Avignon in southern France, where it remained for nearly seventy years, under strong French influence. During this absence, the Papal States fell into disorder. The Roman popular leader Cola di Rienzo tried to revive the ancient Roman Republic in 1347, and the Spanish cardinal Gil Álvarez Carrillo de Albornoz restored papal authority in the 1350s and 1360s, issuing a constitution for the papal lands. Pope Gregory XI returned to Rome in 1377.

## The Great Schism

After Gregory's death in 1378, a disputed election led to the Western Schism, with rival popes in Rome and Avignon, and later a third at Pisa. The schism was ended by the Council of Constance in 1417, with the election of Martin V, a member of the Roman Colonna family, who restored order in the papal lands.

## Renaissance popes

Renaissance popes ruled as Italian princes and patrons. Sixtus IV built the Sistine Chapel and founded the Vatican Library. Alexander VI, of the Borgia family, and his son Cesare Borgia tried to bring the Romagna under their control, inspiring parts of Machiavelli's The Prince. Julius II, the warrior pope, led his own armies, recovered Bologna and Perugia, and commissioned Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and the new St Peter's. Leo X, a Medici, spent lavishly, contributing to the indulgence campaign that provoked Luther.

## The sack of Rome and after

In 1527 the unpaid troops of Emperor Charles V sacked Rome, a shock that ended the high Renaissance in the city. In the following centuries, the papacy led the Counter-Reformation. Popes such as Sixtus V rebuilt Rome with new streets, fountains and obelisks, and the Baroque architect Bernini designed St Peter's Square in the seventeenth century. The Papal States added Ferrara in 1598 and Urbino in 1631.

## Revolution and restoration

French revolutionary armies occupied the Papal States in 1798 and declared a Roman Republic. Pius VI was taken prisoner and died in France. Napoleon annexed the papal lands to France in 1809, and Pius VII was held prisoner. The Congress of Vienna restored them in 1815, but the papal government was widely seen as backward and repressive. In 1848 revolutionaries proclaimed another Roman Republic, led by Giuseppe Mazzini, and defended by Garibaldi, until French troops restored Pius IX in 1849.

## The Roman Question

After Rome became part of Italy in 1870, Pius IX declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican" and refused to recognise the Italian state. The dispute, known as the Roman Question, was resolved by the Lateran Treaty of 1929 between the Holy See and Mussolini's government, which created the independent State of Vatican City, the smallest country in the world.

## On our maps

Our maps draw the Papal States from 800 to 1815. At its largest, on the 1492 map, the outline covers about 44,000 square kilometres, all in today's Italy. Rome is papal on our maps from 1100 to the 1870s. Napoleon annexed the state to France in 1809, and the Congress of Vienna restored it in 1815.
