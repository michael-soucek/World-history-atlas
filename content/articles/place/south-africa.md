South Africa was formed in 1910 as the Union of South Africa, a self-governing British dominion bringing together four colonies: the Cape, Natal, the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony. Its twentieth-century history was dominated by racial segregation and apartheid, and by the struggle that ended them in 1994.

## Before the Union

Khoisan peoples have lived in southern Africa for tens of thousands of years, and their ancestors left rock art and some of the earliest evidence of modern human behaviour, such as at Blombos Cave. Bantu-speaking farmers settled much of the east from the first millennium CE, and states such as Mapungubwe flourished. The Dutch founded Cape Town in 1652, and Dutch-speaking settlers, later called Afrikaners, spread inland. Britain took the Cape in 1806. In the Great Trek of the 1830s, Afrikaners moved inland and founded the Orange Free State and the South African Republic, the Transvaal. The Zulu, Xhosa, Basotho, Pedi and others resisted European expansion.

## Diamonds, gold and war

Diamonds were found at Kimberley in 1867 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, where Johannesburg grew rapidly. Mining depended on Black migrant labour, housed in compounds and controlled by pass laws. British ambitions to control the gold fields led to the South African War of 1899–1902, in which Britain defeated the Afrikaner republics, using a scorched-earth policy and concentration camps in which about 26,000 Afrikaner women and children and at least 20,000 Black Africans died.

## Segregation

The Union of 1910 excluded the Black majority from political power, except for a limited vote in the Cape. The Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted Black ownership of land to about 7 percent, later 13 percent, of the country. The African National Congress, founded in 1912, opposed these laws. Mahatma Gandhi lived in South Africa from 1893 to 1914, leading Indian resistance to discrimination.

## Apartheid

In 1948 the National Party, representing Afrikaner nationalism, won the election and introduced apartheid, "separateness". The Population Registration Act classified everyone by race; the Group Areas Act assigned separate residential areas; mixed marriages were banned; and the Bantu Education Act gave Black children an inferior education. Millions of people were forcibly removed, and ten "homelands", Bantustans, were created for Black South Africans, some declared nominally independent. South Africa became a republic in 1961 and left the Commonwealth.

## Resistance

The ANC's Defiance Campaign of 1952 and the Freedom Charter of 1955 called for a non-racial democracy. On 21 March 1960, police killed 69 people at Sharpeville, protesting against pass laws. The ANC and the Pan Africanist Congress were banned, and the ANC formed an armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe. Nelson Mandela and others were sentenced to life imprisonment in 1964. On 16 June 1976, police fired on students in Soweto protesting against education in Afrikaans, killing hundreds over the following months. The activist Steve Biko died in police custody in 1977. In the 1980s, the United Democratic Front, trade unions, international sanctions and boycotts put the regime under growing pressure, and a state of emergency was declared.

## Transition

President F. W. de Klerk unbanned the ANC and released Mandela on 11 February 1990. Negotiations, amid political violence, led to South Africa's first democratic elections on 27 April 1994. Mandela became president. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, heard testimony about apartheid-era abuses. The 1996 constitution is one of the most progressive in the world. South Africa still faces high inequality, unemployment and crime, and the HIV/AIDS epidemic hit it hard.
