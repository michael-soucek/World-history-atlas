Herders who buried their dead in pits beneath earth mounds left the remains archaeologists call the Yamnaya culture, which spread across the grasslands between the Southern Bug, the Dniester and the Ural rivers from around 3300 to 2600 BCE. Also known as the Pit Grave or Ochre Grave culture, it sits at the centre of one of the most influential, and most argued-over, theories in prehistory: that its people spoke an early form of Proto-Indo-European.

## Discovery and dating

The culture was first identified by the Russian archaeologist Vasily Gorodtsov, who excavated near the Donets River in 1901–1903 and separated it from the later Catacomb and Srubnaya cultures of the same region. Its name comes from the Russian word for a pit. Its dates come from excavated sites and their order relative to neighbouring cultures; the span is usually given as about 3300–2600 BCE, with some authors extending it towards 2500 BCE.

## Origins

Where Yamnaya came from is still debated. Proposed forerunners include the Khvalynsk culture of the middle Volga, the Repin culture of the Don, and the Sredny Stog culture further west. The archaeologist David Anthony placed its emergence in the Don–Volga region around 3400 BCE, followed by a rapid spread across the steppe between about 3400 and 3200 BCE. Others, such as Dmytro Telegin, saw Sredny Stog as its foundation, while Asko Parpola linked it to the late Trypillia culture. Pavel Dolukhanov interpreted it as a social change, the rise of mobile chiefdoms among diverse local groups, rather than the arrival of one people.

## Life on the steppe

Yamnaya people were nomadic or semi-nomadic, raising animals, fishing and foraging, with some cultivation along rivers; fortified sites were rare, Mikhailovka being the biggest. They travelled with carts on two wheels and wagons on four, probably drawn by oxen. Whether they rode horses is disputed: some skeletons show wear that may come from riding, but the archaeozoologist William Taylor argues that true horse domestication came later, and recent genetic studies place it after about 2700 BCE. Metalworkers seem to have had special standing, and metal objects can be plentiful in elite graves.

## Burials

The defining trait is the kurgan: a pit grave covered by a mound, the body laid on its back with knees bent and sprinkled with red ochre. Some graves held animal offerings, carved stone stelae showing human figures, or even complete wooden wagons. Kurgan burial may have been reserved for a minority, mostly adult men.

## Language and ancient DNA

Marija Gimbutas identified Yamnaya with late Proto-Indo-European speakers in her Kurgan hypothesis, and Anthony considers the Pontic–Caspian steppe the strongest candidate for that homeland, perhaps excluding the Anatolian branch. Colin Renfrew argued instead for a Near Eastern origin. Ancient-DNA studies since 2015 model Yamnaya people as a mix of eastern European and Caucasus hunter-gatherer ancestry, with later work refining the picture, and find large shares of related "steppe ancestry" in later populations such as Corded Ware, Bell Beaker, Sintashta and Andronovo. The geneticist David Reich sees them as a coherent group that spread many Indo-European languages. Critics such as Leo Klejn note that the most common Yamnaya male lineage is largely missing from later western Europeans, and a 2022 study by Guus Kroonen and colleagues placed the core Indo-European homeland in the western Yamnaya zone around the Dnieper. These remain hypotheses under active debate.

## Successors

In the west Yamnaya was followed by the [Catacomb culture](/place/catacomb-culture), from about 2800 BCE, and in the east by the Poltavka culture, both later replaced by the Srubnaya culture. Far to the east, the [Afanasievo culture](/place/afanasievo-culture) of the Altai was so similar in material culture and genetics that it may stem from a Yamnaya migration or a shared ancestor, and Yamnaya-related ancestry also reached the later [Andronovo culture](/place/andronovo-culture).
