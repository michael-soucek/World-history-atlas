The Aghlabids were an Arab family who governed Ifriqiya, roughly today's Tunisia with eastern Algeria and western Libya, from 800 to 909. Though they ruled as hereditary emirs and were independent in practice, they always acknowledged the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad. From their base around Kairouan they launched the Muslim conquest of Sicily and raids into southern Italy, and their mosques and fortresses are among the oldest Islamic monuments in North Africa.

## A hereditary governorship

After years of turmoil in the province, the caliph Harun al-Rashid in 800 granted Ifriqiya as a hereditary emirate to Ibrahim ibn al-Aghlab, the son of an Arab commander of the Banu Tamim tribe from Khurasan. In return the dynasty paid an annual tribute, reported at 800,000 dirhams, and named the caliph in Friday prayers. Arabs were still a minority among a mostly Berber population; perhaps 100,000 Arabs then lived in the province. Ibrahim built a new palace-capital, al-Abbasiyya, just outside Kairouan, partly to keep his distance from the city's critical religious scholars.

## The army and its revolt

The emirs relied on two forces: the jund, descendants of the Arab conquerors, and regiments of slave soldiers recruited partly to balance them. Under Ziyadat Allah I, who ruled from 817 to 838, the jund rose in 824 and briefly held Tunis and Kairouan. The emir only crushed the revolt in 827, with help from Ibadi Berbers.

## Sicily and Italy

In the same year an appeal from a rebel Byzantine commander, Euphemios, gave the Aghlabids a pretext to invade Sicily, led by the Kairouan judge Asad ibn al-Furat. The conquest advanced slowly from west to east: Palermo fell in 831 and became the Muslim capital of the island, Messina around 842–843, Syracuse in 878 and Taormina in 902. Muslim forces also seized towns on the Italian mainland, including Taranto and Bari, raided towards Rome and took Malta in 870, though many of the warbands in Italy had only loose ties to the emirs. Whether they ever occupied Sardinia is disputed among historians.

## Prosperity and building

The Sicilian wars gave the restless jund an outlet and brought new revenue, and after 827 Ifriqiya enjoyed relative calm. Agriculture, irrigation and trans-Saharan trade grew. The emirs officially followed the Mu'tazilite doctrine of the Abbasid court, which put them at odds with the Maliki scholars who dominated local religious life, but they won them over with appointments, charity and lavish mosque building. Ziyadat Allah I rebuilt the Great Mosque of Kairouan in 836; its minaret is the oldest standing in North Africa and its teak pulpit the oldest surviving minbar. The ribats of Sousse and Monastir and the great reservoirs of Kairouan also date from this period. In 876 Ibrahim II moved the court to a new palace-city, Raqqada.

## The Fatimid challenge

Decline began under Ibrahim II, who ruled from 875 to 902. From 893 the Ismaili missionary Abu Abdallah al-Shi'i won over the Kutama Berbers on behalf of the future Fatimid caliph. While Ibrahim II campaigned in Sicily and Italy, where he died besieging Cosenza in 902, Kutama forces began taking Aghlabid towns. Ziyadat Allah III's armies failed to stop them, and after a decisive defeat at al-Aribus in March 909 he fled to Egypt. Abu Abdallah entered Raqqada on 25 March 909, and within the year the Fatimid Caliphate was proclaimed in Ifriqiya.

## On our maps

Our 800 and 900 maps show the Aghlabid Emirate in Ifriqiya, a vassal of the [Abbasid Caliphate](/place/abbasid-caliphate), with the rival [Idrisid dynasty](/place/idrisid-dynasty) in Morocco and the [Byzantine Empire](/place/byzantine-empire) across the sea. Its founder was appointed by [Harun al-Rashid](/person/harun-al-rashid); its island conquest is covered under [Muslim Sicily](/place/muslim-sicily), and its successor was the [Fatimid Caliphate](/place/fatimid-caliphate). Its heartland is modern [Tunisia](/country/tunisia).
