The Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba stands near the Guadalquivir river in the old centre of [Córdoba](/history-of/cordoba), in Andalusia in southern Spain. Begun in 785 as the Great Mosque of the Umayyad rulers of al-Andalus and enlarged over the next two centuries, it became a Catholic cathedral after the Castilian conquest of 1236, and a Renaissance church was later built into the middle of its forest of columns. With its rows of double-tiered arches, it is one of the landmark buildings of Islamic architecture and strongly shaped the later Moorish style of the western Mediterranean.

## Before the mosque

Traditional accounts say the site once held a Visigothic church of Saint Vincent, which Christians and Muslims shared after the conquest of Hispania until Abd al-Rahman I bought the Christian half and demolished it. This story, first recorded by the tenth-century historian al-Razi, closely echoes accounts of the founding of the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, and its accuracy is disputed. Some archaeologists, such as Pedro Marfil, interpret remains on the site as part of an episcopal complex; others, including Fernando Arce-Sainz, point to the absence of Christian iconography or burials. A long-repeated claim of a Roman temple of Janus here has been dismissed as speculation.

## The Umayyad Great Mosque

Abd al-Rahman I, one of the few members of the [Umayyad](/place/umayyad-caliphate) dynasty to survive the Abbasid takeover of 750, founded an independent emirate in al-Andalus in 756 with Córdoba as its capital. His mosque was built quickly, between about 785 and 787, reusing Roman and Visigothic columns and capitals. Its prayer hall introduced the two-tiered arches that every later extension copied. Its orientation points south rather than towards Mecca, reflecting the Roman street grid and an older western tradition about the direction of prayer.

Successive rulers of the [Umayyad state of Córdoba](/place/umayyad-state-of-cordoba) enlarged it. Abd al-Rahman II lengthened the prayer hall in the 830s and 840s. [Abd al-Rahman III](/person/abd-al-rahman-iii), who proclaimed himself caliph, rebuilt the courtyard and added a minaret about 47 metres tall, completed in 958. His son al-Hakam II extended the hall again from 961 and created its most celebrated feature: a new mihrab, interlaced multifoil arches and ribbed domes, decorated with gold mosaics that, by the account of the chronicler Ibn Idhari, were made with the help of a craftsman sent by the Byzantine emperor. The last and largest extension, by the powerful vizier al-Mansur in 987 and 988, widened the building eastwards and made it the largest mosque in the Muslim world outside Abbasid Iraq.

## From mosque to cathedral

When the caliphate collapsed in the early eleventh century, the mosque was looted during the ensuing civil war. In 1146 Alfonso VII of León and Castile held Córdoba for a short time, held a Mass in the building and, according to Muslim sources, stripped it of treasures. The [Almohad](/place/almohad-caliphate) rulers restored the city from 1162. In 1236, during the [Reconquista](/event/reconquista), Ferdinand III of [Castile](/place/crown-of-castile) captured Córdoba and the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral of the Virgin Mary. For centuries the changes were modest: chapels lined the walls, the minaret became a bell tower, and local Muslim craftsmen maintained the fabric, some under a labour tax unique to the city. A royal chapel in Mudéjar style was finished in 1371.

The largest intervention came in 1523, when Bishop Alonso Manrique began inserting a Renaissance nave and transept into the centre of the prayer hall. The city council opposed it, but the chapter won permission from [Charles V](/person/charles-v-holy-roman-emperor). The king is said to have regretted it on seeing the result, reportedly remarking that something unique in the world had been destroyed for something that could be built anywhere. The work, by Hernán Ruiz I and his successors, ended with a dome finished in 1607, and between 1593 and 1617 the old minaret was encased in a new bell tower.

## Restoration and debate

From the nineteenth century restorers uncovered and repaired Islamic-era features, including the mihrab in 1816, and the building was declared a national monument in 1882. UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site in 1984, extending the listing to the historic centre of Córdoba in 1994. It remains the city's working cathedral. Its status is contested: Muslim groups have asked in vain to pray there, and the cathedral chapter's registration of the building as church property in 2006 prompted a public dispute over its ownership. A fire on 8 August 2025 damaged chapels in al-Mansur's extension, and repairs were planned to last until mid-2026.
