Sutton Hoo is a group of Anglo-Saxon burial mounds on a ridge above the estuary of the River Deben, opposite the town of Woodbridge in Suffolk, eastern England. Its roughly twenty barrows date from the sixth and seventh centuries and were raised for members of the ruling elite of the kingdom of the East Angles. The great ship burial found under Mound 1 in 1939 is one of the richest archaeological discoveries ever made in England, and it sheds light on a period for which written records are very scarce.

## Before the Anglo-Saxons

The name comes from Old English: Sutton means "southern farmstead" and hoo a spur of land shaped like a heel. The ridge was farmed long before the mounds were built. Neolithic farmers cleared woodland there around 3000 BCE, and Bronze Age families later built roundhouses and grew cereals. Cultivation resumed in the Iron Age and continued under the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire), when the area suffered soil loss and was eventually abandoned to scrub.

## A royal cemetery

After Roman rule in Britain ended in the early fifth century, Angles and Saxons from the Continent settled the south-east, and East Anglia, which takes its name from the Angles, became one of several small kingdoms. The Deben was then a busy route inland, and a royal centre may have stood at nearby Rendlesham. Sutton Hoo was reserved for people buried singly with goods marking exceptional wealth or rank. Some were cremated with horses and other animals, their ashes placed in bronze bowls; one young man was buried under Mound 17 with his sword, shield and spears, beside a separate grave for his horse. Mound 2 held a plank-lined chamber covered by a small ship.

The burial under Mound 1 appears to have been the last. A 27-metre oak ship was dragged up from the river into a trench, a timber chamber built amidships and filled with treasures, and an earth mound raised over it. The chamber held a ceremonial helmet, sword and shield, gold and garnet dress fittings of the highest quality, a lyre and silver plate made in the [Byzantine Empire](/place/byzantine-empire). Imported coins narrow the date to between about 610 and 635.

## Who was buried?

No body survived in the acid soil, and early scholars wondered whether the ship was a memorial rather than a grave, but traces of phosphate found in later analysis point to a body having lain there. Since H. M. Chadwick first proposed it in 1940, most scholars have favoured Rædwald, an East Anglian king and overking who died around 624. Others have suggested his sons Eorpwald or Sigeberht, a different king, or a rich non-royal; some historians judge the case for Rædwald unproven. In 2025 the historian Helen Gittos argued that the man was a local warrior who had served Byzantium. The finds are often compared with the world of the poem Beowulf.

Later centuries used the site differently. From perhaps the eighth or ninth century, a gallows seems to have stood on one mound, and the bodies of executed people, some hanged or beheaded, were buried around it. In the Middle Ages part of Mound 1 was cut away for a boundary ditch, and in the sixteenth century treasure seekers dug a pit that narrowly missed the burial chamber.

## Excavation and display

In 1938 the landowner Edith Pretty engaged Basil Brown, a self-taught local archaeologist, to open the mounds. In May 1939 he began on Mound 1 and soon found ship rivets still in place. When the importance of the find became clear, Charles Phillips of Cambridge took charge of excavating the chamber. A treasure trove inquest ruled the finds Pretty's property, and she gave them to the nation; no living donor had ever given the British Museum a larger gift. When the [Second World War](/event/world-war-ii) broke out the objects went into storage and the site became a military training ground.

A British Museum team under Rupert Bruce-Mitford reopened the site from 1965 to 1971, and Martin Carver led a further campaign from 1983 to 1992 that found the Mound 17 burial and the execution graves while leaving much unexcavated for the future. In 2000 work for a new visitor centre revealed a second cemetery nearby. The site now belongs to the National Trust, whose visitor centre opened in 2002, and the main treasures are displayed at the British Museum. Sutton Hoo lies in the [United Kingdom](/country/united-kingdom).
