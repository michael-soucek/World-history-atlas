The Carnac stones form an unusually dense cluster of prehistoric monuments close to the southern coast of Brittany in north-western France, mostly in the commune of Carnac with some in neighbouring La Trinité-sur-Mer. More than 3,000 standing stones cut from local granite, arranged in long rows and accompanied by burial mounds, dolmens and single menhirs, make it the biggest group of its kind anywhere. They were raised during the Neolithic, mostly around 3300 BCE according to the usual dating, though some may go back to about 4500 BCE.

## The builders and the great mounds

The monuments long predate the [Celts](/place/celts); they were the work of the pre-Celtic peoples of Brittany. Exactly who those people were is still debated: Neil Oliver, in a BBC documentary, argued that the rows were raised by Mesolithic hunter-gatherers rather than farmers, which would set them apart from monuments like [Stonehenge](/site/stonehenge).

The oldest major monuments are three huge burial mounds of the mid-fifth millennium BCE, Saint-Michel at Carnac, Tumiac at Arzon and Mané-er-Hroëk at Locmariaquer. Each covered a stone chamber for a single burial, furnished with large polished axeheads, stone arm-rings and jewellery of callaïs, a mixture of variscite and turquoise. Analysis has shown that many of the axes were cut from Alpine jade quarried in Italy and that the callaïs came by sea from south-western Iberia. Saint-Michel, built of some 35,000 cubic metres of earth and stone, is about 125 metres long and 12 metres high; radiocarbon dates place Tumiac between 4790 and 4530 BCE. Because of the effort these burials required, some researchers interpret them as the graves of powerful rulers, even "divine kings", comparable to elite burials of the same age at Varna in Bulgaria and later at [Newgrange](/site/newgrange) in Ireland. Dolmens and passage tombs, such as Kercado, built around 4600 BCE and used for some 3,000 years, are scattered around the area.

## The alignments

The rows fall into three main groups, which may once have formed a single system before stones were taken away. The Ménec alignments are eleven converging rows about 1,165 metres long and 100 metres wide, with the tallest stones, around 4 metres high, at the western end, where there are remains of a stone enclosure. Further east, Kerlescan has 555 stones in 13 lines about 800 metres long, with a circle of 39 stones at its western end; Kermario, between them, takes a similar fan-like form. Together the rows follow a contour line about 20 metres above sea level. What they were for is unknown. Since the nineteenth century writers have proposed links with the solstices, and in the early 1970s the engineer Alexander Thom and his son surveyed the rows in detail and argued for astronomical alignments and a standard unit of length, the "megalithic yard", though his ideas have been challenged.

## Legends and later history

Later peoples reused and reinterpreted the stones. The Tumiac mound is known locally as Caesar's mound, from a legend that [Julius Caesar](/person/julius-caesar) watched his war against the Veneti from it in 56 BCE. A Christian legend held that the rows were pagan soldiers turned to stone while pursuing Pope Cornelius, and Breton tradition credited Merlin with petrifying a Roman legion. In more recent centuries dolmens were turned into sheep pens, henhouses or even ovens, and many stones were removed for roads and building material.

## Study and protection

Scholarly interest grew from the eighteenth century. In 1796 La Tour d'Auvergne attributed the stones to druids, and in 1834 Francis Ronalds and Alexander Blair made the first detailed survey and accurate drawings. The Scottish antiquary James Miln excavated in the 1860s and found that fewer than 700 of the stones were still upright; his young assistant Zacharie Le Rouzic became a respected expert in the region, and the local museum of prehistory bears both their names.

The French state bought and listed the monuments early in the twentieth century to save them from quarrying, but roads, farming changes and mass tourism later caused damage, and work with bulldozers in the 1930s and 1980s moved some stones. Since 1991 the main rows have been fenced and visited mostly by guided tour, a policy that led protesters to occupy the Kermario visitor centre in 2002. In June 2023, 39 menhirs outside the protected area were destroyed to build a shop, prompting a legal complaint. In 2025 UNESCO inscribed the stones, with some 550 monuments across 28 communes of Morbihan, as a World Heritage Site in [France](/country/france).
