The classical age, from about 500 BCE to 500 CE, is the era of the great Eurasian empires and of the ideas that outlived them. At its height, in the second century CE, the Roman and Han empires each governed something like 50 to 60 million people at opposite ends of the continent, linked by chains of merchants who mostly never met. Philosophies and religions formed or spread in these centuries that still guide billions of people, and the languages of the age, Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, classical Chinese and Persian, became the learned languages of whole civilisations.

## Empires from the Mediterranean to China

The age opened with the Persian Empire at its greatest extent. Its attempt to subdue the Greek cities failed in the [Greco-Persian Wars](/event/greco-persian-wars), decisively at Salamis in 480 BCE and Plataea in 479 BCE and left Athens at the peak of its power until its long war with Sparta. In the 330s and 320s BCE [Alexander the Great](/person/alexander-the-great) of Macedon conquered Persia, Egypt and lands as far as the Indus. His generals divided his empire, and in the kingdoms of the [Hellenistic period](/culture/hellenistic-period) Greek became the language of government and learning from Egypt to Afghanistan.

Rome, meanwhile, rose from a city-state to master of Italy, defeated Carthage in three Punic Wars and absorbed the Hellenistic kingdoms one by one. After a century of civil wars the Republic gave way in 27 BCE to rule by emperors, and the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire) reached its widest extent under Trajan in 117 CE. To the east, the Parthians and, from 224 CE, the Sasanians ruled Iran and Mesopotamia and fought Rome for centuries over Armenia and the upper Euphrates.

In India, the [Maurya Empire](/place/maurya-empire), founded around 321 BCE, briefly united most of the subcontinent, and later the Kushans in the north-west and the Guptas, from about 320 CE, presided over flourishing trade and Sanskrit culture. In China, the Warring States ended in 221 BCE when Qin unified the country; the [Han dynasty](/place/han-dynasty) that followed in 206 BCE lasted four centuries, adopted Confucian learning as the basis for training officials and fought the Xiongnu nomads of the northern steppe.

Beyond Eurasia's great empires, Meroë in Nubia and the kingdom of Aksum in Ethiopia traded with Rome and India. In the Americas, Teotihuacan grew into one of the largest cities in the world, the Maya built cities and refined their writing and calendar, and in Peru the Moche and Nazca cultures flourished.

## Ideas, faiths and trade

The classical age inherited from the late ancient world a ferment of thought that historians sometimes call the Axial Age. Greek philosophers from Socrates to the Stoics set out the questions of Western thought, and Hellenistic scholars made lasting advances: Euclid systematised geometry, Archimedes worked on mechanics and mathematics, and Eratosthenes estimated the circumference of the earth. In China the Hundred Schools of Thought, including Confucians, Daoists and Legalists, debated how society should be ordered.

Religions spread with empires and merchants. Ashoka sent Buddhist missions beyond India, and by the first century CE Buddhism had reached China along the trade routes. Christianity began in Roman Judea in the first century, spread through the cities of the empire despite periods of persecution, was legalised by Constantine in 313 and became the state religion by the end of the fourth century. Rabbinic Judaism took shape after the Romans destroyed the Temple in Jerusalem in 70 CE, and Zoroastrianism was the official faith of Sasanian Iran.

The [Silk Road](/culture/silk-road) caravan routes, opened in earnest under the Han in the second century BCE, carried silk westward and horses, glassware and silver east. At sea, sailors learned to use the monsoon winds to cross the Arabian Sea, and Roman ships traded with ports in southern India.

## Why it ends around 500 CE

The great empires broke down within a few centuries of each other. The Han dynasty collapsed in 220 CE, leaving China divided for more than three hundred years. In the west, migrating Germanic peoples and the Huns put pressure on Roman frontiers, and the [fall of Rome](/event/fall-of-rome) in the west was complete when the last western emperor was deposed in 476, although the eastern empire continued from Constantinople. In India, attacks by the Huna, or Hephthalites, contributed to the decline of the Guptas in the late fifth and sixth centuries. These collapses, and the spread of universal religions across old borders, mark the transition to the medieval world.
