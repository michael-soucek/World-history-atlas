The Fall of Constantinople was the capture of the capital of the Byzantine Empire by the Ottoman Empire, under Sultan Mehmed II, on 29 May 1453, after a siege of fifty-three days. It ended the Byzantine, or Eastern Roman, Empire, which had lasted over a thousand years, and made Constantinople the capital of the Ottoman Empire.

## A shrinking empire

By the fifteenth century, the Byzantine Empire, which had once ruled much of the Mediterranean, was reduced to Constantinople and a few territories, such as the Morea, in Greece. The Fourth Crusade had sacked the city in 1204, and the Ottomans had conquered most of Anatolia and the Balkans, surrounding the city. Its population had fallen to perhaps 50,000. The Byzantines sought help from western Europe, offering to unite the Orthodox and Catholic churches, which was unpopular in Constantinople.

## Preparations

Mehmed II, who became sultan in 1451 at nineteen, was determined to take the city. In 1452 he built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the European shore of the Bosporus, opposite an earlier fortress, controlling the strait. He gathered a large army, perhaps 80,000 men, including janissaries, and a fleet. A Hungarian engineer, Orban, cast huge cannons for him, including one firing stones weighing over 500 kilograms. Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos had only about 7,000 defenders, including about 2,000 foreigners, such as Genoese soldiers under Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, a specialist in siege defence, and Venetians.

## The siege

The siege began on 6 April 1453. Constantinople was protected by the sea and by the Theodosian Walls on the landward side, a triple line of walls and a moat that had held off attackers for a thousand years. The Ottoman cannons bombarded the walls daily, and the defenders repaired the damage at night. A great chain blocked the entrance to the Golden Horn, the city's harbour. On 22 April, Mehmed had about seventy ships dragged overland on greased logs from the Bosporus into the Golden Horn, behind the chain. Ottoman miners dug tunnels under the walls, but the defenders countermined. A few ships brought supplies, but no relief army came.

## The final assault

In the early hours of 29 May, Mehmed launched a final assault, in three waves. The defenders held at first, but Giustiniani was badly wounded and was carried away, which demoralised his men. Ottoman troops entered through a small gate, the Kerkoporta, which, according to some accounts, had been left open, and raised their flag. Constantine XI died fighting; his body was never identified. The Ottoman soldiers sacked the city for a day. Thousands were killed, and many people, including those who had sought refuge in the Hagia Sophia, were enslaved.

## Aftermath

Mehmed entered the Hagia Sophia and converted it into a mosque. He made Constantinople his capital, repopulating it with Muslims, Christians and Jews, and appointed a Greek Orthodox patriarch. The remaining Byzantine territories, the Morea and Trebizond, fell by 1461.

## Significance

The fall of Constantinople shocked western Europe and is sometimes used to mark the end of the Middle Ages. Greek scholars who fled to Italy, bringing manuscripts, contributed to the Renaissance. The Ottomans' control of trade routes encouraged Europeans to seek sea routes to Asia. In Greek memory, the date remains a symbol of loss; Russia claimed the role of a "Third Rome".
