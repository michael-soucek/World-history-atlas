The Battle of Tours, also called the Battle of Poitiers, was fought in October 732 between the Franks, led by Charles Martel, and an army of the Umayyad Caliphate, led by Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi, governor of al-Andalus. The Franks won, and Abd al-Rahman was killed. The battle has long been seen as a turning point that halted the Muslim advance into western Europe, though historians debate its significance.

## The Umayyad advance

In 711 Muslim armies of the Umayyad Caliphate, mostly Berbers led by Arab commanders, crossed from North Africa into the Iberian Peninsula and conquered most of the Visigothic kingdom within a few years. From al-Andalus, as Muslim Iberia was called, they raided north across the Pyrenees into Gaul, taking Narbonne around 719. In 721 the Duke of Aquitaine, Odo, defeated a Muslim army at Toulouse.

## Abd al-Rahman's expedition

In 732 Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi led a large army across the western Pyrenees into Aquitaine. He defeated Duke Odo near Bordeaux and sacked the city. Odo fled north and sought help from Charles Martel, mayor of the palace of the Frankish kingdom, who held real power under the weak Merovingian kings. The Muslim army moved north towards Tours, perhaps attracted by the wealth of the abbey of Saint Martin, one of the most important shrines in Gaul.

## Charles Martel

Charles, born around 688, was the illegitimate son of Pepin of Herstal. He had fought his way to power in the 710s and spent years campaigning against Frisians, Saxons, Bavarians and others, building a strong, experienced army, much of it infantry. He gathered his forces and met the Muslim army somewhere between Poitiers and Tours; the exact location and date, probably in October 732 or possibly 733, are uncertain.

## The battle

According to the sources, a Latin chronicle, the Chronicle of 754, written in Spain, and later Frankish and Arab accounts, the armies faced each other for about a week. The Franks formed a solid defensive formation, "like a wall", on high ground, probably protected by trees. The Muslim cavalry charged repeatedly but could not break the Frankish infantry. Abd al-Rahman was killed. During the night, the Muslim army withdrew, leaving its camp and booty behind. The Franks, expecting another attack the next morning, found the camp empty. Numbers are unknown; later claims of hundreds of thousands of Muslim dead are exaggerations.

## Charles "the Hammer"

The victory strengthened Charles's power. He later earned the name Martel, "the hammer". He continued to campaign against Muslim forces in southern Gaul in the 730s, and extended Frankish control over Burgundy and Provence. His son Pepin the Short became king in 751, founding the Carolingian dynasty, and his grandson was Charlemagne. Narbonne was retaken in 759.

## Significance

Since the eighteenth century, historians such as Edward Gibbon presented Tours as a decisive battle that saved Christian Europe from Islamic conquest; Gibbon imagined that otherwise the Quran might have been taught at Oxford. Many modern historians argue that the expedition was a large raid rather than an invasion aimed at conquest, and that the Umayyad Caliphate was already overstretched, facing Berber revolts in North Africa from 740 and civil war, which ended Umayyad rule in 750. The battle's main significance may have been for the rise of the Carolingians. The battle has also been used in modern political rhetoric, a use that historians often criticise.
