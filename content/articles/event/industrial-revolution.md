The Industrial Revolution was the transition from hand production to machine manufacturing, using new energy sources, particularly coal and steam, which began in Britain in the second half of the eighteenth century and spread to Europe, North America and the world. It transformed how people worked and lived, beginning sustained economic growth and urbanisation.

## Why Britain?

Historians debate why it began in Britain. Factors include abundant coal and iron ore, often near each other and near water transport; an agricultural revolution that increased food production and released labour; a large colonial empire and trade network, including the profits of the slave trade; relatively high wages, which encouraged replacing labour with machines; cheap energy; a culture of practical invention; stable government, property rights and patents; and banks and capital to invest. Historians such as Kenneth Pomeranz have compared Britain with regions of China, emphasising coal and colonies.

## Textiles

The cotton industry led the way. A series of inventions mechanised spinning and weaving: John Kay's flying shuttle in 1733, James Hargreaves's spinning jenny around 1764, Richard Arkwright's water frame in 1769, and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule in 1779. Arkwright built factories, mills, at Cromford, where workers operated machines under supervision. Edmund Cartwright's power loom of 1785 mechanised weaving. Raw cotton came from India and, increasingly, from enslaved labour in the American South, especially after Eli Whitney's cotton gin of 1793.

## Steam

Thomas Newcomen's steam engine, from 1712, pumped water from mines. James Watt, from 1769, greatly improved its efficiency by adding a separate condenser, and with Matthew Boulton produced engines that could drive machinery, freeing factories from the need to be near rivers. Coal production soared.

## Iron

In 1709 Abraham Darby used coke, made from coal, instead of charcoal to smelt iron at Coalbrookdale, where the first iron bridge was built in 1779. Henry Cort's puddling process, in 1784, made wrought iron more cheaply. In the 1850s, Henry Bessemer's process made cheap steel possible.

## Transport

Canals, such as the Bridgewater Canal of 1761, carried coal and goods. George Stephenson's Rocket won the Rainhill trials in 1829, and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830, the first intercity railway. Railways spread rapidly. Steamships crossed oceans.

## Society

Industrialisation caused rapid urbanisation. Cities such as Manchester, Birmingham and Glasgow grew rapidly, with crowded, unsanitary housing and disease. Workers, including women and children, worked long hours in dangerous conditions for low wages. The Luddites smashed machines in 1811–1816. Workers formed trade unions, and reformers campaigned for laws, such as the Factory Act of 1833, which limited child labour. Writers, such as Charles Dickens and Friedrich Engels, described the conditions, and Karl Marx developed his critique of capitalism. Over time, living standards rose.

## Spread

Industrialisation spread to Belgium, France, Germany and the United States in the nineteenth century, and to Japan and Russia later. A "Second Industrial Revolution" from about 1870 brought steel, chemicals, electricity, oil and the internal combustion engine. The Industrial Revolution also began large-scale burning of fossil fuels, the origin of human-caused climate change, and widened the economic gap between industrialised countries and the rest of the world.
