The Atlantic slave trade was the forced transportation of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas, from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century. About 12.5 million people were embarked on ships, and about 10.7 million survived the voyage. It was one of the largest forced migrations in history and shaped the societies of Africa, Europe and the Americas.

## Origins

Slavery existed in many societies, including in Africa, Europe and the Islamic world. In the fifteenth century, Portuguese sailors exploring the West African coast began buying and capturing Africans, taking them to Portugal and to Atlantic islands, such as Madeira and São Tomé, where they worked on sugar plantations. After Europeans reached the Americas, the Indigenous population collapsed from disease, war and forced labour, and colonists turned to Africa for labour. The first enslaved Africans were taken directly from Africa to the Americas in the early sixteenth century.

## The triangular trade

The trade is often described as a triangle. European ships carried manufactured goods, such as cloth, guns, alcohol, metal goods and cowrie shells, to West and Central Africa, where they were exchanged for captives. The ships then crossed the Atlantic, the Middle Passage, and sold the enslaved people in the Americas, returning to Europe with sugar, tobacco, cotton, coffee and other products of slave labour. In reality, much trade went directly between Brazil and Africa.

## Africa

Most captives came from West and Central Africa, from regions such as Senegambia, the Gold Coast, the Bight of Benin, the Bight of Biafra and West Central Africa, Kongo and Angola, which supplied the largest number. African states and merchants sold captives taken in wars, raids, as punishment for crimes, or for debts. States such as Dahomey and Asante became involved in the trade, while others resisted. The trade caused population loss, warfare and political disruption.

## The Middle Passage

Enslaved people were packed tightly into ships' holds, chained, with little space, air, food or water, for voyages of six to eight weeks or more. Disease, such as dysentery, killed many; about 15 percent died on average, more on some voyages. Some resisted through revolts or by refusing to eat or jumping overboard. In the Zong massacre of 1781, the crew of a British ship threw over 130 Africans overboard to claim insurance.

## The Americas

About 40 percent of enslaved Africans went to Brazil and about 40 percent to the Caribbean, especially to sugar colonies such as Jamaica and Saint-Domingue, where death rates were very high. About 4 percent, roughly 400,000, went to British North America, where the enslaved population grew through natural increase. Enslaved people worked on plantations producing sugar, coffee, tobacco, rice, cotton and other crops, and in mines and households. They resisted through escape, forming maroon communities, revolts, such as the Haitian Revolution, and by preserving cultures, languages, religions and music, which shaped the cultures of the Americas.

## The nations involved

Portugal and Brazil carried the largest number, nearly half, followed by Britain, France, Spain, the Netherlands, the United States and Denmark. The trade's profits contributed to the wealth of port cities such as Liverpool, Bristol, Nantes and Lisbon.

## Abolition

From the late eighteenth century, abolitionists, including formerly enslaved people such as Olaudah Equiano, Quakers and campaigners such as Thomas Clarkson and William Wilberforce, campaigned against the trade. Denmark banned it in 1803, and Britain and the United States in 1807–1808. Britain's navy patrolled the African coast to intercept slave ships. Illegal trade continued, especially to Brazil and Cuba, until the 1860s. Slavery itself was abolished in the British Empire in 1833, the United States in 1865 and Brazil, the last in the Americas, in 1888.
