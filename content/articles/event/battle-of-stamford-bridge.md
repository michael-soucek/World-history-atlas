At Stamford Bridge, a village east of York, the English king Harold Godwinson destroyed a Norwegian invasion army on 25 September 1066. The Norwegian king, Harald Hardrada, and Harold's own exiled brother Tostig both died in the fighting. The victory ended the last great Norwegian attempt on the English crown, but it came only days before the Normans landed in the south, and less than three weeks before Harold's death at Hastings.

## A disputed throne

When Edward the Confessor died without children in January 1066, several rulers claimed the English throne. Harold, chosen king in England, faced challenges from William, Duke of Normandy, and from Harald Hardrada of Norway. Tostig, who had lost his earldom of Northumbria and been driven into exile in 1065, threw in his lot with the Norwegians after raiding the English coast earlier in the year.

According to one version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Hardrada sailed with some 300 ships, although these probably included supply vessels; his army, with reinforcements gathered in Orkney, most likely numbered 7,000 to 9,000 men. In September the invaders sailed up the Ouse. On 20 September they beat the northern earls Edwin and Morcar at Fulford, outside York, and the city surrendered. The Norwegians then fell back towards their ships at Riccall, arranging for more hostages and supplies to be handed over at Stamford Bridge.

## Harold's march north

Harold was in the south of England, expecting William to invade. On hearing of the invasion he hurried north with his housecarls and whatever thegns he could collect, covering roughly 185 miles from London in about four days. He passed through York and fell on the Norwegians at the meeting point on 25 September. They had no warning of his approach, and many had left their armour behind.

## The battle

Later Norse tradition, recorded by Snorri Sturluson, tells of a rider who approached the Norwegian leaders before the fighting and offered Tostig his earldom back, but promised Hardrada only "six feet of ground" in England; Tostig then revealed that the rider was Harold himself. English chroniclers tell a different story: a single Norwegian axeman held the narrow bridge against the English, killing many, until an English soldier got beneath it and stabbed upwards through the planks.

The delay let the Norwegians form a shield wall, but after hours of hard fighting it gave way. Hardrada was killed, according to tradition by an arrow in the throat, and Tostig fell too. Late in the day Eystein Orre arrived with the men who had been guarding the ships at Riccall, some 16 miles away. Their counter-attack, remembered as "Orre's Storm", briefly halted the English before it too was overwhelmed and Orre was killed. The fleeing Norwegians were pursued, and some drowned crossing rivers. Estimates of the Norwegian dead and missing run from 6,000 to more than 8,000.

## Aftermath

Harold let the survivors, led by Hardrada's son Olaf and the Earl of Orkney, sail home after they swore never to attack England again. Only 24 ships were needed to carry them away. On 28 September, three days later, William's Normans landed at Pevensey in Sussex. Harold marched his tired army south and was defeated and killed at the [Battle of Hastings](/event/battle-of-hastings) on 14 October, opening the way for [William the Conqueror](/person/william-the-conqueror) to take the kingdom.

Stamford Bridge is often said to mark the end of the Viking Age, though Scandinavian kings still campaigned in Britain and Ireland afterwards, among them Sweyn Estrithson of Denmark in 1069–1070 and Magnus Barefoot of Norway in 1098 and 1102–1103. The exact site is still debated; in 2020 metal detectorists found Viking military objects beside the Derwent roughly a mile downstream of the village.
