The Late Bronze Age collapse was a period of widespread societal collapse in the eastern Mediterranean and Near East around 1200 to 1150 BCE. Within a few decades, many of the great civilisations of the time, the Hittite Empire, the Mycenaean kingdoms of Greece and cities such as Ugarit, were destroyed or declined, while Egypt was weakened. The causes are still debated.

## An interconnected world

In the Late Bronze Age, the eastern Mediterranean and Near East were connected by trade and diplomacy. Great kingdoms, Egypt, the Hittites in Anatolia, Assyria, Babylon and Mitanni, exchanged letters, gifts and royal brides, as recorded in the Amarna letters of the fourteenth century BCE. Palaces in Mycenaean Greece kept records in Linear B. The Uluburun shipwreck, from around 1300 BCE, found off southern Turkey, carried copper ingots from Cyprus, tin, glass, ivory, gold and other goods from across the region. Tin, needed to make bronze from copper, came from far away, perhaps Central Asia.

## Destruction

Around 1200 BCE, many cities were destroyed. The Hittite capital, Hattusa, was abandoned and burned, and the Hittite Empire disappeared. Ugarit, a rich trading city on the Syrian coast, was destroyed around 1185 BCE; its last tablets include letters asking for help against enemy ships. In Greece, the Mycenaean palaces, such as Pylos and Mycenae, were destroyed, and writing disappeared; Greece entered a "Dark Age" until about 800 BCE. Cyprus and many cities in the Levant were also destroyed.

## The Sea Peoples

Egyptian inscriptions describe invasions by groups called the Sea Peoples. At his mortuary temple, Medinet Habu, Ramesses III recorded that around 1177 BCE "the foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands", destroying the Hittites, Cyprus and other lands, and that he defeated them in battles on land and sea. The reliefs show their warriors, ships and families with ox-carts, suggesting migration. The groups named include the Peleset, often identified with the Philistines, who settled in the southern Levant, and the Sherden, Shekelesh and others. Their origins are debated, perhaps the Aegean, Anatolia or elsewhere.

## Possible causes

Historians and archaeologists have proposed many causes: invasions and migrations, such as the Sea Peoples; drought and famine, supported by climate evidence from pollen and sediment cores showing a long dry period, and texts mentioning grain shipments to the Hittites; earthquakes, an "earthquake storm"; internal revolts; disruption of trade, especially of tin; new military tactics, with infantry defeating chariot armies; and disease. Many scholars, such as Eric Cline, argue that a combination of factors caused a "systems collapse" of an interconnected world, where problems in one area spread to others.

## Aftermath

Egypt survived but weakened and lost its empire in the Levant. Assyria survived and later rose again. In the aftermath, new peoples and states emerged: the Philistines, the Israelites, the Phoenician cities, such as Tyre and Sidon, which spread the alphabet across the Mediterranean, the Aramaeans and the Neo-Hittite states. Iron gradually replaced bronze, beginning the Iron Age. In Greece, the memory of the Mycenaean age survived in the epics of Homer.
