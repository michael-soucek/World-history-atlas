The Greco-Persian Wars were a series of conflicts between the Achaemenid Persian Empire and Greek city-states, beginning in 499 BCE and lasting until 449 BCE. The Greeks repelled two Persian invasions of mainland Greece, at Marathon in 490 BCE and at Salamis and Plataea in 480–479 BCE. Our main source is the Greek historian Herodotus, who wrote his Histories to explain the conflict.

## The Ionian Revolt

In the mid-sixth century BCE, Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Lydia and the Greek cities of Ionia, on the coast of Asia Minor. In 499 BCE the Ionian cities rebelled, led by Aristagoras of Miletus. Athens and Eretria sent ships, and the rebels burned Sardis. The Persians crushed the revolt, destroying Miletus in 494 BCE. King Darius I decided to punish Athens and Eretria.

## Marathon

A first Persian expedition in 492 BCE, under Mardonius, was wrecked by a storm off Mount Athos. In 490 BCE a Persian fleet crossed the Aegean, destroyed Eretria and landed at Marathon, near Athens. The Athenians, with the Plataeans, under generals such as Miltiades, defeated the Persians, who withdrew.

## Xerxes's invasion

Darius died in 486 BCE, and his son Xerxes prepared a huge invasion. He built a bridge of boats across the Hellespont and a canal through the Athos peninsula. Herodotus claimed his army numbered over a million; modern historians estimate perhaps 100,000 to 300,000. In 480 BCE the army marched through Thrace and Macedon. Many Greek states submitted, but about thirty, led by Sparta and Athens, formed an alliance. The Athenian Themistocles had persuaded Athens to use the profits of a new silver mine to build a fleet of triremes.

## Thermopylae and Artemisium

In August 480 BCE, a small Greek force led by King Leonidas of Sparta held the narrow pass of Thermopylae for three days. When a local man, Ephialtes, showed the Persians a mountain path around the pass, Leonidas sent most of his troops away and stayed with his 300 Spartans and some others, who were killed. At the same time, the Greek fleet fought the Persian fleet at Artemisium. The Persians advanced and burned Athens, whose people had been evacuated to Salamis and other places.

## Salamis

In September 480 BCE, Themistocles lured the Persian fleet into the narrow strait between the island of Salamis and the mainland, where its larger numbers were a disadvantage. The Greek triremes rammed and sank many Persian ships, while Xerxes watched from a throne on the shore. Xerxes returned to Asia, leaving an army under Mardonius in Greece.

## Plataea and Mycale

In 479 BCE, the Greek army, led by the Spartan Pausanias, defeated and killed Mardonius at Plataea, ending the invasion. On the same day, according to tradition, the Greek fleet destroyed the Persian fleet at Mycale, in Asia Minor.

## Aftermath

The Greeks went on the offensive. In 478 BCE Athens formed the Delian League, an alliance of Greek states to continue the war, which became an Athenian empire. The Athenian general Cimon won at the Eurymedon river around 466 BCE. The wars ended around 449 BCE, with the possibly legendary Peace of Callias. The victory gave Athens confidence and wealth, contributing to its golden age, and was celebrated by playwrights, such as Aeschylus in The Persians, and in later Western tradition as a victory of freedom, a view historians now present more critically.
