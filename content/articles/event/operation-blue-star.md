Operation Blue Star was the Indian Army's assault, between 1 and 10 June 1984, on the Harmandir Sahib, or Golden Temple, in Amritsar, the holiest shrine of Sikhism. Ordered by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, its aim was to remove the Sikh preacher Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale and armed followers who had fortified the complex. The army took control and Bhindranwale was killed, but the heavy loss of life and the damage to sacred buildings deeply alienated many Sikhs, and the operation was followed by Gandhi's assassination and more than a decade of insurgency in Punjab.

## Background

Sikh political demands had a long history in Punjab. A state with a Sikh majority was created in 1966, and in 1973 the Akali Dal party adopted the Anandpur Sahib Resolution, calling for greater autonomy for Punjab within India. The central government rejected it, and Indira Gandhi regarded it as secessionist; Akali leaders insisted that Sikhs had no wish to leave [India](/country/india). Economic strains linked to the Green Revolution, including unemployment among educated young men, fed discontent. In 1982 Bhindranwale and the Akali Dal launched a mass agitation, the Dharam Yudh Morcha, which met with harsh policing.

## Rising violence

Bhindranwale moved into the Golden Temple complex in 1982 and in December 1983 set up his headquarters in the Akal Takht, the seat of Sikh temporal authority, where the sixth guru had first raised an army to fight the [Mughal Empire](/place/mughal-empire), a step the temple's high priest protested. Former army officers, notably Major General Shabeg Singh, helped to fortify the complex, and weapons were reportedly brought in. Violence escalated across Punjab: by the government's account, killings of Hindus and members of the Sant Nirankari sect before the operation numbered 165, and newspaper editors were among those murdered. Indian officials also cited intelligence of support from [Pakistan](/country/pakistan) for the militants. Critics note that most killings were never solved and that the government never substantiated some of its claims.

## The assault

Security forces opened fire on the complex on 1 June. On 3 June, when the temple was crowded with pilgrims marking the martyrdom anniversary of Guru Arjan, Punjab was placed under curfew, with communications cut and the press barred. The main assault, under Major General Kuldip Singh Brar, began on the night of 5 June. Facing stiff resistance from a few hundred defenders, the army brought in tanks and heavy weapons. Fighting largely ended early on 7 June, when the bodies of Bhindranwale and Shabeg Singh were found in the Akal Takht, though isolated resistance continued until 10 June.

The Akal Takht was largely destroyed and the main shrine was struck by bullets. On 7 June, after the army had taken the complex, the Sikh Reference Library, which held manuscripts and historic letters, was burned.

## Casualties

Figures are fiercely disputed. The army initially reported 83 soldiers killed and 554 militants and civilians dead. The journalist Kuldip Nayar wrote that Rajiv Gandhi later spoke of nearly 700 soldiers killed. The Associated Press correspondent Brahma Chellaney, the only foreign reporter to remain in Amritsar, reported at least 1,200 dead in the complex. Some independent and Sikh sources give totals in the thousands. The government blamed the militants for using pilgrims as shields; critics, and a 2014 British government review, pointed to the decision to launch a frontal assault while the complex was full of civilians.

## Aftermath

Around 2,600 Sikh soldiers mutinied in several cantonments, and most were court-martialled. On 31 October 1984 Indira Gandhi was assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards in revenge, and the anti-Sikh violence that followed, centred on Delhi, killed many Sikhs. The army chief at the time of the operation, General Arun Vaidya, was assassinated in 1986. Rather than ending militancy, the operation was followed by its rapid growth; official estimates of annual deaths in Punjab exceeded one thousand each year from 1987 to 1992.
