The Neolithic Revolution, or Agricultural Revolution, was the transition of human societies from hunting and gathering to farming and settlement, which began around 10,000 BCE in the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East and occurred independently in several other regions. It made possible larger populations, permanent villages and, eventually, cities, states and civilisations.

## Hunters and gatherers

For most of human history, people lived by hunting animals, fishing and gathering wild plants, moving seasonally in small groups. At the end of the last ice age, around 11,700 years ago, the climate became warmer and more stable, which may have made farming possible.

## The Fertile Crescent

The earliest known farming developed in the Fertile Crescent, an arc of land from the Levant through south-eastern Turkey to the Zagros mountains of Iran and Iraq. People of the Natufian culture, around 12,500 to 9500 BCE, lived in permanent settlements and harvested wild cereals. Around 9500 BCE, people began cultivating plants, selecting seeds of wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas and flax. Over time, these plants changed, becoming domesticated; for example, the seeds of domesticated wheat stay on the plant until harvested. People domesticated goats, sheep, pigs and cattle, from wild species, between about 9000 and 8000 BCE.

## Early villages

Early farming villages included Jericho, with its stone wall and tower built around 8000 BCE, and Çatalhöyük, in Anatolia, from about 7100 BCE, where thousands of people lived in mud-brick houses entered through the roof, decorated with wall paintings and bulls' horns. Göbekli Tepe, in south-eastern Turkey, built from about 9500 BCE, has large carved stone pillars arranged in circles, built by people who were still largely hunter-gatherers, suggesting that religion and communal gatherings may have helped drive settlement.

## Independent centres

Farming developed independently in several regions. In China, people domesticated rice in the Yangtze valley and millet in the Yellow River valley by around 7000 BCE. In New Guinea, people grew bananas and taro. In Mesoamerica, people domesticated squash around 8000 BCE and maize, from a wild grass, teosinte, over thousands of years, along with beans. In the Andes, people domesticated potatoes, quinoa, llamas and alpacas. In Africa, sorghum, millet and yams were domesticated, and in eastern North America, sunflowers and other plants.

## Spread

From the Fertile Crescent, farming spread to Europe, North Africa and South Asia. Ancient DNA studies show that farming spread into Europe largely through the migration of farmers from Anatolia, from around 6500 BCE, who mixed with local hunter-gatherers.

## Consequences

Farming could support far more people per area than hunting and gathering, and populations grew. People lived in permanent villages, stored food and accumulated possessions, leading to greater inequality, property and specialisation of work. New technologies developed, such as pottery, weaving and polished stone tools. But early farmers often had poorer health than hunter-gatherers: they suffered from a less varied diet, tooth decay, and diseases spread by living close to each other and to animals. Some writers, such as Jared Diamond, have called agriculture "the worst mistake in the history of the human race", a deliberately provocative view. Farming eventually led to cities, writing and states, in Mesopotamia from about 4000 to 3000 BCE.

## On our maps

Our earliest maps, from 5000 BCE onward, show the world after the spread of farming, including early farming cultures such as [Elam](/place/elam) and the [Indus Valley Civilisation](/place/indus-valley-civilisation).
