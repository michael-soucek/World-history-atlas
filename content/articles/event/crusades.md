The Crusades were a series of religious wars between 1096 and the late thirteenth century, sanctioned by the Latin Church, in which Christians from western Europe sought to capture and hold Jerusalem and the Holy Land from Muslim rule. Later crusades were also launched against pagans in the Baltic, heretics in Europe and the Muslims of Iberia.

## The call

In 1095 the Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos asked Pope Urban II for help against the Seljuk Turks, who had taken most of Anatolia after the Battle of Manzikert in 1071. At the Council of Clermont, in France, in November 1095, Urban called on Christians to go to the aid of the eastern Christians and free Jerusalem, promising forgiveness of sins to those who took part. The crowd reportedly shouted "God wills it!" Participants took vows and sewed crosses onto their clothes, hence the name, from the Latin crux.

## The First Crusade

In 1096 a popular movement, the People's Crusade, led by Peter the Hermit, set off, massacring Jews in Rhineland cities, and was destroyed by the Turks in Anatolia. The main army, led by nobles such as Godfrey of Bouillon, Raymond of Toulouse and Bohemond of Taranto, took Nicaea and, after a long siege, Antioch, in 1098. On 15 July 1099, the crusaders took Jerusalem and massacred many of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. They founded four Crusader states: the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch and the County of Tripoli.

## Military orders

Military religious orders, such as the Knights Templar and the Knights Hospitaller, were founded to protect pilgrims and defend the Crusader states, building great castles, such as the Krak des Chevaliers.

## The Second and Third Crusades

In 1144 Zengi, ruler of Mosul, took Edessa. The Second Crusade, led by Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany, failed at Damascus in 1148. Saladin, sultan of Egypt and Syria, defeated the Crusaders at Hattin in 1187 and took Jerusalem. The Third Crusade, led by Richard I of England, Philip II of France and Frederick Barbarossa, who drowned on the way, took Acre and won battles, but did not retake Jerusalem. In 1192 Richard and Saladin agreed a truce allowing Christian pilgrims to visit.

## The Fourth Crusade

The Fourth Crusade, launched in 1202, unable to pay the Venetians for their ships, was diverted to Constantinople, which the crusaders sacked in April 1204, founding a Latin Empire. The sack deepened the division between the Catholic and Orthodox churches.

## Later crusades

The Fifth Crusade attacked Egypt and failed. In the Sixth Crusade, Emperor Frederick II negotiated the return of Jerusalem in 1229, without fighting, but it was lost again in 1244. Louis IX of France led the Seventh Crusade to Egypt, where he was captured in 1250, and died at Tunis on the Eighth Crusade in 1270. The Mamluks took the last Crusader stronghold, Acre, in 1291.

## Other crusades

Crusades were also launched against the pagan peoples of the Baltic, by the Teutonic Knights and others, against the Cathar heretics of southern France, in the Albigensian Crusade from 1209, and in Iberia, as part of the Reconquista. Later crusades fought the Ottomans.

## Legacy

The Crusades increased trade between Europe and the Middle East, enriched Italian cities, such as Venice and Genoa, and led to persecution of Jews in Europe. They have different meanings in European and Middle Eastern memory, and the word "crusade" remains politically charged.
