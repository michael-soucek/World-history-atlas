The revolutions of 1989 were a wave of revolutions that overthrew communist regimes in central and eastern Europe. Within a few months, in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria and Romania, communist governments fell, mostly peacefully, leading to the end of the Cold War, the reunification of Germany and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

## Background

After the Second World War, the Soviet Union imposed communist governments on the countries of eastern Europe. Uprisings were crushed by Soviet forces, in East Germany in 1953, Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968. By the 1980s, these countries faced economic stagnation and debt. Mikhail Gorbachev, Soviet leader from 1985, introduced reforms and, crucially, abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, signalling that the Soviet Union would not intervene militarily to keep communist governments in power.

## Poland

In Poland, the independent trade union Solidarity, led by the electrician Lech Wałęsa, had been founded in 1980 at the Gdańsk shipyard, with millions of members, before being banned under martial law in 1981. After strikes in 1988, the government negotiated with Solidarity at the Round Table talks in early 1989, agreeing to partly free elections on 4 June 1989. Solidarity won almost all the seats it could contest, and in August Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Eastern bloc since the 1940s.

## Hungary

In Hungary, reformers within the Communist Party negotiated a transition to multi-party democracy. In May 1989 Hungary began dismantling its fence on the border with Austria, and in June Imre Nagy, the leader of the 1956 revolution, was reburied with honour. On 19 August, at the Pan-European Picnic near Sopron, hundreds of East Germans crossed into Austria. In September Hungary opened its border, allowing thousands of East Germans to leave for the West.

## The fall of the Berlin Wall

In East Germany, mass protests grew, especially the Monday demonstrations in Leipzig, with hundreds of thousands chanting "We are the people". The leader Erich Honecker was removed in October. On 9 November 1989, after an official, Günter Schabowski, mistakenly announced at a press conference that travel restrictions were lifted immediately, crowds gathered at the Berlin Wall, and border guards opened the crossings. People climbed on the wall and began to break it down. Germany was reunified on 3 October 1990.

## Czechoslovakia

On 17 November 1989, police attacked a student demonstration in Prague, sparking mass protests and a general strike. In the Velvet Revolution, the communist government resigned, and in December the dissident playwright Václav Havel was elected president.

## Bulgaria and Romania

In Bulgaria, the long-time leader Todor Zhivkov was removed by his own party on 10 November. In Romania, the regime of Nicolae Ceaușescu used force against protesters in Timișoara in December 1989. When a rally in Bucharest turned against him, Ceaușescu fled, but he and his wife Elena were captured, tried by a military tribunal and executed on 25 December 1989. Over a thousand people died in the Romanian revolution, the only violent one of the year.

## Aftermath

The revolutions led to democratic elections, market economies and, for many countries, membership of NATO and the European Union in the 1990s and 2000s. The Warsaw Pact was dissolved in 1991, and the Soviet Union collapsed in December 1991. In China, protests in Tiananmen Square were crushed on 4 June 1989.
