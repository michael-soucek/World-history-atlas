The Black Death was a pandemic of bubonic plague that swept across Asia, Europe and North Africa from 1346 to 1353. It killed an estimated 30 to 60 percent of Europe's population, and millions in the Middle East and elsewhere, making it one of the deadliest disasters in human history, with profound social, economic and religious consequences.

## The disease

The plague is caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas living on rodents, such as rats and marmots. When infected fleas bite humans, they cause bubonic plague, with painful swellings, buboes, in the groin, armpits or neck, fever and black patches on the skin; most victims died within days. Pneumonic plague, infecting the lungs, could spread directly between people through coughing and was almost always fatal. DNA from victims' teeth, studied since 2010, has confirmed Yersinia pestis as the cause.

## Origins and spread

The plague probably originated in Central Asia; a 2022 study of graves in Kyrgyzstan dated the origin of the strain to around 1338. It spread along trade routes. In 1346 it struck the Golden Horde army besieging Caffa, a Genoese trading port in Crimea. According to a contemporary account, the Mongols catapulted infected corpses into the city. Genoese ships carried the plague to Constantinople and Messina, in Sicily, in 1347. From there it spread rapidly along shipping routes and roads: to Italy, France, Spain and North Africa, Cairo and Damascus in 1347–1348, England in 1348, Germany, Scandinavia and the Low Countries in 1349 and Russia by 1351–1353.

## Death

Cities were devastated. Florence may have lost over half its population; the writer Giovanni Boccaccio described the horrors in the Decameron. Mass graves were dug. In Cairo, thousands died daily at the height of the epidemic. Some places, such as Milan, which sealed houses of the sick, may have been less affected. Doctors, who explained the disease through bad air, miasma, or the position of the planets, could do little. People fled, isolated the sick, carried flowers and herbs and prayed.

## Reactions

Many saw the plague as God's punishment. Flagellants, groups of penitents, travelled from town to town, whipping themselves. Jews were falsely accused of poisoning wells, and hundreds of Jewish communities in Germany, Switzerland and France were massacred, as in Strasbourg in 1349, despite Pope Clement VI's condemnation. Many Jews fled to Poland. Art showed death, such as the Dance of Death, a skeleton leading people of all ranks.

## Consequences

The loss of so many workers led to labour shortages and higher wages, giving surviving peasants and workers greater bargaining power. Rulers tried to fix wages, as in England's Statute of Labourers of 1351, leading to discontent and revolts, such as the Jacquerie in France in 1358 and the Peasants' Revolt in England in 1381. In western Europe, serfdom declined. Land was abandoned, and many villages disappeared. Some historians argue that the changes contributed to the end of the medieval economy and the rise of new ways of thinking. The church's authority was challenged.

## Return

The plague returned many times over the following centuries, such as the Great Plague of London in 1665 and the plague of Marseille in 1720. Quarantine, from the Italian quaranta giorni, "forty days", was first used in Ragusa, today's Dubrovnik, in 1377. A third pandemic began in China in the 1850s. Plague still exists today but can be treated with antibiotics.

## On our maps

The Black Death falls between our 1300 and 1400 maps. It spread along routes linking the [Golden Horde](/place/golden-horde), the [Byzantine Empire](/place/byzantine-empire), the [Mamluk Sultanate](/place/mamluk-sultanate) and the kingdoms of Europe.
