The Holocaust was the genocide of European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during the Second World War. Between 1941 and 1945, about six million Jews, around two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population, were systematically murdered. The Nazis also persecuted and murdered millions of others, including Roma, disabled people, Polish and Soviet civilians, Soviet prisoners of war, gay men and political opponents.

## Antisemitism and persecution

Antisemitism had a long history in Europe. Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party, which came to power in Germany in 1933, held a racial antisemitic ideology, portraying Jews as a race threatening Germany. The regime excluded Jews from the civil service, professions and public life. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and non-Jews. On 9–10 November 1938, in the Kristallnacht pogrom, synagogues were burned, Jewish shops destroyed, about 30,000 Jewish men sent to concentration camps and around a hundred killed. Many Jews emigrated, but many countries restricted immigration, as at the Évian Conference of 1938.

## Ghettos

After invading Poland in 1939, the Germans forced Polish Jews into ghettos, sealed districts in cities such as Warsaw and Łódź, where hundreds of thousands died of starvation and disease. The Nazis also murdered disabled people in Germany in the "euthanasia" programme, Aktion T4, killing about 70,000 by gas in 1940–1941, developing methods later used in the death camps.

## Mass shootings

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, mobile killing units, the Einsatzgruppen, with police battalions, the army and local collaborators, shot Jews in mass executions. At Babi Yar, near Kyiv, over 33,000 Jews were shot in two days in September 1941. By the end of the war, about two million Jews had been shot.

## The Final Solution

In January 1942, at the Wannsee Conference, Nazi officials coordinated the "Final Solution", the plan to murder all the Jews of Europe. Extermination camps were built in occupied Poland: Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Majdanek and Auschwitz-Birkenau. Jews from across Europe, from France and the Netherlands to Greece and Hungary, were deported in freight trains. At the death camps, most were murdered in gas chambers on arrival. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest, about 1.1 million people were murdered, about a million of them Jews; some prisoners were selected for slave labour. About 1.7 million Jews were murdered in the camps of Operation Reinhard, Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka.

## Resistance

Jews resisted in many ways: by escaping, hiding, smuggling food, keeping records, such as the Oyneg Shabes archive of the Warsaw ghetto, and armed revolt, such as the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April 1943 and revolts at Treblinka and Sobibór. Some non-Jews risked their lives to rescue Jews and are honoured as Righteous Among the Nations; the Danish people helped most of Denmark's Jews escape to Sweden in 1943.

## Liberation

As Allied armies advanced, the Nazis forced prisoners on death marches. Soviet troops liberated Auschwitz on 27 January 1945, now International Holocaust Remembrance Day. British and American troops found thousands of dead and dying prisoners at camps such as Bergen-Belsen and Dachau.

## Aftermath

Some Nazi leaders were tried at the Nuremberg trials in 1945–1946. The term "genocide" was coined by Raphael Lemkin, and the Genocide Convention was adopted in 1948. Many survivors emigrated to Israel, founded in 1948, and the United States. Testimonies, such as Anne Frank's diary, and memorials preserve its memory.
