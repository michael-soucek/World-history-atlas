The Mongol invasion of Europe in the thirteenth century brought the armies of the Mongol Empire across the Rus' principalities, Poland and Hungary between 1237 and 1242. The Mongols destroyed much of Kyivan Rus', which became subject to the Golden Horde for over two centuries, and devastated Poland and Hungary before withdrawing.

## The western campaign

After the death of Genghis Khan in 1227, his son Ögedei became Great Khan. In 1235 a kurultai decided on a great campaign to the west, to conquer the lands of the Kipchaks, the Volga Bulgars and the Rus'. The army was led by Batu, Genghis's grandson, with the brilliant general Subutai, who had already raided the region in 1223, defeating the Rus' and Kipchaks at the Kalka river. Several princes of Genghis's family took part.

## Volga Bulgaria and the Rus'

In 1236 the Mongols destroyed Volga Bulgaria. In the winter of 1237–1238, using frozen rivers as roads, they attacked the Rus' principalities, which were divided and quarrelling. They sacked Ryazan, Kolomna, Moscow and Vladimir, the capital of the grand principality, killing Grand Prince Yuri II at the Sit river in March 1238. Novgorod was spared because the spring thaw made the marshes impassable. In 1239 the Mongols took Chernigov and Pereyaslavl, and in December 1240 they besieged and sacked Kyiv, the old capital of the Rus'. A papal envoy who passed through some years later described the skulls and bones lying on the ground.

## Poland

In 1241 the Mongols advanced into central Europe in several armies. One force, led by Baidar and Kadan, raided Poland, sacking Sandomierz and Kraków. On 9 April 1241, at Legnica, in Silesia, they defeated a Polish and German army led by Duke Henry II the Pious, who was killed; his head was carried on a spear. According to legend, a trumpeter in Kraków was shot in the throat while sounding the alarm, commemorated by a broken-off tune still played every hour from St Mary's Church.

## Hungary

The main army, under Batu and Subutai, invaded Hungary. King Béla IV had alienated his nobles by settling the Cumans, nomads fleeing the Mongols, in Hungary; the Cuman leader Köten was murdered by a mob, and the Cumans left. On 11 April 1241, at Mohi, on the Sajó river, the Mongols surrounded the Hungarian army in its camp, then left a gap through which the Hungarians fled, and cut them down. Much of the Hungarian army was destroyed. Béla fled to the Dalmatian coast, pursued by the Mongols. The Mongols ravaged Hungary for a year; perhaps a quarter of its population died, from the invasion and the resulting famine. Raiding parties reached the outskirts of Vienna.

## Withdrawal

In 1242 the Mongols withdrew from central Europe. The reasons are debated. News arrived of the death of Ögedei in December 1241, and Batu may have wanted to influence the choice of the next Great Khan. Other explanations include the poor pastures of the Hungarian plain for the Mongol horses, a wet spring that turned the plain to marsh, losses, and resistance from stone castles. Batu established the Golden Horde, with its capital at Sarai on the lower Volga.

## Consequences

The Rus' principalities paid tribute to the Golden Horde for over two centuries, the "Tatar yoke", which influenced the rise of Moscow. In Hungary, Béla IV rebuilt the country, encouraging the construction of stone castles and inviting settlers. Europe sent envoys, such as the Franciscan friars Giovanni da Pian del Carpine and William of Rubruck, to the Mongol court. Later raids on Poland and Hungary in the 1280s were repelled.
