The Battle of Hastings, fought on 14 October 1066, was the decisive battle of the Norman conquest of England. William, Duke of Normandy, defeated the English king Harold Godwinson, who was killed, and was crowned king of England on Christmas Day. The battle transformed England's language, aristocracy and history.

## The succession crisis

King Edward the Confessor died in January 1066 without children. The English council chose Harold Godwinson, Earl of Wessex, the most powerful noble in the kingdom, as king. But two others also claimed the throne: William of Normandy, Edward's cousin, who said that Edward had promised him the crown and that Harold had sworn to support him during a visit to Normandy, and Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, who claimed it through an earlier agreement.

## Stamford Bridge

Harold expected an invasion from Normandy and kept an army on the south coast through the summer, but in September he had to send them home. Harald Hardrada, with Harold's exiled brother Tostig, invaded northern England, defeated the northern earls at Fulford, and took York. Harold marched north rapidly and, on 25 September 1066, surprised and defeated the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge, killing Harald and Tostig. Only a fraction of the Norwegian fleet returned home.

## The Norman landing

Three days later, on 28 September, William landed at Pevensey, in Sussex, with an army of perhaps 7,000 to 8,000 men, including knights, archers and infantry, and built a fortification at Hastings. His fleet had been delayed by unfavourable winds. Harold marched south, gathering troops, and reached the area on 13 October.

## The battle

On 14 October, Harold's army, perhaps of similar size, took up position on Senlac Hill, near today's town of Battle, about ten kilometres from Hastings. The English, mainly infantry, including the king's elite housecarls with large two-handed axes, formed a shield wall along the ridge. The Normans attacked uphill with archers, infantry and cavalry. The shield wall held for hours. At one point, a rumour that William had been killed caused panic among the Normans, and William lifted his helmet to show his face. Some English troops broke ranks to pursue fleeing Normans and were cut down. The Normans may have used feigned retreats to draw more English out. In the late afternoon, the English line weakened, and Harold was killed, according to tradition by an arrow in the eye, as possibly shown on the Bayeux Tapestry, though this is debated. His brothers Gyrth and Leofwine also died. The English army broke.

## The conquest

William waited for the English to submit, then marched on London, ravaging the countryside. The English leaders submitted at Berkhamsted, and William was crowned in Westminster Abbey on 25 December 1066. Rebellions continued for years, especially in the north, which William crushed brutally.

## The Bayeux Tapestry

The battle is depicted on the Bayeux Tapestry, an embroidered cloth about 70 metres long, made probably in England in the 1070s, which tells the story from the Norman perspective.

## Consequences

The Norman conquest replaced the English aristocracy with a French-speaking Norman elite, introduced castles and a feudal order, transformed English architecture and the church, and brought thousands of French words into English. William built Battle Abbey on the battlefield, with its altar said to mark where Harold fell.
