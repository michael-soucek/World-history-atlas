In 853 BCE the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III marched into Syria and met a coalition of local rulers near the Orontes River, at a town called Qarqar. The clash is known almost entirely from Shalmaneser's own inscriptions, which claim a crushing victory, but the coalition survived and Assyrian expansion in the region stalled for years. Among the allies was Ahab, king of Israel, and the battle also marks the first appearance of Arabs in written records.

## Assyria moves west

In the ninth century BCE the kings of [Assyria](/place/assyria) led an army out almost every year, demanding tribute and punishing rebels. According to an inscription Shalmaneser later set up, he left Nineveh in the month of Iyar of his campaigning year, crossed the Tigris and Euphrates, and received the submission of several cities, including Aleppo. Beyond Aleppo he fought troops of Irhuleni, king of Hamath, plundered his towns and sacked Qarqar itself before meeting the allied army. Some scholars, using a different version of the Assyrian list of year-names, place the battle in 854 BCE instead.

## The coalition

The Kurkh Monolith, an inscribed Assyrian stele, lists the opposing forces in unusual detail. Their leader was Hadadezer of Damascus, who is credited with 1,200 chariots, 1,200 horsemen and 20,000 foot soldiers. Irhuleni of Hamath and Ahab of Israel brought large contingents, and smaller ones came from Cilicia, from Phoenician cities such as Arwad (see [Phoenicia](/culture/phoenicia)), from Ammon, and from an Arab ruler named Gindibu, who sent 1,000 camel riders. The Assyrian text calls them "twelve kings", a stock phrase for an alliance, although only eleven are named. Taken at face value, the figures add up to roughly 4,000 chariots and more than 50,000 infantry.

Ahab's reported 2,000 chariots, more than any other ally, are disputed. Some scholars suspect a scribal error for 200; others point to large ninth-century stable complexes excavated at Megiddo as evidence that Israel could field a strong chariot force, or suggest the number includes troops from Israel's own allies.

## The battle

Shalmaneser's account is the only description of the fighting. He claims to have routed the allies from Qarqar as far as a place called Gilzau, killed 14,000 of their soldiers, and choked the Orontes with corpses as if with a bridge. These are the king's own figures and imagery, and Assyrian royal inscriptions of this period never admitted defeat; modern historians treat them with caution.

## An uncertain outcome

The aftermath suggests the result was far less decisive than claimed. Shalmaneser did not follow up with conquests in Syria. His own records show him returning to the region several times over the next decade and fighting Hadadezer's coalition repeatedly. Hadadezer remained king of Damascus until at least 841 BCE. Ahab died soon afterwards, but in a separate war. Many historians therefore see Qarqar as an Assyrian success on the field that nonetheless checked Assyria's advance for a time.

## Legacy

Qarqar is usually identified with Tell Qarqur, an archaeological mound near a modern village of the same name in Hama Governorate, in north-western [Syria](/country/syria). The battle is important to historians less for its result than for its records: it ties a biblical king to a precisely dated Assyrian source and shows the scale of armies in the Iron Age Near East. A century and a half later, Assyrian kings would return to the Levant in force, as at the [siege of Lachish](/event/siege-of-lachish).
