The Columbian Exchange was the transfer of plants, animals, diseases, people, technologies and ideas between the Americas and the Old World, Europe, Africa and Asia, which followed Christopher Columbus's voyage in 1492. Named by the historian Alfred Crosby in 1972, it transformed diets, economies, environments and populations across the globe.

## Two worlds

For thousands of years, the Americas had been largely separated from the rest of the world, after people arrived from Asia during the last ice age. Separate ecosystems, crops, domestic animals and diseases had developed. The Norse briefly reached North America around 1000, but the contact had no lasting effects. After 1492, European ships connected the hemispheres continuously.

## From the Americas

American crops spread across the world. Maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes and cassava, high-yielding staples, allowed population growth in Europe, Africa and Asia. The potato became the main food of Ireland and important in northern Europe, and sweet potatoes and maize spread in China, contributing to population growth in the eighteenth century. Cassava became a staple in Africa. Tomatoes transformed Italian cuisine and chilli peppers the cuisines of India, Thailand, Korea and China. Other American products included beans, squash, peanuts, pineapples, vanilla, cacao, the source of chocolate, rubber and tobacco, which became a global habit.

## From the Old World

Europeans brought wheat, rice, barley, sugar cane, bananas, coffee, citrus fruits, grapes and olives to the Americas. They brought horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and chickens. Horses transformed the lives of the Plains peoples of North America, such as the Comanche and Lakota, who became mounted buffalo hunters, and the peoples of the Argentine pampas. Cattle and sheep, spreading rapidly, changed landscapes. Sugar, grown on plantations with enslaved African labour, became the most valuable crop of the Caribbean and Brazil; coffee later became a major crop in Brazil and Colombia.

## Disease

The most devastating part of the exchange was disease. Indigenous Americans had no immunity to Old World diseases, such as smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus and malaria. Epidemics, beginning on Hispaniola in the 1490s and in Mexico in 1520, killed a large proportion of the Indigenous population, perhaps 50 to 90 percent in many regions, over the following century. Estimates of the population of the Americas in 1492 vary widely, from about 8 to over 100 million; most modern estimates are around 40 to 60 million. The epidemics, combined with war, forced labour and famine, made European conquest easier. Some scholars argue that syphilis travelled from the Americas to Europe, appearing in 1495, though this is debated.

## People

The exchange also involved the movement of people. Millions of Europeans migrated to the Americas, and about 12.5 million Africans were forcibly transported as enslaved people, transforming the population and culture of the Americas.

## Environmental change

The decline of the Indigenous population led to the reforestation of abandoned farmland, which, some scientists argue, absorbed enough carbon dioxide to contribute to the cooling of the Little Ice Age. European weeds, pests and animals spread across the Americas. Silver from the Americas also flowed to Europe and China, creating the first global economy.
