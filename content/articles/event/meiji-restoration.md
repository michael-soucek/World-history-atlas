The Meiji Restoration was the political revolution in 1868 that ended the rule of the Tokugawa shogunate and restored practical power to the emperor of Japan. In the following decades, the new government transformed Japan from a feudal, isolated country into a modern industrial state and a world power, in one of the fastest modernisations in history.

## The end of isolation

For over two centuries, the Tokugawa shoguns had ruled Japan, limiting contact with the outside world. In 1853 American warships under Commodore Matthew Perry arrived at Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open its ports. In 1854 the shogunate signed the Treaty of Kanagawa, and in 1858 the Harris Treaty and treaties with European powers, opening ports, setting low tariffs and granting extraterritoriality. These "unequal treaties" humiliated Japan and discredited the shogunate.

## Revere the emperor

Opponents of the shogunate, especially samurai from the domains of Satsuma and Chōshū, in south-western Japan, rallied under the slogan sonnō jōi, "revere the emperor, expel the barbarians". Attacks on foreigners led to British bombardment of Kagoshima in 1863 and Western naval attacks on Chōshū in 1864, which convinced these domains that Japan needed Western technology. Satsuma and Chōshū allied in 1866.

## The restoration

In 1867 the young Mutsuhito became emperor. The last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, resigned his powers in November 1867, hoping to keep influence. On 3 January 1868, Satsuma and Chōshū forces seized the imperial palace in Kyoto and proclaimed the restoration of imperial rule. In the Boshin War of 1868–1869, the new government defeated the shogunate's supporters, including the last holdouts in Hokkaido. The emperor's reign was named Meiji, "enlightened rule", and he moved to Edo, renamed Tokyo.

## Reforms

The new government, led by former samurai such as Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Saigō Takamori and Itō Hirobumi, set out to make Japan strong enough to resist Western powers, under the slogan "rich country, strong army". The Charter Oath of 1868 promised that knowledge would be sought throughout the world. In 1871 the feudal domains were abolished and replaced by prefectures governed by appointed officials. The samurai class was abolished: they lost their stipends and the right to carry swords. A national army based on conscription was created in 1873. A land tax in money provided revenue. Compulsory education was introduced in 1872. The government built railways, telegraphs, shipyards, mines and factories, often later sold to private companies, the zaibatsu, such as Mitsubishi. It hired thousands of foreign experts and sent students abroad. The Iwakura Mission toured the United States and Europe in 1871–1873.

## Resistance

Not everyone accepted the changes. In 1877 Saigō Takamori led a rebellion of discontented samurai in Satsuma, which was crushed by the conscript army. Peasants rioted against conscription and taxes. A movement for freedom and people's rights demanded a parliament.

## The constitution

In 1889 the Meiji Constitution created a parliament, the Diet, while leaving great power to the emperor and his advisers. By 1911 Japan had revised the unequal treaties, defeated China in 1895 and Russia in 1905, and annexed Korea in 1910, becoming an imperial power.
