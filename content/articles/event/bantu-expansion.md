The Bantu expansion was the gradual spread of peoples speaking Bantu languages from an area in west-central Africa, around today's Cameroon and Nigeria, across most of central, eastern and southern Africa, over roughly three thousand years, from about 3000 BCE to 500 CE. Today, over 350 million people speak more than 500 Bantu languages, including Swahili, Zulu, Kikongo and Shona.

## How we know

The Bantu expansion is reconstructed from three kinds of evidence. Linguists noticed in the nineteenth century that languages across a vast area share similar words and grammar, such as noun classes marked by prefixes; the word bantu means "people" in many of them, from ba-, a plural prefix, and -ntu, "person". Comparing languages allows reconstruction of their family tree and of words in the ancestral language, Proto-Bantu, which show what its speakers knew, such as words for yams, goats, boats and pottery. Archaeology traces pottery styles, iron working and settlements, and genetics shows that Bantu speakers across the continent share ancestry from west-central Africa, mixed with local peoples.

## Origins

Proto-Bantu was probably spoken around 3000 to 2000 BCE in the grasslands of western Cameroon and south-eastern Nigeria. Its speakers were farmers, growing yams and oil palms, fishing and keeping goats, and making pottery.

## The migration

Bantu-speaking peoples moved south and east over many centuries, not in a single migration but in a series of movements, as farming communities expanded. One route went south through the equatorial rainforest, along rivers, perhaps when the forest shrank during a drier period around 2500 years ago, reaching the Congo basin and Angola. Another went east around the north of the forest, reaching the Great Lakes region of East Africa around 500 BCE, where Bantu speakers adopted cereal crops, such as sorghum and millet, and cattle from other peoples. From there they spread south, through today's Zambia, Malawi, Mozambique and Zimbabwe, reaching South Africa around 300 to 500 CE.

## Iron

Iron working spread with the Bantu expansion in eastern and southern Africa, though it developed separately in several parts of Africa. In the Great Lakes region, iron smelting began around 500 BCE, associated with Urewe pottery. Iron tools helped clear forest and cultivate land.

## Encounters

As Bantu speakers spread, they met other peoples, such as Pygmy hunter-gatherers in the rainforest, Khoisan peoples in southern and eastern Africa, and Cushitic and Nilotic herders in East Africa. Some were absorbed, others pushed to marginal lands, and there was exchange of languages and technologies; Zulu and Xhosa, for example, include click sounds borrowed from Khoisan languages. Bananas, originally from Southeast Asia, reached Africa and became important crops.

## Legacy

The Bantu expansion shaped the linguistic and cultural map of sub-Saharan Africa. Later Bantu-speaking societies built states such as Kongo, Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe, Buganda and Mutapa, and the Swahili city-states of the East African coast, whose language became a lingua franca. Historians debate the details, timing and routes of the expansion, and the old idea of a single mass migration has been replaced by a more complex picture.

## On our maps

Our earliest maps, from 2000 BCE to around 500 CE, show the gradual spread of farming peoples across central and southern Africa, which later gave rise to states such as the [Kingdom of Kongo](/place/kingdom-of-kongo) and [Great Zimbabwe](/place/kingdom-of-great-zimbabwe).
