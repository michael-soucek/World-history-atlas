The Spanish American wars of independence were a series of wars from 1808 to 1826 in which Spain's colonies in the Americas, from Mexico to Argentina, won their independence. They created most of the countries of Latin America.

## Causes

By the late eighteenth century, the Spanish American colonies were ruled through viceroyalties, with high officials mostly born in Spain, peninsulares. The criollos, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas, resented their exclusion from top positions and the restrictions of the Bourbon reforms, which increased taxes and tightened control. Enlightenment ideas, the American Revolution and the French Revolution inspired some. Indigenous and mixed-race populations, mestizos, and enslaved and free people of African descent had their own grievances; the great revolt of Túpac Amaru II in Peru in 1780–1781 had frightened the elites.

## Napoleon and the juntas

In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and forced King Ferdinand VII to abdicate, placing his brother Joseph on the throne. In the colonies, as in Spain, local juntas formed to govern in Ferdinand's name. In 1810 juntas took power in Caracas, Buenos Aires, Bogotá and Santiago. These movements gradually moved towards independence.

## Mexico

In Mexico, on 16 September 1810, the priest Miguel Hidalgo issued the Grito de Dolores, calling for an uprising. Tens of thousands of Indigenous and mestizo peasants joined him, sacking Guanajuato. Hidalgo was captured and executed in 1811. Another priest, José María Morelos, continued the struggle and declared independence in 1813, but was executed in 1815. In 1821, the royalist officer Agustín de Iturbide joined the rebel Vicente Guerrero, and Mexico became independent, with Iturbide briefly as emperor.

## The north: Bolívar

In northern South America, Simón Bolívar led the struggle. The first Venezuelan republics were crushed, but Bolívar crossed the Andes in 1819 and defeated the royalists at Boyacá, founding Gran Colombia, which included Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador and Panama. He won at Carabobo in 1821, and his general Antonio José de Sucre at Pichincha in 1822.

## The south: San Martín

In the south, Buenos Aires had been independent in practice since 1810, and the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata declared independence in 1816. José de San Martín, an Argentine officer, led the Army of the Andes across the mountains into Chile in 1817, winning at Chacabuco and, in 1818, at Maipú; Chile declared independence under Bernardo O'Higgins. San Martín then sailed to Peru, with a fleet commanded by the Scottish Lord Cochrane, and declared Peru's independence in 1821.

## Peru

After San Martín met Bolívar at Guayaquil in 1822, he withdrew, and Bolívar completed the liberation of Peru, the royalist stronghold. Sucre's victory at Ayacucho on 9 December 1824 effectively ended Spanish rule in South America. Upper Peru became Bolivia in 1825. The last Spanish garrisons surrendered in 1826.

## Aftermath

Spain recognised the new states only gradually. Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish until 1898. Brazil became independent from Portugal more peacefully, in 1822. The new nations faced civil wars, regional conflicts, the power of military leaders, caudillos, and economic difficulties. Slavery was abolished in most of the new countries by the 1850s.
