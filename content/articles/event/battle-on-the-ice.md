On 5 April 1242, at Lake Peipus, not far from the town of Dorpat, today's Tartu in [Estonia](/country/estonia), Prince [Alexander Nevsky](/person/alexander-nevsky) led the forces of Novgorod and Vladimir-Suzdal to victory over the Livonian Order and the bishop of Dorpat. Russian tradition came to see the clash as a defence of Orthodox Christianity against Catholic crusaders. Modern scholars debate how large and how decisive it really was, and much of its popular image, including knights plunging through breaking ice, is a much later invention.

## A contested frontier

From the late twelfth century German missionaries and crusaders pushed into Livonia and Estonia, lands where the [Novgorod Republic](/place/novgorod-republic) had long sought tribute. In 1224 the Sword Brothers took Yuryev, which became the seat of the bishopric of Dorpat. Local politics were tangled by family ties: an exiled Pskov prince, Yaroslav, was the brother-in-law of Bishop Hermann of Dorpat, and with his help he seized Pskov in September 1240. That winter, forces of the bishop of Ösel–Wiek and the Livonian Order also moved into Votia, a region near Novgorod.

Historians once linked these events to a supposed Swedish attack on the Neva in July 1240, from which Alexander later took his name, as parts of a single papal plan against the Rus'. More recent work finds little evidence for a coordinated campaign and treats the Neva fight, if it happened, as a minor clash.

## Alexander's counterattack

After internal disputes delayed a response, Alexander struck back in 1241, recovering Votia and Pskov. He then carried the war into the bishopric's lands. In spring 1242 one of his detachments was defeated about 20 kilometres south of Dorpat, and he took up a position by Lake Peipus.

## The battle

The earliest account, the Livonian Rhymed Chronicle of the 1290s, describes a small crusader force attacking a much larger Rus' army rich in archers. The knights were surrounded and overwhelmed; twenty brothers of the Order were killed and six captured, while some of the Dorpat men escaped. The Novgorod First Chronicle, from around 1350, places the fight by the lake at Raven's Rock and says the Germans and Estonians charged in a wedge, were routed and pursued across the ice, with 400 Germans killed, 50 captured and countless Estonians dead.

Modern estimates vary greatly. One conservative reconstruction by David Nicolle suggests about 2,600 men on the crusader side, including Estonian infantry and Danish and German knights, against roughly 5,000 under Alexander. In this account the knights' charge stalled against the Novgorod militia, and after two hours of fighting Alexander sent in his wings, driving the exhausted attackers back across the ice.

## Debating the legend

In 1983 the historian John Fennell argued that the battle was smaller and less important than claimed, noting how few knights died according to the Order's own chronicle. Donald Ostrowski has shown that the earliest sources say nothing of ice breaking: the Rhymed Chronicle even has the dead falling on grass. Only the fifteenth-century Life of Alexander Nevsky placed the fighting itself on the frozen lake, and the drowning knights first appeared in Sergei Eisenstein's 1938 film Alexander Nevsky. Underwater searches in 1958 and 1959 found no relics of the battle.

## Legacy

The defeat stopped the crusaders from retaking Pskov, and Lake Peipus and the Narva river became a lasting line between Catholic and Orthodox Christendom. The Russian Orthodox Church canonised Alexander in 1547. During the Second World War he became a Soviet symbol of resistance to Germany, with an order named after him founded in 1942, and [Russia](/country/russia) still marks the victory as a day of military honour.
