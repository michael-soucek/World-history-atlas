On 23 April 1014, the High King [Brian Boru](/person/brian-boru) fought a day-long battle at Clontarf, near Dublin, against the king of Leinster, the Norse king of Dublin and Viking warriors from Orkney and the Isle of Man. Brian's army won, but he, his son and his grandson were all killed. Long remembered as the battle that ended Viking power in [Ireland](/country/ireland), it is now also seen by many historians as an Irish struggle for supremacy in which Norsemen fought on both sides.

## Brian's rise

Vikings had raided Ireland since the late eighth century and fortified Dublin in 838. By the tenth century Dublin was a wealthy kingdom whose rulers had interests across the Irish Sea, and many of its settlers had merged into Gaelic society. Meanwhile the Dál gCais, a small dynasty from what is now County Clare, were expanding. Brian claimed the kingship of Munster in 976, forced Leinster to submit and contested the high kingship with Máel Sechnaill of the Uí Néill. Together they crushed Dublin and Leinster at Glenmama in 999, but Brian then turned on his partner, and after taking the hostages of Connacht and Meath at Athlone in 1002 he was unchallenged High King. By 1011 the northern kingdoms had also submitted.

## The revolt

Brian's authority soon came under strain. In 1012 and 1013 fighting broke out in the north, and Sigtrygg Silkbeard, king of Dublin, joined his uncle Máel Mórda, king of Leinster, in raiding Meath. Brian brought an army to Leinster in 1013 and camped outside Dublin from September to the end of the year. Sigtrygg sought help overseas from Sigurd, earl of Orkney, and Brodir of Mann; according to the Icelandic Njáls saga, he promised each of them the kingship of Ireland. Their fleets reached Dublin in Holy Week 1014, and Brian marched on the town with the men of Munster, two Connacht kings and Máel Sechnaill.

## The battle

The contemporary annals name only the leaders who died, so most detail comes from the later Irish text Cogad Gáedel re Gallaib. In its account Brian, by then in his seventies, stayed in his tent to pray while his son Murchad commanded the front. The overseas Vikings, in mail armour, formed the front of the opposing army, with the men of Dublin and then Leinster behind them. Sigtrygg watched from Dublin's walls with his wife, who was Brian's daughter.

The fighting began at dawn and went on until evening. The Irish, though unarmoured, pressed the Vikings with thrown spears and greater numbers. When the Dublin and Leinster lines finally broke, the rising tide cut off their retreat and carried away their ships, and many drowned. Brian's fifteen-year-old grandson Toirdelbach drowned while chasing them into the sea. Murchad killed Sigurd but was himself killed, and Brodir found Brian in his tent and slew him before being killed in turn. Máel Mórda also died. Estimates of the dead range from 7,000 to 10,000.

## Aftermath

Brian's body was carried to Armagh and buried after twelve days of mourning. Máel Sechnaill regained the high kingship and held it until his death in 1022, while Brian's surviving sons fought over his inheritance. Sigtrygg remained king of Dublin until 1036, but the town was now a lesser power, and in 1052 a king of Leinster captured it, asserting Irish overlordship over the Norse of Ireland.

## Memory and debate

The traditional view, especially popular during British rule, cast Clontarf as a national victory over foreign invaders and Brian as its hero. Revisionist historians stress instead that it was largely a civil war between Munster and its allies and the rebels of Leinster and Dublin. A 2018 network analysis of the Cogad broadly supported the older view but found the picture more complicated than a simple Irish-against-Viking conflict.
