The decolonisation of Africa was the process by which most African countries gained independence from European colonial rule, mainly between the late 1950s and the mid-1970s. In just two decades, over fifty new states emerged, ending the colonial system imposed during the Scramble for Africa in the late nineteenth century.

## Colonial Africa

By 1914, almost all of Africa was ruled by European powers, Britain, France, Portugal, Belgium, Germany, Italy and Spain, except Liberia and Ethiopia. Colonial rule exploited African land, labour and resources, imposed borders, and introduced new administrations, economies, languages and religions. Africans resisted colonial rule from the start.

## The rise of nationalism

The Second World War weakened the European powers, and hundreds of thousands of African soldiers fought for them. The Atlantic Charter of 1941 proclaimed the right of peoples to choose their governments. The United States and the Soviet Union opposed old-style colonialism, and Indian independence in 1947 inspired African nationalists. The Pan-African Congress in Manchester in 1945, attended by leaders such as Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta, demanded independence. Educated African elites founded political parties, and trade unions and veterans joined the movement.

## The first independences

Libya became independent in 1951, and Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia in 1956. In 1957 the Gold Coast, under Kwame Nkrumah, became the first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence from Britain, as Ghana. Guinea, under Ahmed Sékou Touré, voted in 1958 to reject the French Community and became independent. In 1960, called the "Year of Africa", seventeen countries became independent, including Nigeria, the Belgian Congo and most of the French colonies in West and Central Africa.

## Peaceful and violent paths

Some transitions were negotiated peacefully, while others involved war. In Kenya, the British fought the Mau Mau uprising from 1952 to 1960, detaining tens of thousands in camps, where many were abused; Kenya became independent in 1963. In Algeria, a French colony with about a million European settlers, the National Liberation Front fought a brutal war from 1954 to 1962, in which hundreds of thousands died, before independence. The Congo's sudden independence from Belgium in 1960 led to a crisis, with secession in Katanga, United Nations intervention and the murder of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba in 1961.

## Portugal and southern Africa

Portugal fought long wars against independence movements in Angola, Mozambique and Guinea-Bissau from 1961. After a revolution in Portugal in 1974, its colonies became independent in 1974–1975; Angola and Mozambique then suffered long civil wars, fuelled by the Cold War. In Rhodesia, the white minority declared independence from Britain in 1965 to avoid majority rule; after a guerrilla war, it became independent as Zimbabwe in 1980. Namibia, ruled by South Africa, became independent in 1990, and apartheid in South Africa ended in 1994.

## After independence

The new states faced many challenges: borders drawn by colonial powers, which often divided or combined ethnic groups, economies dependent on exporting raw materials, few trained administrators, and Cold War interventions. Many fell under one-party rule or military coups. In 1963 the Organisation of African Unity was founded, which accepted colonial borders. Debates continue about the legacies of colonialism.
