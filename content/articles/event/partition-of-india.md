The Partition of India in August 1947 divided British India into two independent states, India and Pakistan, based on religious majorities. The partition, which also divided the provinces of Punjab and Bengal, was accompanied by one of the largest migrations in history, in which about 15 million people were displaced, and by massive violence that killed perhaps several hundred thousand to two million people.

## Towards independence

By the 1940s, the independence movement led by the Indian National Congress, under leaders such as Mahatma Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, was demanding Indian independence. The All-India Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, argued that Muslims, a minority of about a quarter of the population, needed protection, and in the Lahore Resolution of 1940 called for independent states in Muslim-majority areas, an idea that became Pakistan. Britain, weakened by the Second World War, decided to leave India. In 1946 negotiations for a united India failed. On 16 August 1946, the Muslim League called for "Direct Action Day"; riots in Calcutta killed several thousand people, and violence spread.

## The plan

In February 1947, the British government announced that it would leave India by June 1948. The last viceroy, Lord Louis Mountbatten, arrived in March and concluded that partition was unavoidable. On 3 June 1947 he announced a plan to partition India, and brought the date of independence forward to August 1947. The British lawyer Cyril Radcliffe, who had never been to India, was given five weeks to draw the borders dividing Punjab and Bengal. The boundaries were announced on 17 August, after independence.

## Independence

Pakistan became independent on 14 August 1947, with Jinnah as governor-general, made up of two parts, West Pakistan and East Bengal, later East Pakistan, separated by about 1,600 kilometres of India. India became independent at midnight on 15 August, when Nehru spoke of India's "tryst with destiny".

## Violence and migration

As the borders became known, millions of Hindus and Sikhs fled from Pakistan to India, and Muslims fled from India to Pakistan, on foot, in bullock carts and by train. In Punjab, especially, mobs and armed groups of all communities carried out massacres, rapes and abductions; trains arrived full of dead passengers. Tens of thousands of women were abducted. Gandhi fasted to stop the violence in Calcutta and Delhi. Refugee camps housed millions. Estimates of deaths range widely, from about 200,000 to 2 million.

## The princely states

The over 500 princely states, ruled by local princes under British protection, had to join India or Pakistan. Most joined India. The Hindu ruler of Kashmir, a Muslim-majority state, hesitated; when Pakistani tribal forces invaded in October 1947, he acceded to India, leading to the first war between India and Pakistan. Kashmir remains disputed. India annexed Hyderabad by force in 1948.

## Legacy

Gandhi was assassinated in January 1948 by a Hindu nationalist. India and Pakistan have fought several wars, and in 1971 East Pakistan became independent as Bangladesh, after a war. Partition remains a source of trauma and of tension between India and Pakistan, and survivors' memories have been recorded in oral history projects.
