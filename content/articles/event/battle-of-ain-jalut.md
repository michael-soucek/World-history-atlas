The Battle of Ain Jalut, fought on 3 September 1260 in the Jezreel Valley, in today's Israel, was the first major defeat of the Mongols in a pitched battle. The Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt defeated a Mongol army, halting the Mongol advance into the Middle East and securing Egypt and Syria for the Mamluks.

## The Mongol advance

In 1256 Hülegü, a grandson of Genghis Khan, led a great Mongol army west to complete the conquest of the Islamic world. He destroyed the Nizari Ismailis, the "Assassins", in Iran, and in February 1258 sacked Baghdad, killing the last Abbasid caliph and much of the population. In 1260 he invaded Syria, taking Aleppo and Damascus, with the help of Christian allies, such as the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia and the Crusader principality of Antioch. The Ayyubid ruler of Syria, an-Nasir Yusuf, fled.

## The Mamluks

Egypt was ruled by the Mamluks, slave soldiers, mostly Kipchak Turks from the steppe, bought as boys and trained as elite cavalry. In 1250 they had seized power from the Ayyubids. In 1259 one of their leaders, Qutuz, became sultan. Hülegü sent envoys demanding Egypt's submission. Qutuz executed them.

## Hülegü withdraws

In 1260 news arrived that the Great Khan Möngke had died. Hülegü withdrew most of his army to Azerbaijan, partly because of the succession struggle and partly because Syria's pastures could not support a large Mongol army. He left a smaller force, perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 men, under his general Kitbuqa, a Nestorian Christian.

## The battle

Qutuz marched north from Egypt with an army of similar or larger size, including Mamluks and other troops. The Crusaders at Acre allowed the Mamluks to pass through their territory and buy supplies, remaining neutral. One of Qutuz's commanders, Baybars, who had earlier fought the Crusaders, led the vanguard. At Ain Jalut, "Goliath's spring", the Mamluks met the Mongols. According to accounts, Baybars lured the Mongols into an ambush with a feigned retreat, a tactic often used by the Mongols themselves. The Mongols charged and almost broke the Mamluk left wing, but Qutuz rallied his troops, reportedly throwing off his helmet and crying "O Islam!". The Mamluks counter-attacked and defeated the Mongols. Kitbuqa was killed. The Mamluks may have used hand cannons, an early use of gunpowder weapons, to frighten Mongol horses, though this is uncertain.

## Aftermath

The Mamluks retook Damascus and Aleppo. On the way back to Egypt, Baybars and other emirs assassinated Qutuz, and Baybars became sultan, ruling until 1277. He fought the Mongols and the Crusaders, taking Antioch in 1268.

## Significance

Ain Jalut is often described as one of the most important battles in history, as it stopped the Mongols from conquering Egypt and North Africa. The Mongols returned several times but were defeated again, at Homs in 1281 and Marj al-Saffar in 1303, though they won at Wadi al-Khazandar in 1299. The Euphrates became the frontier between the Mamluks and the Mongol Ilkhanate. Historians note that the Mongols at Ain Jalut were a reduced force and that the Mongol Empire was fragmenting, which contributed to the outcome. The Mamluks ruled Egypt and Syria until the Ottoman conquest of 1517, and their victory made Cairo the centre of the Arab Islamic world.

## On our maps

The battle falls between our 1200 and 1279 maps. On the 1279 map, the [Mamluk Sultanate](/place/mamluk-sultanate) holds Egypt and Syria, and the [Ilkhanate](/place/ilkhanate) rules Iran and Iraq.
