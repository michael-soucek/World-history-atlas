The Reconquista, "reconquest", is the name given to the series of campaigns by Christian kingdoms in the Iberian Peninsula to conquer the territories ruled by Muslims, from the eighth century to the fall of Granada in 1492. The term itself, implying a recovery of lost lands, is debated by historians.

## Al-Andalus

In 711 an army of Arabs and Berbers from North Africa, led by Tariq ibn Ziyad, crossed the Strait of Gibraltar, named after him, Jabal Tariq, and defeated the Visigothic king Roderic. Within a few years, most of the peninsula came under Muslim rule, called al-Andalus. Under the Umayyad emirate and, from 929, caliphate of Córdoba, al-Andalus became one of the most advanced societies in Europe, with great cities, such as Córdoba, with its Great Mosque, scholarship, agriculture and trade. Muslims, Christians and Jews lived together, with Christians and Jews as protected but subordinate communities.

## Asturias

In the mountains of the north, Christian resistance survived. According to tradition, around 722, a Visigothic noble, Pelagius, defeated a Muslim force at Covadonga and founded the Kingdom of Asturias, later León. Other Christian states emerged: Navarre, Aragon, the County of Barcelona and, in the tenth century, Castile, which became a kingdom. The discovery of the supposed tomb of the apostle James at Santiago de Compostela in the ninth century made it a great pilgrimage centre, and Saint James became the patron of the Christian struggle.

## Advance

In the early eleventh century, the Caliphate of Córdoba collapsed into small states, the taifas, which paid tribute to the Christian kings. In 1085 Alfonso VI of Castile took Toledo, the old Visigothic capital. The taifas called for help from the Almoravids, a Berber dynasty from North Africa, who defeated Alfonso at Sagrajas in 1086. The Castilian knight Rodrigo Díaz, El Cid, who served both Christian and Muslim rulers, conquered Valencia in 1094. Portugal became a kingdom and took Lisbon in 1147, with help from crusaders.

## Las Navas de Tolosa

In the twelfth century, another North African dynasty, the Almohads, took over al-Andalus and defeated Castile at Alarcos in 1195. In 1212 an alliance of Castile, Aragon, Navarre and Portugal, with crusaders, defeated the Almohads at Las Navas de Tolosa, a decisive victory. In the following decades, Ferdinand III of Castile took Córdoba in 1236 and Seville in 1248, and James I of Aragon took Majorca and Valencia. Portugal completed its conquest with the Algarve in 1249.

## Granada

Only the Nasrid emirate of Granada remained, paying tribute to Castile, for over two centuries. Granada's rulers built the Alhambra palace. After the union of Castile and Aragon through the marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand, the Catholic Monarchs, they waged a ten-year war, and Granada surrendered on 2 January 1492.

## Aftermath

The surrender treaty guaranteed Muslims freedom of religion, but within a decade Muslims were forced to convert or leave. In 1492 Jews were expelled. The Moriscos, converted Muslims, were expelled in 1609–1614. Historians debate whether the Reconquista was a continuous religious crusade, as later nationalist history presented it, or a more complex process of conquest, alliance and coexistence.
