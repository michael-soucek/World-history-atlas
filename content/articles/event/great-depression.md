The Great Depression was a severe worldwide economic depression that began with the stock market crash in the United States in October 1929 and lasted, in most countries, through the 1930s. It was the longest and deepest economic downturn of the twentieth century, causing mass unemployment and poverty, and it contributed to political extremism and the rise of Nazism.

## The crash

In the 1920s the American economy boomed, and stock prices rose rapidly, fuelled by speculation and buying shares with borrowed money. On 24 October 1929, "Black Thursday", prices began to fall sharply, and on 28 and 29 October, "Black Monday" and "Black Tuesday", the market collapsed. By 1932 shares had lost about 89 percent of their 1929 value.

## The depression

The crash was followed by a deep depression. Consumer spending and investment fell. Thousands of American banks failed in waves of panics from 1930 to 1933, wiping out savings, as there was no deposit insurance. The money supply contracted sharply, and prices fell. Between 1929 and 1933, American industrial production fell by nearly half, and unemployment rose to about 25 percent. Homeless people lived in shanty towns, called "Hoovervilles" after President Herbert Hoover. Farmers, already suffering low prices, were hit by drought and dust storms in the Great Plains, the Dust Bowl, and many migrated to California, as depicted in John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath.

## Spreading worldwide

The depression spread around the world through trade and finance. American banks recalled loans from Europe, especially Germany, which depended on them. In 1930 the United States raised tariffs with the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, and other countries retaliated, causing world trade to fall by about two-thirds. In 1931 the failure of Austria's largest bank, the Creditanstalt, set off a financial crisis in Europe. Britain left the gold standard in 1931. Countries that exported raw materials, such as Australia, Argentina and Brazil, were hard hit by falling prices.

## Causes

Economists debate the causes. Keynesians emphasise the collapse in spending and demand. Monetarists, such as Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, blamed the Federal Reserve for allowing the money supply to shrink and banks to fail. Many economists emphasise the gold standard, which spread deflation between countries and prevented central banks from responding; countries that left gold earlier recovered sooner.

## Responses

In the United States, Franklin D. Roosevelt, elected in 1932, launched the New Deal from 1933, with bank reform, deposit insurance, public works, farm support, social security and labour rights. In Britain, a National Government cut spending. In Sweden, the Social Democrats introduced policies of public spending. The economist John Maynard Keynes, in his General Theory of 1936, argued that governments should increase spending during depressions to support demand.

## Political consequences

The depression brought political turmoil. In Germany, where unemployment reached about six million, support for the Nazi Party rose, and Hitler came to power in January 1933. In Japan, militarists gained influence. Democracies were weakened in many countries, while dictatorships spread. Recovery was slow; it was the massive spending of the Second World War that ended high unemployment in the United States. The depression shaped economic policy for decades, leading to greater government involvement in the economy and international institutions, such as the International Monetary Fund and World Bank, created in 1944.
