The Self-Strengthening Movement, also called the Western Affairs Movement, was a programme of reforms in late [Qing](/place/qing-dynasty) China lasting roughly from 1861 to 1895. Shaken by defeat in the [Second Opium War](/event/second-opium-war) and by the [Taiping Rebellion](/event/taiping-rebellion), officials set out to adopt Western weapons, industry and technical knowledge while keeping China's Confucian political order intact. The movement built arsenals, shipyards, schools, mines and telegraphs, but its limits were exposed by defeat at the hands of Japan in 1894–1895.

## Origins

By 1860 defeat by Britain and France and the successes of the Taiping rebels had forced the court to recognise a crisis. In 1861 Prince Gong and the Grand Councillor Wenxiang set up the Zongli Yamen, in effect a foreign ministry, and in July that year Prince Gong announced imperial approval for buying foreign arms. The scholar Feng Guifen urged learning from Western technology in essays presented to Zeng Guofan in 1861. Zeng and other Han Chinese provincial officials had already raised regional armies, such as the Xiang Army, armed with Western weapons, and these forces defeated the Taiping by 1864.

## First phase, 1861–1872

The first phase focused on weapons, translation and training. The Tongwen Guan, a school of foreign languages for future diplomats, opened in Beijing in 1862, with similar schools in Shanghai, Guangzhou and Fuzhou, and it added astronomy and mathematics in 1867. Zeng Guofan and [Li Hongzhang](/person/li-hongzhang) founded the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai, which also translated 143 Western books between 1868 and 1879. Zuo Zongtang created the Fuzhou Navy Yard with French help, along with a naval school in 1866. A foreign-run Imperial Maritime Customs Service, led from 1863 by Robert Hart, gave the court a growing income. Yet the arsenals were costly: Jiangnan's first rifles were dearer and worse than imported ones.

## Second phase, 1872–1885

After the Tianjin riots of 1870, Li Hongzhang became the movement's dominant figure, and by one estimate over 90 per cent of its projects were launched under his patronage. Attention turned to profit-making enterprises run by merchants under official supervision, including the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company (1872), the Kaiping coal mines (1877), and a telegraph administration (1881). The Chinese Educational Mission sent students to study in the United States from 1872 until conservatives ended it in 1881, and naval cadets went to Britain and France. Regional armies held their own against France on land in the Sino-French War of 1884–1885, though China's navy remained its weak point.

## Third phase, 1885–1895

In the final decade a Navy Board tried to coordinate defence, and Li built up the Beiyang Fleet, at the time regarded as the strongest in East Asia. In Hubei, Zhang Zhidong founded ironworks and textile mills and began minting silver "dragon dollars" in 1889. But reform enthusiasm had faded at court. [Empress Dowager Cixi](/person/empress-dowager-cixi) had ousted Prince Gong in 1884, conservatives distrusted Western learning, and power and revenue lay with rival provincial strongmen whose fleets and armies rarely cooperated.

## Defeat and judgement

The [First Sino-Japanese War](/event/first-sino-japanese-war) of 1894–1895 shattered the Beiyang Fleet and ended the movement. Historians remain divided. Critics such as Immanuel Hsu argue that it borrowed Western technology without the institutions behind it, in contrast to Japan's [Meiji Restoration](/event/meiji-restoration), and was hampered by weak central control, scarce capital and corruption. Others stress real achievements in industry, education and the spread of Western knowledge, and see it as preparing the way for later reforms such as the [Hundred Days' Reform](/event/hundred-days-reform) of 1898.
