The Siege of Baghdad in 1258 was the capture and sack of the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate by the Mongol army of Hülegü, grandson of Genghis Khan. The city fell on 10 February 1258 after a siege of about two weeks; the last Abbasid caliph, al-Musta'sim, was executed, and much of the population was killed. The event ended the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad, which had lasted five centuries.

## Baghdad

Baghdad, founded by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur in 762, had been one of the largest and richest cities in the world and a great centre of learning, home to the House of Wisdom. By the thirteenth century, the caliphs had lost most of their political power, ruling only central Iraq, but they remained the symbolic leaders of Sunni Islam, and Baghdad was still a large and wealthy city.

## Hülegü's campaign

In 1253 the Great Khan Möngke sent his brother Hülegü with a large army to the west, to destroy the Nizari Ismailis and subdue the caliphate. In 1256 Hülegü took the Ismaili castles, including Alamut. He then demanded that the caliph al-Musta'sim submit and come in person. The caliph, poorly advised and overconfident, refused and threatened him, believing that the Muslim world would rise to defend him, but he had a weak army and did little to prepare.

## The siege

Hülegü's army, perhaps over 100,000 strong, including Mongol, Turkic, Georgian and Armenian troops, and Chinese engineers with siege machines, advanced on Baghdad in late 1257. In January 1258 the Mongols defeated the caliph's army outside the city, breaking dykes to flood its camp. They surrounded Baghdad on both banks of the Tigris and bombarded the walls with catapults. The city surrendered on 10 February 1258.

## The sack

The Mongols entered Baghdad on 13 February and sacked it for about a week. Much of the population was massacred; contemporary sources give figures from 90,000 to 800,000 or more, probably exaggerated, but the killing was immense. Mosques, palaces, libraries and hospitals were destroyed. According to accounts, so many books were thrown into the Tigris that the river ran black with ink. The Christian population, protected by Hülegü's Christian wife Doquz Khatun, was mostly spared.

## The caliph's death

The caliph was forced to reveal his treasures. According to most accounts, he was then executed by being rolled in a carpet and trampled by horses, because the Mongols believed that spilling royal blood brought misfortune. Most of his family was killed.

## Aftermath

Hülegü founded the Ilkhanate, a Mongol state ruling Iran and Iraq. The Mongols advanced into Syria, but were defeated by the Mamluks at Ain Jalut in 1260. A member of the Abbasid family escaped to Cairo, where the Mamluks maintained a line of "shadow caliphs" until the Ottoman conquest in 1517. Baghdad never regained its former importance, and its irrigation system was damaged. The fall of Baghdad is often seen as the end of the Islamic Golden Age, though historians note that intellectual life continued in other centres, and that the Ilkhans later converted to Islam and patronised scholars, such as the astronomer Nasir al-Din al-Tusi.
