The Cuban Missile Crisis was a thirteen-day confrontation in October 1962 between the United States and the Soviet Union over Soviet nuclear missiles secretly placed in Cuba. It is generally regarded as the moment when the Cold War came closest to a full-scale nuclear war.

## Background

In 1959 Fidel Castro's revolution took power in Cuba, about 150 kilometres from Florida, and moved towards the Soviet Union. In April 1961, a CIA-backed invasion by Cuban exiles at the Bay of Pigs failed, and the United States continued efforts to overthrow Castro, such as Operation Mongoose. The United States had deployed nuclear missiles in Turkey and Italy, near the Soviet Union, and had a large lead in nuclear weapons. In 1962 the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev decided to deploy nuclear missiles in Cuba, to protect Cuba from invasion and redress the strategic balance. Soviet ships carried medium- and intermediate-range missiles, nuclear warheads, bombers and about 40,000 troops to Cuba in secret.

## Discovery

On 14 October 1962, an American U-2 spy plane photographed missile sites under construction in Cuba. On 16 October President John F. Kennedy was informed and assembled a group of advisers, the Executive Committee, ExComm. Some, including the military chiefs, favoured air strikes and an invasion. Others favoured a naval blockade, which would give time for negotiation.

## The quarantine

Kennedy chose a blockade, called a "quarantine" to avoid the legal implications of the word blockade, an act of war. On 22 October, in a televised address, he informed the world of the missiles and announced the quarantine, warning that any missile launched from Cuba would be regarded as a Soviet attack on the United States, requiring a full retaliatory response. American forces went to DEFCON 2, the highest alert level ever reached. Soviet ships approached the quarantine line, and on 24 October some turned back. At the United Nations, the American ambassador Adlai Stevenson showed photographs of the missiles.

## The crisis peaks

On 26 October, Khrushchev sent a letter offering to remove the missiles if the United States pledged not to invade Cuba. On 27 October, "Black Saturday", a second, tougher letter demanded the removal of American missiles from Turkey. That day, a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson. American ships dropped practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine, B-59, whose commanders, out of contact with Moscow, considered launching a nuclear torpedo; according to later accounts, the flotilla's chief of staff, Vasili Arkhipov, opposed the launch.

## Resolution

Kennedy's administration replied publicly to the first letter, accepting the no-invasion pledge, while his brother, Attorney General Robert Kennedy, secretly told the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, that the missiles in Turkey would be removed later, if this was kept secret. On 28 October, Khrushchev announced on Radio Moscow that the missiles would be dismantled. The United States lifted the quarantine on 20 November. The missiles in Turkey were removed in 1963.

## Aftermath

The crisis frightened leaders on both sides. In 1963 a direct "hotline" was set up between Washington and Moscow, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere. Khrushchev's apparent retreat contributed to his removal in 1964. Castro was angry that he had not been consulted. Historians later learned that Soviet forces in Cuba had tactical nuclear weapons, unknown to the Americans at the time.
