The Protestant Reformation was a religious, political and cultural movement in sixteenth-century Europe that challenged the authority of the Catholic Church and led to the creation of Protestant churches. Beginning with Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses in 1517, it divided Western Christianity permanently and contributed to wars, new states and profound changes in European society.

## Background

By the early sixteenth century, many Christians criticised the Catholic Church for corruption, worldliness and abuses, such as the sale of church offices and of indulgences, which reduced punishment for sins. Earlier reformers, such as John Wycliffe in England and Jan Hus in Bohemia, burned in 1415, had challenged church authority. Humanist scholars, such as Erasmus, studied the Bible in its original languages and called for reform. The printing press, invented by Johannes Gutenberg around 1450, allowed ideas to spread rapidly.

## Luther

On 31 October 1517, Martin Luther, a German monk and professor at Wittenberg, published his Ninety-Five Theses against the sale of indulgences. He taught that salvation comes through faith alone, by God's grace, not through good works, and that the Bible alone, not the pope or church tradition, is the authority for Christians. He was excommunicated in 1521, and at the Diet of Worms he refused to recant before Emperor Charles V. Protected by the Elector of Saxony, he translated the Bible into German. Many German princes and cities adopted Lutheranism, partly for religious reasons and partly to gain control of church lands and independence from the emperor. The name "Protestant" came from the protest of Lutheran princes at the Diet of Speyer in 1529.

## Other reformers

In Zurich, Huldrych Zwingli led a more radical reform from 1519. In Geneva, John Calvin, a French theologian, established a reformed church from 1541, teaching predestination, that God has decided in advance who will be saved. Calvinism spread to France, where its followers were called Huguenots, the Netherlands, Scotland, under John Knox, and Hungary. The Anabaptists, who rejected infant baptism, were persecuted by both Catholics and Protestants.

## England

In England, King Henry VIII broke with Rome in 1534, when the pope refused to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, and made himself head of the Church of England. He dissolved the monasteries. Under his son Edward VI, the church became more Protestant; under Mary I it returned to Catholicism, and under Elizabeth I a Protestant settlement was established.

## The Catholic response

The Catholic Church responded with the Counter-Reformation, or Catholic Reformation. The Council of Trent, from 1545 to 1563, reaffirmed Catholic doctrine while reforming abuses, such as the sale of indulgences, and improving the education of priests. New religious orders, such as the Jesuits, founded by Ignatius of Loyola in 1540, spread Catholic education and missions. The Inquisition and the Index of Forbidden Books suppressed heresy.

## Wars of religion

Religious divisions led to wars. In Germany, the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 allowed each prince to choose the religion of his territory. In France, the Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, including the St Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, ended with the Edict of Nantes in 1598. The Dutch revolt against Spain combined religious and political causes. The Thirty Years' War, from 1618 to 1648, devastated Germany.

## Legacy

The Reformation contributed to the growth of literacy, as Protestants encouraged reading the Bible in vernacular languages, to the power of states over churches, and, eventually, to ideas of religious tolerance.
