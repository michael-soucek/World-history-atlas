The Hundred Years' War was a series of conflicts between the kings of England and France from 1337 to 1453, over the English kings' claims to the French throne and to lands in France. Despite great English victories, such as Crécy and Agincourt, France won, driving the English from all of France except Calais. The war helped shape the national identities of both countries.

## Causes

Since William the Conqueror, Duke of Normandy, became king of England in 1066, English kings had held lands in France as vassals of the French king. By the fourteenth century, they held Gascony, in south-western France. In 1328 the last Capetian king of France, Charles IV, died without a son. His nephew, Edward III of England, son of Charles's sister Isabella, had a claim, but the French nobles chose Philip VI of Valois, a cousin through the male line. In 1337 Philip confiscated Gascony, and Edward claimed the French throne, beginning the war. Disputes over Flanders, whose wool trade depended on England, and Scotland, allied with France, added to the tensions.

## Edwardian phase

The English won a great naval victory at Sluys in 1340. In 1346 Edward invaded Normandy and, at Crécy, English longbowmen destroyed the French cavalry. He took Calais in 1347 after a long siege. The Black Death, from 1348, interrupted the war. In 1356 Edward's son, the Black Prince, defeated and captured King John II of France at Poitiers. The Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 gave England a large part of south-western France. Armies of mercenaries, the "free companies", ravaged the French countryside, and peasants rose in the Jacquerie of 1358.

## French recovery

Under Charles V and his general Bertrand du Guesclin, the French avoided pitched battles and recovered most of the lost territory in the 1370s. England faced its own troubles, the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 and the deposition of Richard II. France suffered under Charles VI, who had episodes of madness, and a civil war between the Armagnacs and the Burgundians.

## Agincourt

Henry V of England invaded in 1415 and, at Agincourt, on 25 October, his small, exhausted army defeated a much larger French army, thanks to the longbowmen and the muddy ground. He conquered Normandy. By the Treaty of Troyes in 1420, Charles VI disinherited his son, the dauphin Charles, and recognised Henry as his heir, marrying him to his daughter Catherine. But Henry died in 1422, months before Charles VI, leaving his infant son, Henry VI, king of England and, according to the treaty, of France.

## Joan of Arc

The English and their Burgundian allies controlled northern France, including Paris. In 1428 they besieged Orléans. In 1429 Joan of Arc, a peasant girl who said she was guided by saints, inspired the French to lift the siege and took the dauphin to Reims to be crowned Charles VII. She was captured in 1430 and burned as a heretic by the English in 1431.

## French victory

In 1435 the Burgundians made peace with Charles VII. The French reorganised their army, with a permanent force and powerful artillery, under the brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau. They retook Paris in 1436, Normandy in 1450, after the battle of Formigny, and Gascony in 1453, after the battle of Castillon, where French cannon defeated the English. Only Calais remained English, until 1558.

## Consequences

The war devastated France, with armies and bands of soldiers looting the countryside. Military technology changed, as longbows and cannons reduced the dominance of armoured knights. The war strengthened royal power and national feeling in both countries, and English became the language of the English court. Soon after, England fell into the Wars of the Roses.
