The Satsuma Rebellion, known in Japan as the Seinan War or Southwestern War, was an uprising of former samurai against the new imperial government, fought on the island of Kyushu from 29 January to 24 September 1877. Led by Saigō Takamori, one of the heroes of the [Meiji Restoration](/event/meiji-restoration), it was the last and largest of several samurai revolts. Its defeat by a conscript army marked the end of the samurai as a fighting class, and it remains the most recent civil war fought in Japan.

## Discontent in Satsuma

The Satsuma domain, in the far south-west, had played a leading part in overthrowing the [Tokugawa shogunate](/place/tokugawa-shogunate) and in the [Boshin War](/event/boshin-war), and many of its men held high office in the new government. Yet the reforms that followed stripped the samurai of their privileges and income, and the rapid adoption of Western ways struck many of them as a betrayal. Saigō shared some of these grievances. In 1873 he pressed for war with Korea; when the government rejected the plan, he resigned and went home to Kagoshima, followed by many Satsuma officers and police.

## Saigō's academies

In 1874 Saigō founded a network of private schools in Kagoshima, which soon had over a hundred branches across the prefecture. Alongside the Chinese classics, students drilled with weapons and studied tactics, and the schools came to act as a political and military movement. With the backing of the local governor, their members took over much of the prefecture's administration, so that by the end of 1876 Satsuma was in effect beyond the control of Tokyo.

## The outbreak

Alarmed, the government sent police agents to investigate in December 1876. They were seized and, under torture, confessed to a plot to kill Saigō. Their leader later retracted the confession, but many in Satsuma believed it. When a government ship arrived at the end of January 1877 to remove weapons from the Kagoshima arsenal, students raided arsenals and naval yards. Saigō, reluctant at first, agreed to lead the movement and in February marched north, saying he meant to put questions to the government in Tokyo.

## Kumamoto and the long retreat

The rebels laid siege to Kumamoto Castle, held by about 3,800 soldiers and 600 police under General Tani Tateki, but could not take it. Local samurai joined Saigō, raising his force to about 20,000. Government troops landed behind the rebel lines, and after a hard-fought battle at Tabaruzaka in March, in which each side suffered more than 4,000 killed or wounded, relief forces reached the castle in mid-April. Saigō retreated south to Hitoyoshi and then to Miyazaki, fighting rearguard and guerrilla actions. By August his army had shrunk to some 3,000 men with almost no artillery. Surrounded near Mount Enodake, most surrendered or took their own lives, but Saigō slipped through with some 500 followers and reached Kagoshima on 1 September.

## Shiroyama

Saigō's men occupied the hill of Shiroyama above the city. The imperial forces under Yamagata Aritomo, vastly outnumbering them, surrounded the hill with trenches and shelled it, supported by warships in the harbour. On 24 September they attacked. Saigō was badly wounded by gunfire and was beheaded by his companion Beppu Shinsuke; tradition says he first committed ritual suicide, but this is doubted. His last followers died charging down the hill.

## Consequences

Government losses are given as about 15,000 killed and wounded, while the rebel army was almost destroyed. The war was ruinously expensive, forcing the government to print paper money and later to sell state enterprises cheaply to well-connected businessmen, which helped give rise to the great industrial conglomerates. The victory of conscripts with rifles and artillery showed that the age of the samurai was over. Saigō himself was pardoned after his death, in 1889, and became a popular tragic hero, honoured with a statue in Tokyo's Ueno Park.
