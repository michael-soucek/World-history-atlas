The fall of the Western Roman Empire was the process by which the Roman Empire lost control of its western provinces in the fifth century CE, and they were divided into kingdoms ruled by Germanic peoples. It is traditionally dated to 476, when the last emperor in the West, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed. The Eastern Roman Empire, based in Constantinople, survived for nearly another thousand years.

## The crisis of the third century

In the third century, the empire suffered a crisis: civil wars, with over twenty emperors in fifty years, invasions by Germanic peoples and the Sasanian Persians, plague, inflation and economic disruption. Diocletian, emperor from 284, restored order, reorganising the government, dividing the empire between several emperors, the tetrarchy, and enlarging the army. Constantine, who became sole emperor in 324, founded Constantinople as a new capital and supported Christianity.

## Division

After Theodosius I died in 395, the empire was permanently divided between his sons: Arcadius in the East, ruling from Constantinople, and Honorius in the West, whose capital moved from Milan to Ravenna in 402. The East was wealthier and more populous.

## The Goths

Around 376 the Goths, fleeing the Huns, who had arrived from the steppes, asked to settle within the empire. Mistreated by Roman officials, they rebelled and, in 378, at Adrianople, defeated and killed the eastern emperor Valens. Theodosius settled them as allies, foederati, under their own leaders. In 410 the Visigoths, under Alaric, sacked Rome, the first time in eight hundred years that the city had fallen to a foreign enemy. The event shocked the Roman world; Augustine of Hippo wrote The City of God in response.

## Invasions

On the last day of 406, Vandals, Alans and Suebi crossed the Rhine into Gaul and then Spain. Rome withdrew from Britain around 410. The Vandals crossed into North Africa in 429 and took Carthage in 439, depriving the West of its richest provinces and grain supply. The Huns under Attila invaded Gaul in 451, stopped by the general Aetius, and Italy in 452. The Vandals sacked Rome in 455. Visigoths, Burgundians, Franks and others established kingdoms in former Roman territories.

## The end in the West

Power in the West passed to generals, often of Germanic origin, such as Ricimer, who made and unmade emperors. In 476 the Germanic general Odoacer deposed the young emperor Romulus Augustulus and, instead of naming a new emperor, sent the imperial regalia to Constantinople, ruling Italy as king. Julius Nepos, a rival emperor recognised by the East, held out in Dalmatia until 480. In 493 Theodoric the Great, king of the Ostrogoths, killed Odoacer and ruled Italy.

## Why did it fall?

Historians have proposed hundreds of reasons for the fall: pressure from invasions, the loss of tax revenue, civil wars, the reliance on Germanic soldiers, economic decline, plague, climate change, the division of the empire and, as Edward Gibbon argued in the eighteenth century, the spread of Christianity, a view now rejected. Many historians emphasise the combination of external pressure and internal weakness.

## Fall or transformation?

Since the 1970s, many historians have described the period as a "transformation" of the Roman world into early medieval Europe, stressing continuity: the new kingdoms kept Roman law, Latin and the church. Others, such as Bryan Ward-Perkins, argue that the archaeological evidence shows a real collapse of living standards, trade and population. The Eastern Roman Empire continued until 1453.
