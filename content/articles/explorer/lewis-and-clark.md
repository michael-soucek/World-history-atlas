The Lewis and Clark Expedition, officially the Corps of Discovery, was a United States expedition from 1804 to 1806 led by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, which crossed the western part of North America from the Mississippi river to the Pacific Ocean and back. Commissioned by President Thomas Jefferson after the Louisiana Purchase, it mapped the West, recorded its plants, animals and peoples, and strengthened American claims to the region.

## The Louisiana Purchase

In 1803 the United States bought the Louisiana Territory from France for 15 million dollars, roughly doubling the size of the country. The territory, west of the Mississippi, was largely unknown to Americans. Jefferson, who had long been interested in the West, sent an expedition to explore it, find a practical water route to the Pacific, establish trade and diplomatic relations with Native American nations and assert American sovereignty.

## The leaders

Meriwether Lewis, born in 1774, was an army officer and Jefferson's personal secretary. He studied botany, astronomy, medicine and navigation in preparation. He chose William Clark, born in 1770, a former army officer and his friend, as co-leader. Clark was the expedition's main mapmaker. The Corps of Discovery included about thirty to forty soldiers and civilians, among them York, Clark's enslaved African American servant, who took part fully in the expedition.

## Up the Missouri

The expedition left Camp Dubois, near St Louis, on 14 May 1804, travelling up the Missouri river in a keelboat and canoes. They held councils with Native American nations, such as the Otoe, Missouria and Lakota, presenting peace medals and announcing American sovereignty. The only member to die on the expedition, Sergeant Charles Floyd, died of probable appendicitis in August 1804. The expedition spent the winter of 1804–1805 at Fort Mandan, near the villages of the Mandan and Hidatsa, in today's North Dakota.

## Sacagawea

At Fort Mandan, the expedition hired the French-Canadian trader Toussaint Charbonneau as an interpreter, with his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, who had been captured by the Hidatsa as a girl. She gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste, in February 1805, and carried him on the journey. Her knowledge, her translation and her presence, which showed that the expedition was peaceful, were valuable. When the expedition reached the Shoshone, she discovered that their chief, Cameahwait, was her brother. The Shoshone provided horses to cross the Rocky Mountains.

## To the Pacific

The expedition crossed the Rocky Mountains in difficult conditions, nearly starving in the Bitterroot Mountains, before being helped by the Nez Perce. They travelled by canoe down the Clearwater, Snake and Columbia rivers and reached the Pacific in November 1805. They built Fort Clatsop, near today's Astoria, Oregon, where they spent the winter. In a vote on the location of their winter camp, York and Sacagawea were included.

## Return

On the return journey, in 1806, the group split to explore more territory. Lewis's party had a violent encounter with Blackfeet warriors, killing two. The expedition reached St Louis on 23 September 1806. They had recorded over 170 plants and 120 animals new to science, and made maps. Their journals were published. The expedition opened the way for American expansion westward, with severe consequences for Native American nations.
