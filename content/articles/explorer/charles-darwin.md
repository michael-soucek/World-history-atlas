Charles Darwin was an English naturalist whose five-year voyage around the world on HMS Beagle, from 1831 to 1836, gave him the observations that led to his theory of evolution by natural selection. The voyage took him to South America, the Galápagos Islands, Tahiti, New Zealand, Australia and southern Africa.

## The invitation

In 1831 Darwin, aged twenty-two, had just graduated from Cambridge, where he had studied to become a clergyman but spent much of his time collecting beetles and studying natural history with the botanist John Stevens Henslow. Henslow recommended him to Captain Robert FitzRoy, who wanted a gentleman companion and naturalist for a Royal Navy survey voyage to chart the coasts of South America. Darwin's father at first opposed the idea, but his uncle, Josiah Wedgwood, persuaded him.

## The ship

HMS Beagle was a small ship, about 27 metres long, with a crew of about seventy. Darwin shared a cramped cabin, slept in a hammock and suffered badly from seasickness throughout the voyage. He and FitzRoy, a devout Christian with a fierce temper, argued at times, notably over slavery, which Darwin hated. The Beagle sailed from Plymouth on 27 December 1831.

## South America

Darwin spent most of the voyage, over three years, in South America, often travelling on land while the ship surveyed the coast. In Brazil, he was overwhelmed by the tropical rainforest and saw the cruelty of slavery. In Argentina, at Punta Alta, he dug up fossils of giant extinct mammals, such as Megatherium, a giant ground sloth, and Toxodon, and wondered why they had disappeared and why they resembled living species. He rode with gauchos across the pampas. In Tierra del Fuego, he met the Fuegians, and the Beagle returned three Fuegians whom FitzRoy had taken to England on an earlier voyage. He crossed the Andes, finding fossil seashells high in the mountains, and in Chile in 1835 experienced an earthquake that raised the coastline by several feet, supporting Charles Lyell's theory that the earth is shaped by gradual processes over vast time.

## The Galápagos

In September and October 1835, the Beagle spent five weeks in the Galápagos Islands, in the Pacific, about 1,000 kilometres west of Ecuador. Darwin collected birds, plants and animals, and observed giant tortoises and marine iguanas. The vice-governor told him that he could tell which island a tortoise came from by its shell. Darwin noticed that mockingbirds differed from island to island. Later, in London, the ornithologist John Gould identified his finches as many distinct species, though Darwin had not labelled them all by island. These observations led him to question whether species were fixed.

## Across the Pacific and home

The Beagle sailed on to Tahiti, New Zealand and Australia, where Darwin was struck by its unique animals, such as the platypus, and to the Cocos (Keeling) Islands, where he developed a theory of how coral atolls form, which proved correct. The ship visited Mauritius, the Cape of Good Hope, Saint Helena and Brazil again, and returned to Falmouth on 2 October 1836.

## After the voyage

Darwin published his journal, known as The Voyage of the Beagle, in 1839, which became popular. He never left Britain again. Over the next twenty years, he developed his theory of natural selection, published in On the Origin of Species in 1859.
