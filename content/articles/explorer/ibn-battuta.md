Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan scholar and traveller who, over nearly thirty years, from 1325 to 1354, journeyed about 120,000 kilometres across Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia and China, visiting most of the Islamic world and beyond. His account of his travels, the Rihla, is one of the most important sources on the fourteenth-century world.

## The pilgrim

Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Battuta was born in Tangier, Morocco, on 24 February 1304, into a family of Berber descent known for Islamic legal scholarship. In June 1325, aged twenty-one, he set out alone to make the pilgrimage to Mecca, travelling along the coast of North Africa. He would not return home for twenty-four years.

## The Middle East

Ibn Battuta reached Alexandria and Cairo, then travelled through Syria and Palestine, visiting Damascus and Jerusalem, and reached Mecca in 1326. Instead of going home, he decided to see the world, setting himself the rule never to travel the same road twice, as far as possible. He travelled to Iraq and Iran, visiting Baghdad and Tabriz, and made several more pilgrimages to Mecca. He sailed down the coast of East Africa, visiting Mogadishu, Mombasa and Kilwa, which he praised as one of the most beautiful towns in the world.

## Anatolia and the steppe

He travelled through Anatolia, where he was received by the Turkish emirs, and crossed the Black Sea to the lands of the Golden Horde, visiting the court of the khan Özbeg. According to his account, he accompanied one of the khan's wives, a Byzantine princess, to Constantinople, where he met the former emperor. He then travelled across Central Asia, through Bukhara and Samarkand, and through Afghanistan to India.

## India

In 1334 Ibn Battuta arrived in Delhi, where the sultan, Muhammad bin Tughluq, known for his generosity and unpredictable cruelty, appointed him a qadi, judge, with a large salary. He stayed for about eight years. In 1341 the sultan sent him as an ambassador to China, but the expedition was attacked by bandits and his ships were wrecked near Calicut.

## The Maldives and China

Ibn Battuta went to the Maldives, where he served as a judge for over a year and married several times. He visited Sri Lanka, where he climbed Adam's Peak, Bengal, Sumatra and, according to his account, China, reaching the port of Quanzhou and perhaps Beijing, though some scholars doubt parts of this account.

## Home and Mali

He returned to Morocco in 1349, during the Black Death, which had killed his mother. He visited al-Andalus, and in 1351–1353 crossed the Sahara to the Mali Empire, describing the court of Mansa Sulayman, the brother of Mansa Musa, and Timbuktu.

## The Rihla

In 1354 the Marinid sultan of Morocco, Abu Inan, commissioned the scholar Ibn Juzayy to write down Ibn Battuta's account. The book, A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling, known as the Rihla, "journey", describes the people, customs, rulers and places he saw. Scholars note that some passages were borrowed from earlier writers and some details may be exaggerated or invented, but it remains invaluable. Ibn Battuta died around 1368 or 1369, probably in Morocco.
