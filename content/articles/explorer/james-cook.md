James Cook was a British navigator, explorer and cartographer who made three voyages to the Pacific Ocean between 1768 and 1779. He mapped New Zealand and the east coast of Australia, crossed the Antarctic Circle, charted many Pacific islands, and became the first European to reach the Hawaiian Islands, where he was killed in 1779.

## Early life

Cook was born on 27 October 1728 in Marton, in Yorkshire, the son of a farm labourer. He worked in a shop, then was apprenticed to a ship owner in Whitby, sailing on colliers, coal ships, in the North Sea, where he learned navigation. In 1755 he joined the Royal Navy. During the Seven Years' War, he surveyed the St Lawrence river, helping the British attack on Quebec in 1759, and then charted the coast of Newfoundland, gaining a reputation as a skilled surveyor.

## The first voyage

In 1768 the Royal Society and the navy sent Cook, commanding HMS Endeavour, to Tahiti to observe the transit of Venus across the sun in June 1769, which would help calculate the distance between the earth and the sun. He also had secret orders to search for the hypothetical southern continent, Terra Australis. The naturalist Joseph Banks and the botanist Daniel Solander travelled with him. With the help of Tupaia, a Tahitian navigator, Cook sailed to New Zealand, which he mapped in six months, showing it was two islands. In April 1770, he reached the east coast of Australia, landing at Botany Bay, and claimed the coast for Britain, naming it New South Wales. The Endeavour struck the Great Barrier Reef and was repaired. Cook returned in 1771.

## The second voyage

On his second voyage, from 1772 to 1775, with HMS Resolution and Adventure, Cook sailed further south than anyone before, crossing the Antarctic Circle three times, and showed that any southern continent must lie near the pole, in the ice. He visited Easter Island, Tonga, the New Hebrides and New Caledonia. He tested John Harrison's marine chronometer, which allowed accurate calculation of longitude. By providing fresh food, including sauerkraut, and keeping the ship clean, he reduced deaths from scurvy.

## The third voyage

On his third voyage, beginning in 1776, Cook searched for a Northwest Passage from the Pacific to the Atlantic. In January 1778 he reached the Hawaiian Islands, which he named the Sandwich Islands. He then charted the Pacific coast of North America, from Oregon to Alaska, and sailed through the Bering Strait, until stopped by ice.

## Death in Hawaii

Cook returned to Hawaii, arriving at Kealakekua Bay in January 1779, during a festival for the god Lono, and was welcomed with ceremonies. Some historians argued that Hawaiians identified him with Lono, a view debated by others. After he left, a damaged mast forced him to return. Tensions rose, and when a ship's boat was stolen, Cook tried to take the chief Kalaniʻōpuʻu hostage. On 14 February 1779, in the resulting fight, Cook was killed.

## Legacy

Cook's maps were remarkably accurate. His voyages opened the Pacific to European colonisation, and in Australia, New Zealand and Hawaii, his legacy is contested because of its consequences for Indigenous peoples.
