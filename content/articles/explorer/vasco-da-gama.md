Vasco da Gama was a Portuguese explorer who, in 1497–1499, commanded the first fleet to sail from Europe around Africa to India, opening a sea route between Europe and Asia. His voyage made Portugal a major power in the Indian Ocean and began centuries of European maritime trade and colonialism in Asia.

## Background

Since the early fifteenth century, Portuguese expeditions, sponsored by Prince Henry the Navigator, had explored the west coast of Africa, seeking gold, slaves and a route to the spices of Asia, which reached Europe through Muslim and Venetian merchants at high prices. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope, showing that it was possible to sail from the Atlantic into the Indian Ocean.

## The voyage

Vasco da Gama, born around 1469 in Sines, the son of a nobleman, was chosen by King Manuel I to lead the expedition. He left Lisbon on 8 July 1497 with four ships, the São Gabriel, São Rafael, Berrio and a supply ship, and about 170 men. Instead of following the African coast, he made a wide arc through the South Atlantic, spending over three months out of sight of land, then rounded the Cape of Good Hope in November 1497.

## East Africa

Sailing up the east coast of Africa, the Portuguese reached the Swahili trading cities, part of a rich Indian Ocean trading world. At Mozambique and Mombasa, they met hostility. At Malindi, in today's Kenya, the ruler, a rival of Mombasa, welcomed them and provided an experienced pilot to guide them across the Indian Ocean, using the monsoon winds.

## Calicut

On 20 May 1498 the fleet reached Calicut, on the Malabar coast of India, the main centre of the spice trade. The Zamorin, the ruler, received da Gama, but was unimpressed by his gifts, cloth, hats, coral and washbasins, and the Muslim merchants who dominated the trade opposed the newcomers. Da Gama left in August 1498, taking some spices and Indian hostages. The return voyage across the Indian Ocean, against the monsoon, took three months, and many men died of scurvy. Da Gama reached Lisbon in September 1499, with two ships and about 55 survivors. The spices he brought back were worth many times the cost of the expedition.

## The second voyage

In 1502 da Gama led a heavily armed fleet of twenty ships to India, to establish Portuguese dominance by force. On the way, he captured a ship carrying Muslim pilgrims returning from Mecca, and burned it with hundreds of passengers on board, including women and children. He bombarded Calicut and made treaties with Calicut's rivals, Cochin and Cannanore.

## Viceroy and death

The Portuguese built an empire of fortified trading posts, including Goa, taken in 1510, Malacca in 1511 and Hormuz. In 1524 da Gama was appointed viceroy of Portuguese India, but he fell ill and died in Cochin on 24 December 1524. His remains were later moved to the Jerónimos Monastery in Lisbon. His voyage is celebrated in Luís de Camões's epic poem, The Lusiads, published in 1572. In India, he is also remembered for the violence of his second voyage.
