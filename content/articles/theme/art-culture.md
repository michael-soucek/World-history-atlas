Art often outlives the states that produced it. Long after a kingdom has disappeared from the map, its sculpture, buildings and craft techniques remain the clearest evidence of how its people lived, worshipped and saw themselves.

## Traditions across continents

In West Africa, the [Nok culture](/culture/nok-culture) of central Nigeria made expressive terracotta figures in the first millennium BCE, among the oldest large-scale sculpture south of the Sahara. Centuries later, artists at Ife modelled naturalistic heads in copper alloy and clay, and casters in the [Kingdom of Benin](/place/kingdom-of-benin) produced hundreds of brass plaques and many commemorative heads recording court life; most were seized by a British military expedition in 1897, and some have since been returned to Nigeria. In Ethiopia, churches were carved downward out of solid rock at Lalibela, traditionally under King Lalibela around 1200.

In the Andes, the masons of the [Inca civilisation](/culture/inca-civilization) fitted huge stones together without mortar so precisely that the walls of Cusco and Machu Picchu have survived centuries of earthquakes. Maya painters covered the rooms of Bonampak, in southern Mexico, with murals of court ceremony and battle around 790 CE. In Australia, rock art traditions in places such as Arnhem Land and the Kimberley stretch back many thousands of years.

Asian court art was shaped by imperial patrons. The Mughal emperor [Shah Jahan](/person/shah-jahan) built the Taj Mahal at Agra as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, while Mughal workshops produced miniature paintings that blended Persian, Indian and, later, European influences. In Iran, Shah Abbas I moved his capital to [Isfahan](/history-of/isfahan) in 1598 and rebuilt it around a vast square lined with mosques and a palace, decorated with glazed tile. In Edo-period Japan, woodblock prints of actors, landscapes and city life were made cheaply for townspeople, and in the nineteenth century they influenced European painters such as Monet and Van Gogh.

## Art on the move

Styles crossed borders far more freely than armies did, and the maps help explain the mixtures. In Gandhara, in present-day Pakistan and Afghanistan, sculptors under the [Kushan Empire](/place/kushan-empire) gave the Buddha a human form draped in robes that recall Greek and Roman statues, a legacy of the Hellenistic kingdoms that had ruled the region; this style travelled with Buddhism along the Silk Road towards China. Chinese porcelain, in turn, was exported in enormous quantities and copied by potters in Iran, the Ottoman lands and Europe, where Delft and Meissen imitated it.

Buildings preserve the memory of former rulers in the landscape. The Alhambra in [Granada](/history-of/granada), built by the Nasrid sultans in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, still stands in a city ruled by Christian monarchs since 1492, and Ottoman mosques and bridges survive across the Balkans. When a region changes colour on the atlas, its architecture often records the earlier layers that the new borders cover over.
