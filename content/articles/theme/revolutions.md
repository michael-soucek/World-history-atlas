Not every revolution redraws the map. Some change who rules inside borders that stay exactly where they were; others create new states where none existed. Reading the atlas well means telling the two apart.

## Revolutions inside the borders

The revolutions of 1848 broke out across Europe within a few weeks, in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest and many smaller capitals, driven by demands for constitutions, national rights and relief from economic hardship. Most were defeated within two years. In Hungary, the government led by [Lajos Kossuth](/person/lajos-kossuth) declared independence from the Habsburgs in 1849 and was crushed only after Russia sent an army to help Austria. The uprisings left Europe's borders almost unchanged, yet the ideas of 1848 fed directly into the unifications of Italy and Germany a generation later.

Other revolutions transformed a country while leaving its outline intact. The Mexican Revolution, which began in 1910, toppled the long rule of Porfirio Díaz and drew in peasant armies such as that of [Emiliano Zapata](/person/emiliano-zapata), whose demand for land reform shaped the constitution of 1917. In the former Ottoman heartland, [Mustafa Kemal Atatürk](/person/mustafa-kemal-ataturk) founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923 and abolished the caliphate the following year. In Cuba, the guerrillas of [Fidel Castro](/person/fidel-castro) took power in 1959 and created a communist state about 150 kilometres from the coast of Florida. Iran's revolution of 1979 replaced a monarchy with an Islamic republic. None of these moved a border, but each transformed the state inside it.

## Revolutions that made new states

Independence movements, by contrast, leave clear marks on the atlas. When Ghana won independence in 1957 under [Kwame Nkrumah](/person/kwame-nkrumah), it became an example for movements across Africa, and the following years saw the colonial map dissolve at remarkable speed. Some transitions were negotiated; others came only after long wars, as in Algeria between 1954 and 1962. Revolution in a colonial power could free its colonies too: in Portugal, the largely bloodless Carnation Revolution of April 1974 brought down the dictatorship and led to the independence of Guinea-Bissau, Mozambique, Angola, Cape Verde and São Tomé and Príncipe within about a year and a half.

Late in the twentieth century, peaceful mass protest brought down governments with growing frequency. In the Philippines, the People Power movement of 1986 removed Ferdinand Marcos, and the [revolutions of 1989](/event/revolutions-of-1989) in central and eastern Europe were mostly non-violent. In 2010 and 2011 protests spreading from Tunisia brought down governments in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya and Yemen, with outcomes ranging from democratic reform to prolonged civil war. When stepping through the snapshots, watch for both kinds of change: new borders and new names, and the quieter shifts in who held power within the old ones.
