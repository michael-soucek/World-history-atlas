Religions rarely spread by a single route. Armies, merchants, missionaries, marriages between ruling families and the decisions of individual kings all played a part, and the mixture varied from one faith and one region to the next.

## How faiths travelled

Buddhism owed much of its early reach to royal sponsorship. The Mauryan emperor [Ashoka](/person/ashoka) sent envoys to promote the teaching beyond India, and tradition credits his son with bringing [Buddhism](/culture/buddhism) to Sri Lanka. From there and from north-west India it moved with merchants into Central Asia, China and Southeast Asia, and from Korea it reached Japan in the sixth century. In Tibet it became so closely bound to government that from 1642 the Dalai Lamas headed the state, making [Tibet](/place/tibet) one of history's clearest examples of rule by religious leaders.

Hindu and Buddhist ideas of kingship shaped the temple states of Southeast Asia. Angkor Wat was built in the twelfth century as a temple to Vishnu and later became a Buddhist shrine, a reminder that the two traditions often coexisted under the same rulers. [Islam](/culture/islam), by contrast, spread first through conquest in the Middle East and North Africa, but in West Africa, the East African coast and island Southeast Asia it travelled mainly with traders and scholars. West African rulers such as [Mansa Musa](/person/mansa-musa) of Mali patronised mosques and learning, and Indonesia, converted largely through commerce and Sufi teachers, is today the country with the largest Muslim population in the world.

Christianity, too, reached far beyond Rome. The kingdom of Aksum in Ethiopia adopted it in the fourth century, and Christians of the Church of the East carried it across Asia: a stone stele set up at Chang'an, the Tang capital, in 781 records their presence in China.

## Faith on the map

The atlas rarely shows religion directly, but its effects are everywhere. Some states were themselves religious institutions: the caliphates claimed to lead the whole Muslim community, the Papal States were ruled by the pope, and the [State of the Teutonic Order](/place/state-of-the-teutonic-order) on the Baltic was governed by a crusading order of knights until its grand master turned Lutheran and made it the secular Duchy of Prussia in 1525.

Elsewhere, dynastic frontiers became frontiers of belief. After the Safavids made Shia Islam the faith of Iran in the sixteenth century, the long Ottoman and Safavid border also became a dividing line between Sunni and Shia worlds. In Europe, the Peace of Augsburg in 1555 let each German prince choose between Catholicism and Lutheranism for his territory, so the patchwork of the Holy Roman Empire became a confessional patchwork too, and the [Thirty Years' War](/event/thirty-years-war) was fought partly over its religious balance. In 1947 the [partition of India](/event/partition-of-india) drew new borders largely on the basis of Hindu and Muslim majorities, followed by one of the largest migrations in history. Following any of these frontiers through the snapshots shows how often maps of power have also been maps of faith.
