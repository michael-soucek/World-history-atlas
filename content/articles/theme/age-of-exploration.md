Long-distance voyaging did not begin with Europe. The Austronesian and Polynesian settlers of the Pacific crossed far wider stretches of open ocean than any fifteenth-century caravel, and the sweet potato, an American plant, was being grown in Polynesia before Europeans arrived, which suggests that [Polynesian navigation](/culture/polynesian-navigation) reached South America and returned. Around 1000, Norse sailors from Greenland built a short-lived settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. What changed after 1400 was that voyages became sustained, state-backed and linked to permanent trade and conquest.

## From coastal probing to ocean crossing

Portuguese expansion began with island colonies in the Atlantic, Madeira and the Azores, settled from the 1420s, and with voyages down the West African coast sponsored by Prince Henry, known as the Navigator. Each expedition pushed a little further south, until Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488. The small, manoeuvrable caravel, able to sail closer to the wind than older ships, and improving knowledge of Atlantic wind systems made these voyages possible. Sailors learned to swing far out into the ocean to catch favourable winds, a technique that led Pedro Álvares Cabral to Brazil in 1500 on his way to India.

The Pacific posed a different problem. Crossing from America to Asia was relatively easy, but for decades the Spanish could not find a route back against the winds. In 1565 the navigator Andrés de Urdaneta found it by sailing north to catch westerly winds, opening the way for the annual galleons between Manila and Acapulco. Later voyages, such as Vitus Bering's for Russia in 1728 and 1741, probed the far north between Asia and America.

## Lines on the map

Exploration drew some of the most ambitious lines in political history. In 1494 Spain and Portugal agreed in the Treaty of Tordesillas to divide newly found lands along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, Portugal taking the east and Spain the west. That line is why [colonial Brazil](/place/colonial-brazil) became Portuguese and why the [Portuguese Empire](/place/portuguese-empire) looks east towards Africa and Asia on the early modern maps, while the [Spanish Empire](/place/spanish-empire) fills most of the Americas. A second agreement, at Zaragoza in 1529, fixed a corresponding line on the other side of the world.

When following the snapshots from 1492 to 1800, notice how European claims first appear as coastal footholds and only gradually reach inland. The voyages also set in motion the [Columbian exchange](/event/columbian-exchange) of crops, animals and diseases, and the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade), whose effects reshaped the populations of three continents far more deeply than any line drawn by a treaty.
