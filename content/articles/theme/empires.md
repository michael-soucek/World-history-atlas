Conquest could make an empire, but only administration could keep one. The empires that lasted longest were those that found ways to bind conquered peoples to the centre, and the methods they chose differed enormously from one continent to another.

## How empires held together

Some empires ruled lightly. The [Aztec Empire](/place/aztec-empire) left most conquered city-states under their own rulers and demanded regular tribute in cloth, food, feathers and captives, which made it rich but brittle: when the Spanish arrived in 1519, many subject peoples joined them against Tenochtitlan. Steppe empires such as the Xiongnu and the early Mongols worked in a similar way, collecting tribute and the loyalty of chiefs rather than governing villages directly.

Others built dense machinery of control. The [Inca Empire](/place/inca-empire) moved whole communities to new regions, spread Quechua as a common language, required labour service from its subjects and tied its provinces together with a road system tens of thousands of kilometres long, complete with relay runners and storehouses. China's emperors relied on a salaried bureaucracy, and from the Sui and Tang dynasties onward officials were increasingly chosen through written examinations in the Confucian classics, a system that survived until 1905. Rome took a different route, gradually extending citizenship to provincial elites until, in 212 CE, almost all free inhabitants became citizens.

Many empires governed diversity by tolerating it. The [Ottoman Empire](/place/ottoman-empire) allowed Christian and Jewish communities to run their own religious and legal affairs, while recruiting soldiers and officials through the devshirme system, which took boys from Christian families into palace service. The Mughals employed Hindu nobles at the highest levels of the state, and the Achaemenid kings famously respected local gods and customs.

## Reading empires on the map

On the atlas, an empire's colour often claims more than its rulers truly held. Deserts, mountains and forests inside the outline might see no official for years, and the edges were usually zones of tribute and alliance rather than fixed borders. The [Mongol Empire](/place/mongol-empire) shows size outrunning unity: the lands conquered by Genghis Khan and his heirs covered more than 20 million square kilometres, but by the 1260s they had split into separate khanates that often fought one another. Maritime empires look different again. The Portuguese and Dutch empires in Asia appear as scattered dots of ports and forts, because their power lay in controlling trade routes, not territory.

The largest empire by area was the [British Empire](/place/british-empire), which around 1920 ruled roughly a quarter of the world's land and a similar share of its population. Its outline on the maps of 1900 to 1945 spans every inhabited continent, yet it ranged from self-governing dominions such as Canada and Australia to directly ruled colonies and protectorates where local rulers kept their thrones. Following one region, such as Anatolia or northern India, through the snapshots shows how often the colour of empire changed, and how much of what was built under one empire, roads, cities, languages and faiths, was inherited by the next.
