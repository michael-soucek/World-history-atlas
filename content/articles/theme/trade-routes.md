Behind the famous routes lay thousands of local exchanges, and the people who ran them were rarely the ones who made or used the goods. Most long-distance trade worked as a relay, with each group of middlemen carrying cargo over one stage of the journey and taking a profit at every handover.

## Middlemen and their cities

The [Phoenicians](/culture/phoenicia) were among the first great maritime middlemen. From Tyre and Sidon they shipped cedar wood, glass and cloth coloured with a costly purple dye made from sea snails, and founded trading posts along the Mediterranean as far as southern Spain, in search of silver and tin. On the overland routes of Central Asia, Sogdian merchants from cities such as [Samarkand](/history-of/samarkand) formed communities across northern China between roughly the fourth and eighth centuries, and their language served as a commercial lingua franca along the caravan roads.

In the Indian Ocean, the Swahili towns of the East African coast bought gold from the interior, including from the region of Great Zimbabwe, and ivory, and sold them to Arab, Persian and Indian merchants. [Swahili culture](/culture/swahili-culture), with its Bantu language enriched by Arabic words and its coral-stone mosques, grew directly from that commerce. In northern Europe, Viking traders went down the rivers of Russia to the Caspian and the Black Sea, and hoards buried on Gotland and in mainland Scandinavia contain tens of thousands of silver coins minted in the Islamic world. Later the Hanseatic League, an alliance of merchant towns led by Lübeck, dominated Baltic and North Sea trade and kept a trading post in the [Novgorod Republic](/place/novgorod-republic) to buy furs and wax.

The Americas had their own networks. Aztec long-distance merchants, the pochteca, travelled far beyond the empire's borders and doubled as spies, and cacao beans served as small change in Mesoamerican markets. After 1565 Spanish galleons sailed every year between Acapulco and [Manila](/history-of/manila), carrying American silver to buy Chinese silk and porcelain, a link that lasted until 1815 and connected the Pacific to the world economy.

## Trade on the map

Trading powers often look odd on the atlas because they wanted routes, not land. The [Republic of Venice](/place/republic-of-venice) held a chain of ports and islands down the Adriatic and across the eastern Mediterranean, including Crete for more than four centuries, as stepping stones for its galleys. The Portuguese empire in Asia appears as a string of forts from Mozambique to Malacca and Macau. Other trading networks, such as the Hanseatic League or the Sogdian diaspora, never formed a state at all, and so leave no colour on the map even though they shaped the economies of whole regions. When following a route across the snapshots, look for the cities and straits where it passed from one power's hands to another's: places such as Samarkand, Aden, Malacca and Constantinople repeatedly became the prizes of conquest because whoever held them could tax the trade.
