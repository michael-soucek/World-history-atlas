Many everyday habits of thought carry the fingerprints of distant civilisations. The 60 minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle descend from the base-60 arithmetic of Mesopotamian scribes, and the numerals used across the world today were developed in India, adopted in the Islamic world and only later taken up in Europe.

## Knowledge in many centres

Indian mathematicians made especially lasting contributions. Aryabhata, writing at the end of the fifth century CE, calculated with a place-value system and gave a good value for pi, and in 628 Brahmagupta set out rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. In Baghdad, the scholar [al-Khwarizmi](/person/al-khwarizmi), associated with the [House of Wisdom](/event/house-of-wisdom), wrote on Indian numerals and on solving equations; the word "algebra" comes from the title of one of his books and "algorithm" from his name. [Avicenna](/person/avicenna)'s Canon of Medicine, completed in the early eleventh century, was still being taught in European universities five hundred years later, and Ibn al-Haytham's work on optics argued that vision works by light entering the eye.

In the Americas, the Maya independently developed a symbol for zero, used in their Long Count calendar, and tracked the movements of Venus with great accuracy. The Inca, without writing in the usual sense, kept census and tax records on quipus, cords knotted in a decimal system. Pacific navigators held detailed knowledge of stars, winds and ocean swells that allowed them to find islands across thousands of kilometres of open sea.

Observatories marked the patronage of rulers. The Mongol ruler Hulagu founded one at Maragheh in Iran in 1259, and Ulugh Beg, Timur's grandson, built a great observatory at [Samarkand](/history-of/samarkand) in the 1420s, producing one of the most precise star catalogues made before the telescope. When [Nicolaus Copernicus](/person/nicolaus-copernicus) published his sun-centred model in 1543, he drew on astronomical work that had passed through Arabic and Persian hands.

## Science behind the maps

Science shaped the atlas in a very direct way, because the maps themselves depend on it. Ptolemy's Geography, written in Roman Egypt in the second century CE, listed thousands of places by latitude and longitude, and when it was translated into Latin around 1400 it influenced European mapmakers. Gerardus Mercator's projection of 1569 let sailors plot a constant compass bearing as a straight line. Finding longitude at sea remained difficult until John Harrison's marine chronometers proved their accuracy in trials during the 1760s, and in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries surveyors in France, Britain and British India used triangulation to measure entire countries.

The maps also show where science flourished: in wealthy, connected states that could pay scholars and gather books, from Abbasid Baghdad and Song China to the capitals of the [Scientific Revolution](/culture/scientific-revolution). Following the snapshots, the centres of learning move with the centres of power and trade, and the great translation movements, from Greek into Arabic in Baghdad and from Arabic into Latin in twelfth-century Spain, cluster along the frontiers where civilisations met.
