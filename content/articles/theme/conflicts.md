Wars change maps in two stages. The fighting decides who holds ground, but the borders that last are usually written afterwards, in treaties and settlements negotiated by diplomats, and those documents can outlive the armies by centuries.

## Battles that set limits

Some of history's most important battles mattered because they marked the edge of an expansion. In 751 an Abbasid army beat Tang Chinese forces near the Talas River, at the high-water mark of Tang power in Central Asia. In 1260 the Mamluks of Egypt beat a Mongol army at [Ain Jalut](/event/battle-of-ain-jalut) in Palestine, halting the Mongol advance towards Egypt. Japan twice escaped Mongol invasion, in 1274 and 1281, helped both times by storms that wrecked many of the invading ships. In 1896 Ethiopia's victory over Italy at Adwa kept it independent through the European partition of Africa.

Internal wars could be more destructive than foreign ones. The [An Lushan Rebellion](/event/an-lushan-rebellion), which began in 755, devastated Tang China and left the dynasty permanently weakened. The [Taiping Rebellion](/event/taiping-rebellion) of 1850 to 1864, a civil war led by a man who claimed to be the younger brother of Jesus, killed somewhere between 20 and 30 million people, a toll comparable to that of the First World War. Japan's [Sengoku period](/event/sengoku-period) of warring lords lasted more than a century before reunification under Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu, whose victory at Sekigahara in 1600 settled the country for two and a half centuries.

New weapons repeatedly overturned the balance of power. At Panipat in 1526, Babur's field guns and matchlocks helped him defeat a much larger Delhi army and found the Mughal Empire. In southern Africa, the [Zulu Kingdom](/place/zulu-kingdom) built a disciplined army that destroyed a British force at Isandlwana in January 1879, yet was defeated within months by British rifles and artillery.

## How wars appear on the maps

On the atlas, wars show up as sudden jumps between snapshots: a colour that expands across a whole region, a state that disappears, a border that moves. To see a war's real outcome, look at the snapshot after the peace treaty. The Peace of Westphalia in 1648, ending the [Thirty Years' War](/event/thirty-years-war), confirmed the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederation and gave Sweden territory on the German coast. In 1689 Russia and Qing China signed the Treaty of Nerchinsk, which kept Russia out of the Amur basin for more than 150 years. After the [Napoleonic Wars](/event/napoleonic-wars), the Congress of Vienna of 1815 rebuilt the map of Europe so carefully that most of its borders lasted for decades, and after 1918 the peace treaties dismantled whole empires. Comparing the snapshots just before and just after each of these settlements shows more clearly than any battle how war remade the world.
