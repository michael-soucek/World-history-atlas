Sidon, Saida in Arabic, is a port on the Mediterranean coast of southern Lebanon, about 40 kilometres from both Beirut and Tyre. One of the oldest and most important Phoenician cities, it was famous for glass and purple dye, competed with Tyre for leadership of Phoenicia, and was ruled in turn by Egyptians, Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Arabs, Crusaders and Ottomans. It is now Lebanon's third-largest city and the capital of the South Governorate.

## Bronze Age port

Stone tools from sites around the city show that people lived here deep in prehistory. In the Middle Bronze Age Sidon became a busy port trading with Egypt, and around 1350 BCE it was part of the Egyptian empire, ruled by a prince named Zimredda. The diplomatic letters of the Amarna period show him competing with the rulers of Tyre to the south and [Byblos](/history-of/byblos) to the north as Egyptian power weakened. The city's Phoenician name probably meant "fishing town".

## Phoenician metropolis

Sidon may have been the oldest of the Phoenician cities, and from its harbours merchants built a trading network across the Mediterranean. Tradition says that colonists from Sidon founded [Tyre](/history-of/tyre), and the two cities long disputed the title of mother city of [Phoenicia](/culture/phoenicia); outsiders often called Phoenicians in general "Sidonians" or "Tyrians". Homer praised Sidonian craftsmanship. Glass and purple dye were the great industries, and a large heap of broken murex shells near the southern harbour still shows the scale of dye making.

The city later fell to the Assyrians and then to Nebuchadnezzar II of [Babylon](/history-of/babylon). Under the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire) it overtook Tyre as the leading city of Phoenicia; its fleet fought on the Persian side at Salamis in 480 BCE, and Cambyses II rewarded its help in conquering Egypt with Dor, Joppa and the Plain of Sharon. The sarcophagus of the fifth-century BCE king Eshmunazar II, discovered in 1855, names Eshmun and Astarte among the city's gods. Around 352 BCE Sidon rose against Persia; Artaxerxes III invaded Phoenicia and the city was destroyed. It passed to [Alexander the Great](/person/alexander-the-great) in 333 BCE and enjoyed some autonomy under his successors. Its royal necropolis later yielded the so-called Alexander Sarcophagus, probably made for the local king Abdalonymus.

## Romans, Crusaders and Ottomans

Under Rome Sidon kept minting its own silver coins, Herod of Judaea built it a theatre, and under Elagabalus it became a Roman colony. After the earthquake of 551 ruined most cities on the coast, the law school of Berytus moved here. Life in the town went on quietly until the Muslims conquered the Levant in 636.

On 4 December 1110 Sidon fell after a siege to Baldwin I, king of Jerusalem, aided by the Norwegian king Sigurd I, and it became the centre of an important lordship within the [Kingdom of Jerusalem](/place/kingdom-of-jerusalem). [Saladin](/person/saladin) took it in 1187, German crusaders won it back in 1197, the Ayyubids destroyed it in 1249, and the Mongols under Kitbuqa did so again in 1260. The Sea Castle by the harbour was built by the Crusaders in the early thirteenth century.

After it came under the [Ottoman Empire](/place/ottoman-empire) early in the sixteenth century, Sidon became the capital of a province and regained much of its trade. In the eighteenth century the Hammud family controlled the region's cotton exports and built palaces and public works. During the Egyptian–Ottoman war a British-led squadron bombarded the city on 26 September 1840, and it surrendered within two days. From 1887 the Ottomans excavated the royal necropolis and carried its treasures to Istanbul. By 1900 Sidon was a small fishing town of about 10,000 people.

## Modern Sidon

After the First World War Sidon became part of the French Mandate, and after the Second it was a major city of independent [Lebanon](/country/lebanon). Palestinian refugees who arrived after 1948 were settled in two camps, Ein el-Hilweh and Mieh Mieh, which grew into districts of the city with a separate legal and political status. During the civil war, shelling by the South Lebanon Army killed at least sixteen people in the city centre in April 1981, and after the 1982 Israeli invasion the Israeli army occupied Sidon for almost two and a half years. Today about 80,000 people live within the city limits and more than a quarter of a million in the wider urban area.
