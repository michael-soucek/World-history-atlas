Zürich lies at the northern end of Lake Zürich, where the Limmat River flows out of the lake, in the hilly lowlands of northern Switzerland. The lake and river made it a natural transfer point for goods moving between the Rhine and the Alpine passes. A small Roman customs post and later a medieval town of abbeys and guilds, it became the centre of the Swiss Reformation in the 16th century and of Swiss banking and industry in the 19th. It is today the largest city in Switzerland, though not its capital.

## Lake dwellers, Romans and abbeys

People lived on the shores of the lake from the Neolithic onwards, building villages of wooden houses on piles at the water's edge; several of these sites form part of a UNESCO listing of prehistoric pile dwellings around the Alps. A Celtic settlement occupied the Lindenhof hill above the river before the Romans arrived. Under [the Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire) the place was known as Turicum and had a customs station collecting duties on goods crossing between provinces; a 2nd-century tombstone preserves the name. A small fort stood on the Lindenhof in the late Roman period.

After Roman rule ended, the area was settled by the Alemanni and came under the [Frankish kingdom](/place/frankish-kingdom). Local legend credits [Charlemagne](/person/charlemagne) with founding the Grossmünster church over the graves of the city's martyr saints, Felix and Regula. In 853 his grandson Louis the German founded the Fraumünster abbey for his daughter Hildegard, and its abbesses held important rights over the town for centuries. A royal palace stood on the Lindenhof.

## Free city and Swiss canton

When the Zähringen dukes, the town's overlords, died out in 1218, Zürich became an imperial free city within the [Holy Roman Empire](/place/holy-roman-empire), answerable only to the emperor. In 1336 the knight Rudolf Brun led a revolt that gave the craft guilds a share of power, with himself as mayor for life. To defend his regime against the Habsburgs, Zürich joined the Swiss Confederation in 1351. In the Old Zürich War (1440 to 1446) the city fought its own confederates in alliance with the Habsburgs and lost.

From 1519 the priest Huldrych Zwingli, preaching at the Grossmünster, led a [Reformation](/event/protestant-reformation) that the city council adopted in the following years, abolishing the mass, closing monasteries and stripping the churches of images. Zwingli was killed in 1531 at the Battle of Kappel, fighting the Catholic cantons, but Zürich remained Protestant. Protestant refugees from Italy and France later brought silk and other textile skills that enriched the city.

## The modern city

The old patrician government collapsed when French revolutionary armies invaded in 1798 and created the Helvetic Republic, and two battles were fought near the city in 1799. Under the federal state founded in 1848, Zürich became the country's economic centre. The first railway built entirely on Swiss soil, from Zürich to Baden, opened in 1847; the federal polytechnic, now ETH Zürich, opened in 1855; and the bank that became Credit Suisse was founded in 1856. [Albert Einstein](/person/albert-einstein) studied at the polytechnic, and during the First World War [Lenin](/person/vladimir-lenin) lived in the city while the Dada artists launched their movement at the Cabaret Voltaire in 1916.

The city absorbed its suburbs in 1893 and 1934. It is now a global financial centre with about 430,000 inhabitants and more than a million in its metropolitan area, and its residents vote directly on many local decisions in the Swiss tradition.
