Skopje lies on the upper Vardar River in the north of North Macedonia, where the river leaves a mountain gorge and crosses a broad basin. The Vardar valley is the main north-south route through the central Balkans, linking the Danube lands to Thessaloniki and the Aegean, so the town has always been a junction and a prize. Its history has been shaped by changes of empire, by more than five centuries of Ottoman rule, and by earthquakes, above all the one that destroyed much of the city in 1963.

People were living on the Kale hill, the rocky height above the river crossing, by about 4000 BCE. In Roman times the main settlement, Scupi, stood a few kilometres to the north-west. It became a Roman colony for veterans and later the chief town of the province of Dardania, and it had a bishop by the fourth century. An earthquake levelled Scupi in 518 CE. The emperor [Justinian I](/person/justinian-i), born in a village nearby, is often said to have rebuilt the town, though historians debate exactly where.

In the early Middle Ages Slavic settlers moved into the valley and the town, now on its present site around the Kale, passed back and forth between [the Byzantine Empire](/place/byzantine-empire) and the Bulgarian rulers. Byzantium held it for most of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, Bulgarian rulers again in parts of the thirteenth, and in 1282 the Serbian king Stefan Milutin took it. His grandson Stefan Dušan was crowned emperor of the Serbs and Greeks in Skopje in 1346, briefly making it the capital of a large Balkan empire.

## Üsküb under the Ottomans

[The Ottoman Empire](/place/ottoman-empire) captured the town in 1392 and held it, as Üsküb, until 1912. Under Ottoman rule it gained the stone bridge over the Vardar in its present form, mosques such as the Mustafa Pasha Mosque of 1492, inns for caravans, baths and a sprawling bazaar, which still makes up the old quarter on the left bank. Muslim, Orthodox Christian, Jewish and Roma communities lived side by side. In 1689, during a war with the Habsburgs, the Austrian general Piccolomini occupied the town and his troops set it on fire as they withdrew, perhaps to halt an outbreak of plague. Üsküb recovered slowly, and the arrival of the railway from Thessaloniki in 1873 revived its trade.

In the First Balkan War of 1912 Serbian troops took the city, and after the First World War it became part of the new South Slav kingdom, renamed Yugoslavia in 1929. During the Second World War it was occupied by Bulgaria, and in 1943 the city's Jewish community was deported to Treblinka and murdered. [Mother Teresa](/person/mother-teresa) was born in Skopje in 1910 to an Albanian Catholic family.

From 1945 Skopje was the capital of the Macedonian republic within [Tito's](/person/josip-broz-tito) Yugoslavia. On 26 July 1963 an earthquake killed more than a thousand people and destroyed most of the city's buildings. International aid poured in, and a master plan by the Japanese architect Kenzo Tange shaped much of the concrete modernist centre. Skopje became capital of an independent state in 1991; after a long dispute with Greece over the name, the country adopted the name [North Macedonia](/country/north-macedonia) in 2019. A government building programme in the 2010s, known as Skopje 2014, added many statues and neoclassical facades. The city has around half a million inhabitants, about a quarter of the national population.
