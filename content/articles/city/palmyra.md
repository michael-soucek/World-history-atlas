Palmyra, called Tadmor in its own Aramaic and in Arabic, is an oasis city in the desert of central [Syria](/country/syria). A minor watering stop for most of its early history, it grew rich in the first three centuries AD on the caravan trade between the Roman world and the lands beyond the Euphrates, and for a few years in the 270s it was the capital of an empire that challenged Rome. Several of its famous temples and tombs were deliberately destroyed in 2015.

## Oasis and caravan city

People lived around the Efqa spring as early as the Neolithic, and a mud-brick building of about 2500 BC lies beneath the later Temple of Bel. The town enters written history around 2000 BC, when a merchant called Puzur-Ishtar the Tadmorean appears in a contract at the Assyrian trading post of Kültepe. Biblical and later Arabic traditions credit its founding to King Solomon, but scholars regard this as a confusion with another place, and archaeology shows Palmyra was only a small settlement in the tenth century BC.

Under the [Seleucid Empire](/place/seleucid-empire) the town prospered and its first great temples and tombs were begun. When Rome annexed the Seleucid lands in 64 BC Palmyra stayed independent, trading with both Rome and Parthia, and in 41 BC its people fled across the Euphrates from troops sent by Mark Antony. Around 14 AD, early in the reign of Tiberius, it was brought into the province of Syria within the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire), though it kept wide self-government. The Temple of Bel was dedicated in 32 AD, and in the second century, after Rome absorbed the Nabataean kingdom of Petra, Palmyrene merchants came to dominate the desert routes. Hadrian visited in 129 and made it a free city.

## Odaenathus, Zenobia and Aurelian

The rise of the Sasanian Persian empire hurt Palmyra's trade and drew the city into war. After the Persians captured the emperor Valerian in 260, the Palmyrene lord Odaenathus defeated Shapur I's army, crushed rival claimants in Syria and was made governor of the whole East by Gallienus, later styling himself King of Kings. When he was assassinated in 267, his young son Vaballathus succeeded him, with real power held by his mother [Zenobia](/person/zenobia). In 270 and 271 she took Roman Arabia, Egypt and much of Anatolia, and in late 271 mother and son took the imperial titles Augusta and Augustus.

The emperor Aurelian defeated Zenobia near Antioch and at Emesa in 272 and besieged Palmyra; she was captured while seeking Persian help, and the city surrendered. When it rebelled again in 273, Aurelian razed it and massacred its people. Diocletian later built a legionary camp in its western part, and the reduced town survived as a Christian frontier fortress.

## Caliphs, sultans and Ottomans

Khalid ibn al-Walid took Palmyra for the [Rashidun Caliphate](/place/rashidun-caliphate) in 634, and it did well under the Umayyads, who turned part of the Temple of Bel into a mosque. It supported failed revolts against Marwan II and the early Abbasids, and from the ninth century its population shrank until the inhabitants lived only inside the walls of the Bel sanctuary. Over the following centuries it passed between Hamdanid, Fatimid, Seljuk, Burid, Zengid and Ayyubid rulers; the castle on the hill above was built around 1230. Under the Mamluks it was held by the Bedouin Al Fadl clan until attacked by [Tamerlane](/person/tamerlane) in 1400, after which it dwindled to a village. The Ottomans made it the centre of a district by around 1560, chiefly for its salt.

## Excavation, war and damage

English scholars described the ruins in the 1690s. Under the French Mandate, the archaeologist Henri Seyrig began large-scale digs in 1929, and the villagers living in the Temple of Bel were moved to a new village, Tadmur, by 1932. UNESCO listed the site as World Heritage in 1980.

During the [Syrian civil war](/event/syrian-civil-war) the site was used by the Syrian army, and the Islamic State group occupied it between May 2015 and March 2016, and again between December 2016 and March 2017. Its fighters killed the retired antiquities chief Khaled al-Asaad and blew up the Temple of Baalshamin, the inner sanctuary of the Temple of Bel, several tower tombs and much of the Tetrapylon, and damaged the Monumental Arch and theatre. The Syrian Free Army took the town in December 2024, the day before the fall of the Assad government; since then, restoration work has been limited mainly to surveys.
