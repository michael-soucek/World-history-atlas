Milan lies in the middle of the Lombard plain, between the Alps and the river Po, with no sea, no great river and no natural defences. What it has instead is position: roads from the Alpine passes to the south and from Venice to France meet here, and that has made it rich in almost every century. Its history swings between periods as a capital in its own right, under late Roman emperors and Renaissance dukes, and long stretches as the prize of foreign powers, before it became the industrial and financial engine of modern Italy.

## From Mediolanum to the duchy

Celtic Insubres settled the site by around 600 BCE. Roman armies took it in 222 BCE and called it Mediolanum, "the place in the middle of the plain". Its moment of imperial glory came in the late third century, when [Diocletian](/person/diocletian) made it the residence of the western emperors in 286 CE. In 313 [Constantine I](/person/constantine-i) and Licinius agreed there the policy of toleration remembered as the Edict of Milan, and under its bishop Ambrose (374 to 397) the city became a powerhouse of Latin Christianity. Its days as a capital ended in 402, when the court moved to the safety of Ravenna. Attila sacked it in 452, Gothic forces devastated it in 539 during Justinian's wars, and the Lombards, who gave Lombardy its name, captured it in 569.

By the eleventh century Milan was a self-governing commune within the [Holy Roman Empire](/place/holy-roman-empire) and the leading city of northern Italy. Its defiance of [Frederick Barbarossa](/person/frederick-barbarossa) led him to raze much of it in 1162, but the Lombard League, with Milan at its heart, defeated him at Legnano in 1176. Power then passed to signori: the Visconti family won the city in 1277, and in 1395 Gian Galeazzo Visconti bought the title of duke from the emperor. After the Visconti line died out in 1447 and a short-lived republic collapsed, the condottiere Francesco Sforza took over in 1450. The Sforza court made Milan a centre of the [Renaissance](/culture/renaissance), and [Leonardo da Vinci](/person/leonardo-da-vinci) painted the Last Supper there in the 1490s.

## Foreign rule and the modern city

The French invaded in 1499, and after decades of war the duchy passed to Spain when the last Sforza duke died in 1535. Spanish rule lasted until the War of the Spanish Succession, which handed Milan to the Austrian Habsburgs (confirmed in 1714). The plague of 1630 killed a large part of the population, an episode later made famous by Alessandro Manzoni's novel The Betrothed. Austrian reforms under Maria Theresa modernised the city, and the opera house La Scala opened in 1778.

[Napoleon](/person/napoleon) entered in 1796 and made Milan the capital first of the Cisalpine Republic and then of his Kingdom of Italy (1805 to 1814). Austria returned in 1815, but in March 1848 the citizens drove its garrison out during the Five Days of Milan. After the battle of Magenta in 1859 Lombardy joined Piedmont-Sardinia, and two years later the new Kingdom of Italy.

Milan industrialised fast, and its politics were turbulent: troops fired on bread rioters in 1898, and in 1919 [Benito Mussolini](/person/benito-mussolini) founded the Fascist movement at a meeting in the city. Allied bombing in 1943 badly damaged it, and in April 1945 the dictator's body was displayed in Piazzale Loreto. After the war Milan led the "economic miracle", becoming Italy's capital of banking, publishing, fashion and design. The city has about 1.4 million inhabitants, with more than 3 million in its metropolitan area.
