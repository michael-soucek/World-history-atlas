Ljubljana lies in a basin in central Slovenia, where the Ljubljanica river runs between a castle hill and the wide Ljubljana Marshes. The gap here is the easiest route from the Danube lands to the head of the Adriatic, so the site has carried traffic between central Europe and Italy since prehistory. For six centuries it was Laibach, a provincial capital of the Habsburgs; only in the twentieth century did it become the capital of a Slovene nation, first inside Yugoslavia and from 1991 as an independent state.

## Emona and medieval Laibach

People lived in pile dwellings on the edge of the marshes from the fifth millennium BCE onwards, and a wooden wheel and axle found there, dated to about 3150 BCE, are among the oldest known anywhere. After the region came under Rome, a military camp was followed in the early first century CE by the walled colony of Emona, on the main road from Aquileia to the Danube. It was a town of perhaps 5,000 people within the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire), and its rectangular street grid still shapes part of the modern centre. Emona declined in the troubled fifth century and was sacked by the Huns in 452, and Slavs, ancestors of the Slovenes, settled the area from the late sixth century.

The medieval town grew beneath the castle hill and is first named in documents of the 1140s. It received town rights in the thirteenth century and in 1335 passed to the Habsburgs, who made it the capital of the duchy of Carniola within the [Holy Roman Empire](/place/holy-roman-empire). A bishopric was founded in 1461. The town survived Ottoman raids in the fifteenth century, and after an earthquake in 1511 much of it was rebuilt. During the [Protestant Reformation](/event/protestant-reformation) the preacher Primož Trubar, who worked in Ljubljana, published the first printed books in Slovene in 1550, laying the foundations of the written language. The Catholic Counter-Reformation then returned the town to the church, and the Baroque cathedral and churches of the old town date mostly from around 1700.

## Habsburg province to national capital

Under [Napoleon](/person/napoleon), Ljubljana was the capital of the French-ruled Illyrian Provinces from 1809 to 1813, a brief period remembered for encouraging the Slovene language in schools. Restored to the [Habsburg Monarchy](/place/habsburg-monarchy), it hosted the Congress of Laibach in 1821, where the conservative powers agreed to crush revolution in Naples. The railway from Vienna reached the city in 1849, and Slovene national feeling grew steadily, especially after Slovenes gained control of the city council in the 1880s.

A strong earthquake in 1895 damaged much of the town, and the rebuilding gave it many Vienna Secession buildings. When [Austria-Hungary](/place/austria-hungary) collapsed in 1918, Ljubljana became the chief city of the Slovene lands within the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, later Yugoslavia. Between the wars the architect Jože Plečnik reshaped its riverbanks, bridges and squares.

In April 1941 Italy occupied and annexed the city, and in 1942 surrounded it with a barbed-wire fence some 30 kilometres long to cut off the resistance. German forces took over after Italy's surrender in 1943. After the war Ljubljana was capital of the Socialist Republic of Slovenia within [Josip Broz Tito](/person/josip-broz-tito)'s Yugoslavia. Slovenia declared independence on 25 June 1991, and after a ten-day war it left the federation. The country joined the European Union in 2004. Ljubljana, with about 300,000 inhabitants, is the capital of [Slovenia](/country/slovenia) and by far its biggest city.
