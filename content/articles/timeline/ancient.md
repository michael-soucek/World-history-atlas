In 3000 BCE the states of the world could be counted on two hands, and nearly all of them sat in the valleys of the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, and the lowlands of south-western Iran. Each was compact, often a single city with its fields, and the land between them belonged to villagers, herders and hunters who answered to no king. By 500 BCE one state, the [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire), stretched more than four thousand kilometres from the Aegean to the Indus. Following how that change happened is the main reason to step through the maps of this age.

The first shift to look for is consolidation. Mesopotamia's patchwork of city-states is pulled together, briefly, under Akkad and later under Babylon, then splits again. Egypt holds its long ribbon of the Nile almost throughout, and from around 1500 BCE it reaches south into Nubia and north-east into the Levant, where it meets the [Hittites](/place/hittites) pushing down from Anatolia. Syria becomes the meeting ground of rival powers, a role it would play many times again.

The second is the spread of states into new regions. Shading appears in north-west India with the Indus cities, on Crete and the Greek mainland, along the middle Nile with the kingdom of Kerma and then [Kush](/place/kingdom-of-kush), on the Yellow River with the Shang, and later in Mesoamerica with the [Olmecs](/place/olmecs). Those in China and Mesoamerica arose with no contact with the Near East at all, a reminder that the state was invented more than once.

The third is the shock of the years after 1200 BCE. On the map of 1000 BCE the great Bronze Age powers have shrunk or vanished, and the eastern Mediterranean is a scatter of small kingdoms and city-states, with Phoenician ports, the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, and Aramaean and Neo-Hittite states in Syria.

Then comes the age of conquest. By 700 BCE [Assyria](/place/assyria) has swallowed the Levant and much of Mesopotamia, with Urartu as its northern rival and Kushite pharaohs ruling Egypt. Within two centuries both Assyria and its Babylonian successor are gone, and the whole region has been absorbed into Persia. Elsewhere, borders remain soft or absent. Most of Europe, Africa, the Americas and northern Asia are still shown as peoples or cultures rather than states, and even the largest empires controlled roads, cities and river valleys more firmly than the deserts and mountains inside their shaded outlines.
