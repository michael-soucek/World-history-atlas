The map of 1800 still has large blank spaces in the political sense. Napoleon's wars are rearranging Europe, Spain and Portugal still hold most of Central and South America, and the Mughal, Ottoman, Qing and Russian empires dominate Asia. Yet the interior of Africa, most of Australia, the northern forests of the Americas and the American West belong to peoples and states that no European cartographer of the time would have recognised as sovereign. Two centuries later, almost every square kilometre of land outside Antarctica lies inside a recognised state, and the number of independent countries has risen to around two hundred.

The modern snapshots come closer together, and the changes between them are sharp. From 1815 to 1880, watch Latin America break into independent republics, the United States spread to the Pacific, and Italy and Germany consolidate. Between 1880 and 1914 comes one of the fastest land grabs in history, as the [Scramble for Africa](/event/scramble-for-africa) divides the continent among European powers with straight lines drawn across deserts and savanna, while Britain, France and Russia push deeper into Asia and Japan takes Taiwan and Korea.

The 1914 map is the high-water mark of empire. The 1920 map shows what the [First World War](/event/world-war-i) did to it: Austria-Hungary has disappeared, a chain of new states runs from Finland and the Baltic republics through Poland and Czechoslovakia to Yugoslavia, the [Soviet Union](/place/soviet-union) is forming in place of the Russian Empire, and the Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire have become British and French mandates. The snapshots for 1938 and 1945 then capture Axis expansion and its reversal, with Germany divided into occupation zones and Japan's empire dismantled at the end of the war.

From 1945 to 1960, and on through the following decades, the great colonial blocs break apart into dozens of smaller states. Watch India and Pakistan emerge in 1947, then the colours of Britain, France, Belgium and Portugal disappear from Africa, mostly between 1957 and 1975, and colonial borders survive almost unchanged as national ones. The [dissolution of the Soviet Union](/event/dissolution-of-the-soviet-union) in 1991, visible in the 1994 snapshot, turns one state into fifteen, alongside the reunification of Germany and the breakup of Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia.

The latest maps look stable, but they are not final. South Sudan became independent in 2011, Kosovo's 2008 declaration of independence is recognised by some states and not others, and Kashmir, Western Sahara, Crimea, Taiwan and the Israeli-Palestinian territories remain the subject of competing claims that a single map can only simplify.
