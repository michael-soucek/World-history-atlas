The map of 500 BCE is dominated by a single giant. Persia covers the land from Thrace and Egypt to the Indus, while around its edges sit much smaller units: the quarrelling Greek city-states, Carthage on the North African coast, a modest Roman republic in central Italy, the kingdoms and republics of the Ganges plain, and the rival states of Zhou China. A thousand years later the picture is almost reversed in the west and transformed in the east, and the snapshots in between are some of the most eventful on the atlas.

Watch first for the sudden swelling and splitting of Alexander's realm. The map for 323 BCE, the year of his death, shows a single empire from Greece to the Punjab; by 300 BCE it has been carved into successor kingdoms, with the Ptolemies in Egypt and the [Seleucid Empire](/place/seleucid-empire) holding most of Asia. At the same moment India gains its first giant state in the [Maurya Empire](/place/maurya-empire), and within a century China is unified for the first time under Qin and then the [Han](/place/han-dynasty).

From then on the Old World settles into a belt of large empires stretching across the middle latitudes. Rome spreads out from Italy until, by the turn of the era, it rings the whole Mediterranean, which Romans called simply "our sea". East of the Euphrates lies Parthia, later Sasanian Persia; then the Kushans straddling the Hindu Kush; then Han China, reaching west into the Tarim Basin. North of this belt the steppe nomads, such as the [Xiongnu](/place/xiongnu), appear as huge but loosely bounded confederations whose edges should be read as zones of influence, not lines.

The frontiers to watch most closely are the long Roman ones on the Rhine and Danube and the contested Roman and Persian border in Mesopotamia and Armenia, which shifts back and forth across the centuries. In China the reverse of consolidation appears after 220 CE, when the Han realm breaks into the Three Kingdoms and later into separate northern and southern dynasties.

The final snapshots record the great reshuffle of the Migration Period. The Roman Empire appears divided into eastern and western halves, and by 500 CE, after the [fall of Rome](/event/fall-of-rome) in the west, the western half has been replaced by Germanic kingdoms of the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Franks, Vandals and others, while the eastern empire holds firm. Beyond the old centres, newer states have risen: Aksum in the Horn of Africa, the Maya city-states and Teotihuacan in Mesoamerica, and the Moche on the coast of Peru. Even so, most of the world's land remains outside any state.
