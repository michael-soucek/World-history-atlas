Oceania covers Australia, New Guinea, New Zealand and the thousands of islands scattered across the Pacific, conventionally grouped as Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia. It contains both one of the oldest continuous human cultures on earth and some of the last places humans ever reached. For most of its history it had few states in the European sense: authority rested with clans, chiefs and kin networks rather than kings with fixed borders, which is why the region appears on historical maps mostly as peoples and cultures rather than empires.

## Sahul and the first Australians

During the Ice Age, lower sea levels joined Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania into a single continent called Sahul, but it was never connected to Asia, so the first settlers must have crossed open water. People had arrived by at least 50,000 years ago, and some archaeologists argue for an earlier date. [Aboriginal Australians](/culture/aboriginal-australians) and Torres Strait Islanders developed hundreds of languages, complex systems of law and kinship, rock art traditions spanning tens of thousands of years, and deep knowledge of country, managing the land with fire. In the New Guinea highlands, people at Kuk Swamp were cultivating bananas and taro by about 7,000 years ago, making New Guinea one of the world's independent centres of agriculture.

## The settlement of the Pacific

The remote Pacific was settled much later by Austronesian-speaking voyagers. From around 1500 BCE the Lapita culture, known for its finely stamped pottery, spread from the Bismarck Archipelago east of New Guinea through island Melanesia and reached Fiji, Tonga and Samoa by roughly 800 BCE. After a long pause, [Polynesian navigation](/culture/polynesian-navigation) carried people in double-hulled voyaging canoes across thousands of kilometres of open ocean, reading stars, swells and birds. They reached the Society Islands and much of eastern Polynesia around 1000 to 1200 CE, Hawaii and Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in the same period, and New Zealand, last of all, around 1250 to 1300, where their descendants became the Māori. Tonga built a maritime chiefdom whose influence reached neighbouring archipelagos.

## Colonisation and independence

Magellan's expedition crossed the Pacific in 1521, the Dutch navigator Abel Tasman sighted Tasmania and New Zealand in 1642, and James Cook's voyages between 1768 and 1779 mapped much of the ocean. Britain founded a penal colony at [Sydney](/history-of/sydney) in 1788, and over the following century settlers spread across Australia, taking land through frontier violence while introduced diseases killed many Aboriginal people. In New Zealand, Māori chiefs and the British Crown signed the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840, but disputes over land led to the New Zealand Wars of the 1840s to 1870s.

In Hawaii, Kamehameha I unified the islands by 1810, creating the [Hawaiian Kingdom](/place/hawaiian-kingdom), which was overthrown in 1893 by American-backed businessmen and annexed by the United States in 1898; it became a state in 1959. By 1900 nearly every Pacific island had been claimed by a colonial power, chiefly Britain, France, Germany or the United States, and after the First World War Japan, Australia and New Zealand took over Germany's possessions. The six Australian colonies federated as the Commonwealth of [Australia](/country/australia) in 1901, and New Zealand became a dominion in 1907. The Pacific was a major battleground of the Second World War between 1941 and 1945, and later a site of British, American and French nuclear testing.

Independence came from 1962, when Samoa became the first Pacific island nation to achieve it, followed by Nauru, Fiji, Papua New Guinea and others in the 1960s to 1980s. In Australia, a referendum in 1967 allowed Aboriginal people to be counted in the national census, and the High Court's Mabo decision of 1992 recognised native title. Today Oceania has fourteen sovereign states, and rising sea levels pose a direct threat to low-lying nations such as Tuvalu and Kiribati.
