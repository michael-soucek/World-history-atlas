The Middle East, stretching from Egypt and Anatolia to Iran and the Arabian Peninsula, saw the world's first farming villages, cities, writing and territorial empires. Its river valleys and trade corridors made it rich and its position between Europe, Africa and Asia made it contested, so that few regions have changed hands as often. Judaism, Christianity and Islam all emerged here, and religion has remained bound up with power ever since.

## The first cities and empires

Wheat and barley were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent by around 9000 BCE. In southern Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and Euphrates, villages grew into the cities of [Sumer](/culture/sumer), where cuneiform writing developed around 3200 BCE. Egypt was unified under a single king at about the same time. In the 2300s BCE Sargon of Akkad joined the Sumerian cities into the first empire to rule many peoples from one centre, and in the eighteenth century BCE Hammurabi of Babylon issued his famous law code. The second millennium brought chariot warfare and great-power diplomacy between Egypt, the Hittites of Anatolia, Mitanni and Babylon, until the Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200 BCE brought down several of these states.

From the ninth century BCE [Assyria](/place/assyria) built an empire of unprecedented size and brutality, ruling from Mesopotamia to Egypt before its capital Nineveh fell in 612 BCE to the Babylonians and Medes. The Neo-Babylonian kingdom of Nebuchadnezzar II, who destroyed Jerusalem's temple in 587 or 586 BCE, was in turn conquered by Cyrus the Great in 539 BCE. His [Achaemenid Empire](/place/achaemenid-empire) ran from the Aegean to the Indus, governed through provinces called satrapies and linked by royal roads. Alexander the Great destroyed it in the 330s BCE, and his Seleucid successors were later displaced by the Parthians in Iran and Mesopotamia and by Rome in the Levant. For seven centuries the frontier between Rome and the Iranian empires, Parthian and then Sasanian from 224 CE, ran through the region.

## The caliphates and their successors

Islam arose in western Arabia in the early seventh century. Within a decade of the Prophet Muhammad's death in 632, Arab armies under the Rashidun caliphs had taken Syria, Iraq and Egypt from the exhausted Byzantine and Sasanian empires, and by 651 the Sasanian state was gone. The Umayyad caliphs ruled from Damascus over a realm stretching from Spain to Central Asia until 750, when the [Abbasid Caliphate](/place/abbasid-caliphate) replaced them and built Baghdad, founded in 762, into one of the largest cities in the world and a centre of science and translation.

Abbasid power fragmented from the ninth century. The Shia Fatimids founded Cairo in 969, the Seljuk Turks took Baghdad in 1055 and much of Anatolia after 1071, and Latin crusaders held Jerusalem from 1099 until Saladin recaptured it in 1187. The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid caliphate there, but were stopped in 1260 at Ain Jalut by the Mamluks, slave soldiers who ruled Egypt and Syria until 1517.

## Ottomans, Safavids and the modern map

From the fourteenth century the [Ottoman Empire](/place/ottoman-empire) grew from a small Anatolian principality into the dominant power of the region, taking Constantinople in 1453 and the Mamluk lands in 1516 and 1517. To the east, the [Safavid dynasty](/place/safavid-dynasty), founded in 1501, made Twelver Shia Islam the faith of Iran, and the long Ottoman and Safavid rivalry fixed a frontier close to the modern border between Turkey and Iraq on one side and Iran on the other.

The Ottoman Empire was defeated in the First World War, and Britain and France divided its Arab provinces into mandates, drawing many of today's borders. The Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923, Saudi Arabia was proclaimed in 1932, and Iraq, Syria, Lebanon and Jordan became independent between the 1930s and 1940s. The State of Israel was declared in 1948, and the Arab-Israeli wars and the Israeli-Palestinian conflict over land, statehood and Jerusalem have continued since. Oil wealth transformed the Gulf states in the twentieth century, while revolution in Iran in 1979, the Iran-Iraq War, the 2003 invasion of Iraq and the uprisings of 2011, followed by civil wars in Syria, Yemen and Libya, have kept the region's political map unusually unsettled.
