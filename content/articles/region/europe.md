Europe is a small, deeply indented peninsula of the Eurasian landmass, and its history has been shaped by that geography: long coastlines, navigable rivers and mountain chains that divided it into many competing units. No single power ever held the whole continent for long. Instead its map has been redrawn again and again by empires, dynasties, religious divisions and, in the last two centuries, nations.

## From the first farmers to Rome

Farming reached south-eastern Europe from Anatolia around 7000 BCE and spread north and west over the following three thousand years. The first palace societies arose in the Aegean: the [Minoan civilisation](/place/minoan-civilization) on Crete from about 2000 BCE, then the Mycenaean Greeks of the mainland, whose world collapsed around 1200 BCE. After a period of decline, Greek city-states revived in the eighth century BCE and planted colonies from the Black Sea to Spain. Athens and its rivals produced the philosophy, drama and political experiments that later Europeans would treat as their classical inheritance, and Alexander the Great carried Greek rule as far as the Indus.

Rome began as a city-state in central Italy and by the second century BCE dominated the Mediterranean, having destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE. Under Augustus, who became the first emperor in 27 BCE, the [Roman Empire](/place/roman-empire) stretched from Britain and the Rhine to Egypt and Syria. Roman roads, law, Latin and, from the fourth century, Christianity left a lasting imprint, but the empire was too large to defend from one centre. It was divided administratively, and in the west Germanic kingdoms replaced imperial rule during the fifth century; the last western emperor was deposed in 476. The eastern half, known to historians as the [Byzantine Empire](/place/byzantine-empire), survived with its capital at Constantinople until 1453.

## Kingdoms, faith and plague

Medieval Europe was built from the successor states of Rome. The Franks under Charlemagne, crowned emperor in Rome in 800, briefly united much of western and central Europe in the [Carolingian Empire](/place/carolingian-empire), which split among his grandsons in 843 and gave rise to the later kingdoms of France and Germany and to the Holy Roman Empire. Scandinavian raiders and traders of the Viking Age reached from Ireland to the Volga between about 800 and 1066, while Slavic, Magyar and Scandinavian rulers adopted Christianity around the turn of the millennium, bringing Poland, Hungary, Bohemia, Kievan Rus' and the Nordic kingdoms into Latin or Orthodox Christendom.

Popes and emperors struggled for authority, crusaders went east from 1096, and Christian kingdoms in Iberia gradually conquered the Muslim south until Granada fell in 1492. Towns, universities and long-distance trade grew rapidly after 1000, until the [Black Death](/event/black-death) of 1347 to 1351 killed perhaps a third or more of the population. In the south-east, the Ottomans crossed into Europe in the 1350s and took Constantinople in 1453, beginning more than four centuries of Ottoman rule in the Balkans.

## Reformation, revolution and the nation-state

The Renaissance, the printing press and the voyages of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries turned Europe outward. Martin Luther's protest of 1517 split western Christianity, and the resulting wars ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which helped establish the idea of sovereign states. Spain, France, Britain, the Dutch Republic, the Habsburg Monarchy, Sweden and later Prussia and Russia competed for power at home and empire overseas.

The [French Revolution](/event/french-revolution) of 1789 and the conquests of Napoleon swept away much of the old order. After 1815 nationalism and industrialisation reshaped the continent: Italy was unified by 1861 and Germany in 1871. The First World War destroyed four empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman) and created a belt of new states from Finland to Yugoslavia. Twenty years later the Second World War, with the Holocaust at its centre, left Europe divided between a Soviet-dominated east and a western bloc tied to the United States.

That division ended with the revolutions of 1989, the reunification of Germany in 1990 and the breakup of the [Soviet Union](/place/soviet-union) in 1991, which added fifteen successor states to the map. Yugoslavia broke apart in a series of wars in the 1990s. Western European cooperation, begun in the 1950s, grew into the European Union, which had 27 members after the United Kingdom left in 2020. In 2022 Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the largest war in Europe since 1945.
