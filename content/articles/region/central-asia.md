Central Asia is a world of steppe grassland, desert and high mountains lying between China, Iran, India and Russia. Two ways of life shaped it: the mounted pastoral nomads of the northern steppes, whose mobility made them formidable warriors, and the settled oasis cities of the south, such as Samarkand, Bukhara and Merv, which grew rich on irrigation and long-distance trade. The interplay between the two, and the region's position on the routes linking east and west, made it the launching ground of empires that reached from Korea to Hungary.

## Nomads and oasis cities

Horses were domesticated on the western steppe, and by about 2000 BCE peoples of the Sintashta culture, east of the Urals, were burying light spoked-wheel chariots with their dead. In the oases of present-day Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and Afghanistan, the Bronze Age Oxus civilisation built fortified towns around 2300 to 1700 BCE. In the first millennium BCE Iranian-speaking nomads, called [Scythians](/place/scythians) by the Greeks and Saka by the Persians, dominated the steppe, while the southern oases of Bactria and Sogdia became provinces of the Achaemenid Empire. Alexander the Great campaigned there between 329 and 327 BCE, and a Greek kingdom survived in Bactria for more than a century after him.

From the late third century BCE the [Xiongnu](/place/xiongnu) built a nomad confederation on the Mongolian plateau that rivalled Han China. Han missions westward in the second century BCE, beginning with the envoy Zhang Qian, opened the network of caravan routes later called the [Silk Road](/culture/silk-road), on which Sogdian merchants became the great middlemen. The Kushans ruled a large empire from Bactria into northern India in the first centuries CE, helping carry Buddhism towards China.

## Turks, Muslims and Mongols

In 552 the Göktürks founded the first empire to carry the name Turk, and Turkic peoples gradually spread across the steppe. Arab armies conquered Transoxiana in the early eighth century, and in 751 an Abbasid force defeated a Tang army near the Talas River, marking the limit of Chinese power in the west. Under the Persian [Samanid Empire](/place/samanid-empire) in the ninth and tenth centuries, Bukhara became a capital of Islamic scholarship and New Persian literature. Turkic dynasties followed, among them the Kara-Khanids and the Seljuks, while the Khazars ruled the steppe between the Caspian and Black seas.

In 1206 [Genghis Khan](/person/genghis-khan) united the Mongol tribes, and in 1219 to 1221 his armies destroyed the Khwarazmian Empire, devastating its cities. His descendants divided the empire into khanates: the Chagatai Khanate in the heart of Central Asia, the Golden Horde on the western steppe and the Ilkhanate in Iran. In the late fourteenth century Timur, a Turkic-Mongol warlord, conquered from Anatolia to Delhi and made Samarkand his capital; under the [Timurid Empire](/place/timurid-empire) his grandson Ulugh Beg built a celebrated observatory there in the 1420s.

## Khanates, empires and new states

After the Timurids, the Uzbek khanates of Bukhara, Khiva and later Kokand held the oases, the Kazakh Khanate emerged on the steppe in the fifteenth century, and the Dzungars built the last great nomad empire until the Qing destroyed it in the 1750s. As sea routes took over long-distance trade, the region lost much of its old commercial importance. Russia advanced south through the nineteenth century, absorbing the Kazakh steppe and taking Tashkent in 1865, while Britain and Russia competed for influence in Afghanistan.

After the Russian Revolution the region was divided in the 1920s and 1930s into Soviet republics, whose borders became international frontiers when the [Soviet Union](/place/soviet-union) dissolved in 1991 and Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan became independent. Soviet-era irrigation for cotton shrank the Aral Sea to a fraction of its former size. Mongolia, a Soviet ally after 1921, held its first multiparty elections in 1990, and Xinjiang and Tibet, to the east, remain part of China.
