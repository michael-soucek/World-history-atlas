Southeast Asia divides into a mainland of long river valleys, the Irrawaddy, Chao Phraya, Mekong and Red River, and a vast archipelago of islands stretching from Sumatra to the Philippines. Lying on the sea route between India and China, and governed by the monsoon winds that made that route work, the region absorbed religions, scripts and ideas of kingship from both, while building states and monuments distinctly its own. Its history moves from river-valley kingdoms and maritime trading powers, through the arrival of Islam and European colonial rule, to the independent nations formed after 1945.

## Early kingdoms and Indian influence

Rice farming spread through the mainland during the third and second millennia BCE, and Austronesian-speaking seafarers, moving south from Taiwan, settled the Philippines and Indonesia from around 2000 BCE. In northern Vietnam the Đông Sơn culture cast elaborate bronze drums in the first millennium BCE. From the early centuries CE, Indian merchants and Brahmin priests brought Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist ideas, which local rulers adopted. Funan, centred on the Mekong delta, was an early trading state, and Champa held the central coast of Vietnam for more than a thousand years. Northern Vietnam, by contrast, was ruled by China for about a thousand years until 938, when the Vietnamese won the battle of Bạch Đằng; independent [Đại Việt](/place/dai-viet) later pushed south at Champa's expense.

## Temple kingdoms and maritime empires

From the seventh century [Srivijaya](/place/srivijaya), based at Palembang in Sumatra, controlled the Strait of Malacca and became a great centre of Buddhist learning. In central Java the Sailendra dynasty built Borobudur around 800. On the mainland, the [Khmer Empire](/place/khmer-empire), founded around 802, ruled much of the Mekong basin from Angkor, where Angkor Wat was built in the early twelfth century. The [Pagan Kingdom](/place/pagan-kingdom) in Burma covered its plain with thousands of Buddhist temples between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, and Theravada Buddhism became the dominant faith of Burma, Siam, Laos and Cambodia.

The Javanese [Majapahit](/place/majapahit) empire, founded in 1293, claimed influence over much of the archipelago in the fourteenth century. The Thai kingdom of Ayutthaya, founded in 1351, became the leading mainland power after Angkor declined. Islam, carried by merchants, spread from northern Sumatra in the late thirteenth century; the sultanate of Malacca, founded around 1400, became the great port of the region and helped make Islam the majority faith in what are now Malaysia, Indonesia and Brunei.

## Colonial rule and independence

The Portuguese captured Malacca in 1511, the Spanish founded Manila in 1571, and the Dutch East India Company made Batavia, modern Jakarta, its headquarters from 1619, gradually turning the [Dutch East Indies](/place/dutch-east-indies) into a territorial empire. In the nineteenth century Britain took Burma, Malaya and Singapore, founded as a British post in 1819, and France built French Indochina from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos. Siam, modernising under its Chakri kings, was the only state in the region to avoid colonisation. The United States took the Philippines from Spain in 1898.

Japan occupied almost the whole region between 1941 and 1945, discrediting the colonial powers. The Philippines became independent in 1946, Burma in 1948 and Indonesia, after declaring independence in 1945 and fighting the Dutch, in 1949. Vietnam's struggle lasted much longer: the French were defeated in 1954, the country was divided, and the [Vietnam War](/event/vietnam-war) ended with reunification under the communist north in 1975. In Cambodia the Khmer Rouge regime that took power in 1975 killed perhaps 1.5 to 2 million people before Vietnamese forces ousted it in 1979. Malaysia formed in 1963 and Singapore separated from it in 1965. The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, founded in 1967, now has eleven members after Timor-Leste joined in 2025.
