Africa is where the human story begins: the oldest known fossils of Homo sapiens, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old, and people everywhere else on earth descend largely from groups who left the continent. Its later history is one of great diversity, from the river kingdoms of the Nile and the trading empires of the Sahel to the Swahili ports of the east coast and the farming states of the forest and savanna, before the shocks of the Atlantic slave trade and European colonisation and the rebuilding of independent nations after 1950.

## Kingdoms of the Nile and the Horn

Egypt was united under a single ruler around 3100 BCE and for nearly three thousand years remained one of the great powers of the ancient world, from the pyramid builders of the Old Kingdom to the empire of the New Kingdom. Upstream, the [Kingdom of Kush](/place/kingdom-of-kush) in Nubia traded and fought with Egypt, ruled it as its 25th Dynasty in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, and later flourished at Meroë, a centre of iron working. On the Mediterranean coast, the Phoenician colony of Carthage dominated the western sea until Rome destroyed it in 146 BCE. In the Ethiopian highlands the [Kingdom of Aksum](/place/kingdom-of-aksum) grew rich on Red Sea trade and minted its own coins, and its king Ezana adopted Christianity in the fourth century CE. Arab armies brought Islam to Egypt in the 640s and across North Africa by the early eighth century, and later Berber dynasties such as the Almoravids and Almohads ruled from Morocco into Spain.

## The Sahel, the forests and the coasts

South of the Sahara, the [Bantu expansion](/event/bantu-expansion) carried farming, Bantu languages and later iron working from the area of present-day Cameroon and Nigeria across much of central, eastern and southern Africa over several thousand years. In West Africa the Nok culture produced terracotta sculpture and smelted iron in the first millennium BCE. Camel caravans across the Sahara linked the Mediterranean to the Sahel, where gold and salt trade built the Ghana Empire, then the [Mali Empire](/place/mali-empire) founded by Sundiata Keita in the thirteenth century, whose ruler Mansa Musa made a famously lavish pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, and then the Songhai Empire, which fell to a Moroccan army in 1591. Cities such as Timbuktu and Djenné became centres of Islamic learning.

On the east coast, Swahili city-states such as Kilwa and Mombasa traded across the Indian Ocean, while inland the stone city of Great Zimbabwe flourished from about the eleventh to the fifteenth century. In the forest zone and savanna, the kingdoms of Benin, Oyo, Kongo and, later, Asante built powerful states.

## Slavery, conquest and independence

Portuguese ships reached the Gulf of Guinea in the fifteenth century, and from the 1500s to the 1860s the [Atlantic slave trade](/event/atlantic-slave-trade) carried about 12.5 million Africans across the ocean, while older trades sent captives across the Sahara and the Indian Ocean. The trade enriched some coastal states and devastated many communities. The nineteenth century brought new African powers, including the Sokoto Caliphate in the north of present-day Nigeria and the [Zulu Kingdom](/place/zulu-kingdom) under Shaka, alongside the abolition of the Atlantic trade.

Then, in the [Scramble for Africa](/event/scramble-for-africa) after the Berlin Conference of 1884 and 1885, European powers divided almost the entire continent. By 1914 only Ethiopia, which defeated an Italian invasion at Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remained independent. Colonial borders, often drawn with little regard for the peoples they divided, became the frontiers of the new states. Egypt gained formal independence in 1922, and after the Second World War the pace quickened: Ghana became independent in 1957, seventeen countries followed in 1960 alone, and the Portuguese colonies won independence in 1974 and 1975 after long wars. Apartheid in South Africa ended with the election of Nelson Mandela in 1994. Africa today has 54 independent countries and a population of about 1.5 billion, the fastest-growing of any continent.
